If you have ever taken your eyes off the road to hunt through a touchscreen menu just to change the fan speed, Polestar's latest concept car is aimed squarely at you. The Formula 2030, a design study from the Swedish electric car brand, puts eight physical controls beneath its central screen and lets the owner decide whether each slot is a rocker switch or a rotary dial. It is not a car you can buy, but it is one of the clearest signs yet that carmakers are rethinking the all-screen cabin. Here is what Polestar showed, how it differs from today's touchscreen-heavy interiors, what has and has not been announced, and why safety testers and rival brands are pushing in the same direction.

How we checked: the details below were confirmed by at least two independent sources, including Polestar's own concept page, Electrek, electrive, InsideEVs, Top Gear, Yanko Design, Autocar, Motor1, Euro NCAP and the European Transport Safety Council. Anything carried by only one outlet is marked "unconfirmed." Production plans and pricing for the Formula 2030 itself have not been announced. Details are current as of 13 November 2026.

What Polestar showed

The Formula 2030 is a low-slung, four-door electric sports car built to preview the design direction of future Polestar models. Polestar has a page for it on its official website, and Electrek, electrive, InsideEVs and Top Gear all covered it under the same name.

The reveal happened in two steps. Top Gear and South Africa's TimesLIVE reported that in early September 2026 Polestar showed the car privately at The Cube, its headquarters building in Gothenburg, to investors, retail partners and a group of VIP customers rather than at a motor show. The full public reveal followed later, with Electrek and electrive publishing detailed coverage on 10 November.

The interior is what caught people's attention. According to Yanko Design and Electrek, a row of eight physical controls sits under the central display, and each position can be specified as either a rocker switch or a rotary dial. Fleet News described it as a customisable physical control panel, and Core77 framed the user-configurable buttons as the headline feature. Evo went a step further and said the controls are modular and can be rearranged; that detail came from one outlet, so the ability to physically move the modules around is unconfirmed. Several outlets, including Electrek and InsideEVs, quoted Polestar interior design head Juan Pablo Bernal describing the idea as a refocus on the driver and a rebalancing between digital and physical controls for a performance brand.

How it differs from today's touchscreen cabins

Polestar's current cars lean hard on Scandinavian minimalism. Most functions live on the central screen, and physical buttons have been cut back. Autocar and Motor1 reported that owners of the current Polestar 3 complained about its steering wheel, which uses unmarked touch-sensitive pads that give no tactile feedback and make drivers look down to check what they are pressing.

The Formula 2030 does not throw out the screen. Multiple outlets, Electrek among them, put the central display at 12.2 inches. The difference is that the screen and the physical controls split the work. Instead of simply adding more fixed buttons, the concept lets the driver pick the type of control for each slot, so frequently used functions can live on something you can find by touch.

The practical argument for physical controls is simple: you can find a knob or switch by feel, and you know it has registered because you felt it move. A touchscreen needs your eyes. Polestar has not published any test data showing how much the Formula 2030 layout reduces distraction, so the safety benefit of this specific design remains a reasonable expectation rather than a measured result.

Production and price

The Formula 2030 is not headed to showrooms as it stands. Polestar has not announced production plans or a price for the concept, and both should be treated as not announced. What Polestar has said is that it previews future cars. Electrek and InsideEVs reported that design elements from the concept will feed into the next-generation Polestar 2, due in 2027, and the Polestar 7 compact SUV, due in 2028.

Whether the configurable eight-slot panel makes it into those cars is still open. Electrek noted that Polestar has not confirmed the physical controls will carry over. The broader direction is clearer. Polestar chief executive Michael Lohscheller told Autocar that customers have been very vocal on the subject and that "we will do buttons." Autocar and Motor1 reported that the 2027 model year Polestar 3 is expected to swap its steering wheel touch pads for physical buttons. No production car with the swappable panel has been announced.

Why buttons are coming back across the industry

Polestar is not alone. Volkswagen said in late 2023 that it would bring physical buttons back after sustained criticism of its touch-sensitive controls. InsideEVs and Electrek reported that VW design chief Andreas Mindt said key functions such as volume, temperature for both sides of the cabin, fan speed and the hazard lights would get physical controls again.

Regulators and safety testers are adding pressure. Euro NCAP, the European new car assessment programme, has said that from its 2026 protocols it will check whether basic functions can be operated with physical controls. Those functions include the turn signals, hazard lights, horn, windscreen wipers and the eCall emergency call. The European Transport Safety Council reported the same change. Cars that bury these functions in touchscreen menus will find it harder to earn the top five-star rating, which gives manufacturers a strong commercial reason to put buttons back.

Taken together, the Formula 2030 reads less like a one-off design flourish and more like a signal of where cabins are heading in the second half of the decade: screens for navigation and media, physical controls for the things you need without looking.

What this means if you are shopping for a car

You cannot buy the Formula 2030, and no current Polestar offers its configurable panel. Polestar 2 and Polestar 4 owners in particular should not expect a retrofit. If physical controls matter to you, there are useful steps you can take with cars already on sale.

  • Test the basics on a drive. Try adjusting temperature, fan speed, volume and wipers while the car is moving, with a passenger timing how long your eyes leave the road. Do this on a quiet road or in a car park first.
  • Check the Euro NCAP rating date. Ratings from 2026 onward reflect the new physical-controls checks. Earlier ratings were awarded under the old protocol, so read the year next to the stars.
  • Look at the steering wheel closely. Unmarked touch pads and real buttons can look similar in photos. Press them in the showroom to find out which you are dealing with.
  • Ask about model-year changes. If a brand has said buttons are coming, as Polestar has for the 2027 Polestar 3, ask the dealer whether waiting for the updated model makes sense for you.
  • Treat concept features as previews. Concept cars change on the way to production. Base a purchase on what is confirmed for the specific model and trim you are considering.

Before your next test drive, write down the five functions you use most while driving and check whether each one has a physical control you can operate by feel. If Polestar confirms which Formula 2030 features reach the 2027 Polestar 2 or the 2028 Polestar 7, use that announcement, not the concept, to decide whether the wait is worth it.
