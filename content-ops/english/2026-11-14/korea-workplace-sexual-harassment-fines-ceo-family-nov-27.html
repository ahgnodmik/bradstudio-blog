If you work for a family-run business in Korea, or for a company whose chief executive behaves as if the rules do not apply to him, a change taking effect on Friday 27 November 2026 is worth knowing about. From that date, administrative fines for workplace sexual harassment no longer stop at the employer. They also reach the representative of a corporation and the owner's or representative's relatives who work at the company. This explainer covers what changes, why the law was written this way, how much the fines are, and what workers and employers can do in the next two weeks.

This is general information, not legal advice. How we checked: the dates and amounts below appear in at least two independent sources, including the Ministry of Employment and Labor's own press release and reports from Women News, Seoul Economic Daily, The Korea Economic Daily, Namdo Ilbo and Wikitree. What changes on 27 November

On Tuesday 3 November, the Cabinet approved an amendment to the Enforcement Decree of the Equal Employment Opportunity and Work-Family Balance Assistance Act, the main Korean law on sex discrimination and harassment at work. The Ministry of Employment and Labor (MOEL) announced the decision the same day. The decree fills in the details of an amended version of the Act that takes effect on 27 November.

Until now, only the employer could be fined directly for committing workplace sexual harassment. From 27 November, three groups are covered:

  • the employer (unchanged);
  • the representative of a corporation, such as the chief executive of a company;
  • relatives of the employer or corporate representative who work at the business as supervisors or employees.

"Relatives" has a defined scope: a spouse, blood relatives within the fourth degree of kinship, and relatives by marriage within the fourth degree. In Korean family terms, that can include children, siblings, nephews and nieces, and first cousins, plus in-laws within the same range. Being related to the boss is not enough on its own. The fine applies only when the relative works at that workplace and commits sexual harassment there.

How much the fines are

SituationFine
Employer or corporate representative commits harassment3 million won
Relative of the employer or representative, working as a supervisor or employee, commits harassment2 million won
Repeated harassment of the same person, or two or more victims (any of the above)5 million won
A repeat offence within three years of a previous harassment fine10 million won

The ceiling is 10 million won. These are administrative fines imposed by the labor authorities, not criminal penalties, and they do not create a criminal record. If the conduct also amounts to a crime under the Criminal Act, such as indecent assault, a police investigation and prosecution can proceed separately. Paying a fine does not close that door.

Why the law needed this fix

Korean harassment law is built around the employer's duties. The employer must run prevention training, investigate complaints, protect the person who reported, and discipline the harasser. When an ordinary employee harasses a colleague, the system expects the company to act.

That design breaks down when the harasser is the person at the top or someone close to them. An owner cannot be expected to discipline himself, which is why employers who commit harassment have long been subject to a direct fine. Two gaps remained. First, when the business is a corporation, the legal "employer" is the company itself, not the individual who runs it, so the chief executive's own conduct sat awkwardly outside the rule. Second, the owner's son working as a team leader, or the chief executive's spouse running the office, is formally just another employee, yet in practice nobody inside the company is in a position to investigate or punish them. Staff in that situation often stay silent. The amendment lets the government fine those people directly instead of relying on a company that has every reason not to act.

A second change on the same day: parental reduced hours

The same amendment touches a separate procedure. Korean law lets parents of young children ask to cut their working hours. Previously, an employer that refused had to explain why in writing, but an employer that agreed had no deadline for saying so. From 27 November, an employer that approves a request must tell the worker within 14 days of receiving it, in writing or electronically. What penalty applies if an employer misses that deadline was not stated in the reports we reviewed, so that point is unconfirmed.

What is still unclear

  • Incidents before 27 November: whether the wider fine rules apply to conduct that happened earlier depends on the decree's transitional provisions. Reports did not settle this, so it is unconfirmed.
  • When the Act itself was promulgated: outlets give different months for the parliamentary amendment, so we have not stated a date.
  • Enforcement in practice: how often MOEL will impose the new fines will only become clear after the rules take effect.

Who should pay attention

The rule matters most in settings such as language academies run by a husband-and-wife team, family restaurants, small clinics, and start-ups where a founder's relatives hold management roles. If your manager is related to the owner or chief executive, the new rule is directly relevant to you. Employers with relatives on the payroll, including foreign-owned small businesses in Korea, are equally affected.

What to do now

If you are a worker, write down any incident on the day it happens: date, time, place, the exact words or actions, and who else was there. Screenshot messages and keep the original chats. Find out who handles harassment complaints at your company, and file in writing so there is a record. If the company does not act, or you face retaliation for reporting, you can contact MOEL's customer counseling line on 1350 for guidance and take the matter to a regional labor office; the Labor Relations Commission also handles requests for corrective orders when an employer fails to take proper action or treats a complainant unfairly. If you are an employer, list every relative who works for you before 27 November, make sure your work rules contain no carve-outs for family members, put someone outside the family in charge of complaints, include yourself and your relatives in this year's prevention training, and set up a simple log so every parental reduced-hours request gets a written answer within 14 days.
