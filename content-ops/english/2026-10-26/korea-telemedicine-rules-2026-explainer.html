If you live in Korea and refill your prescriptions through a telemedicine app, the rules you rely on are about to change on December 24, 2026.

Korea has run telemedicine (bidaemyeon jinryo) as a temporary pilot program for years. An amended Medical Service Act turns it into a permanent, law-based system on December 24. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has now published draft rules that fill in the details. The headline number: prescriptions after a first remote visit are capped at 7 days in principle. Public comment runs until November 9. None of this is final yet.

A legislative notice is the stage where the government asks the public for feedback before finalizing a rule. The drafts amend the Enforcement Rules of the Medical Service Act and related administrative penalty rules. Korean reports date the notice from October 19. English-language outlets dated the announcement anywhere from October 18 to October 23, so check the official notice for the exact window.

Who can use telemedicine under the draft

Returning patients come first

The system centers on returning patients. Under the draft, that means someone who had an in-person visit at the same clinic for the same condition within the past six months. If you already see a doctor in person regularly, your remote follow-ups should be the least affected.

First visits: same province, clinics only

First-time remote visits are allowed, with limits. You can only use a clinic (uiwon), not a hospital, located in the same province or metropolitan city as your registered address. In plain terms, a Seoul resident generally cannot get a first remote visit from a clinic in Busan.

Some patients are exempt from the regional limit. Reports list people with rare diseases, Type 1 diabetes patients, registered persons with disabilities and long-term care recipients. Island and remote-area residents are also mentioned as an exception.

Prescription limits: 7 days, 30 days once

ItemDraft rule
First-visit prescriptionUp to 7 days in principle
First-visit exceptionUp to 30 days, once, if a treatment and prescription history for the same condition is confirmed and the doctor judges it necessary
Returning-patient prescriptionNo separate cap in the draft
Banned by remote prescriptionNarcotics and psychotropic drugs, drugs with misuse risk, obesity treatments (in principle)
Clinic-level capRemote visits limited to 30% of a clinic's total visits per half-year

The obesity drug ban directly targets medicines many people had been getting through apps. The draft keeps a narrow path for some controlled drugs for patients such as those with rare diseases, under set conditions.

Why both sides are unhappy

Platform companies and doctors are pushing back for opposite reasons.

  • Telemedicine platforms say the rules go too far. Their industry council, as reported by Seoul Economic Daily and Segye Ilbo, said the 7-day limit effectively forces in-person visits. It cited its own figure of 33.7 days as the average prescription length per remote visit. A patient with high blood pressure could need several visits to get a month's supply.
  • The Korean Medical Association says a 7-day cap is the minimum needed for patient safety.
  • A national association of private-clinic doctors (Daehan Gaewonui Hyeopuihoe) questioned whether the 30-day exception is needed at all.
  • The Korean Pharmaceutical Association wants the 7-day cap kept and a limit on returning-patient prescriptions too.

The Ministry of Health and Welfare says the goal is to keep in-person care as the default while making remote care safer. It said some standards may be adjusted after the comment period.

What foreigners living in Korea should check

Your registered address

The regional rule for first visits is tied to your registered address. For Korean citizens, that is the resident registration address. The drafts as reported do not spell out how this applies to foreign residents, whose address is registered with immigration. Treat this as not final. If you moved recently, make sure your registered address is up to date.

Your clinic history

Returning-patient status depends on an in-person visit for the same condition at the same clinic within six months. If you manage a chronic condition through an app, one in-person visit at your regular clinic before December 24 may help. Ask your doctor first, because every case is different.

Your medication list

Check whether your regular medicine falls on the banned list. If you have been getting obesity drugs or psychotropic medicines remotely, plan for in-person visits. Do not stop or switch medication on your own. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist.

Still not final

  • Prescription lengths. The 7-day rule and the 30-day exception are draft numbers and may change after November 9.
  • Medicine delivery. Reports say delivery will focus on island and remote-area residents and some vulnerable groups. Who qualifies, and in what areas, is not final.
  • Platform certification. The drafts include a reporting and certification system for telemedicine platforms. The detailed requirements are not final.
  • Exclusions from the 30% cap. Some reports say night and holiday care will not count toward the clinic cap. The exact scope is not final.

Want a say? Before November 9, search for the draft partial amendment to the Enforcement Rules of the Medical Service Act on the government's public legislative comment site (opinion.lawmaking.go.kr) and submit your comment.
