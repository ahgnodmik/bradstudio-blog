On the evening of October 29, 2026 (UTC), attackers drained roughly $6.3 billion in crypto from wallets run by Binance, widely described in coverage of the hack as the world's largest crypto exchange. Binance froze withdrawals within hours. Three days later it began letting customers take money out again, one batch of assets at a time. If you hold anything on the platform, you probably want to know three things: what actually happened, how much of the money is back, and what you should be doing with your own account right now.

This article summarizes company statements and press reports as of November 2, 2026. It is not investment advice and does not recommend buying, selling, or holding any crypto asset.

The short version

  • When: October 29, 2026 (UTC). Blockchain analytics firm TRM Labs said most of the funds left within about 20 minutes.
  • How much: Binance's own estimate is about $6.3 billion. TRM put the outflow at about $6.36 billion. The final figure is not settled.
  • Where from: Binance wallets on ten different blockchains, according to TRM and Euronews.
  • Frozen: About $3 billion of the stolen assets were frozen on-chain with help from partners, including stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle.
  • Who: The FBI has attributed the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor hacking group.
  • Withdrawals: Reopened in phases from Sunday, November 1, starting with bitcoin.
  • Losses: Binance says it will cover user losses from company funds. By November 1 it said about 95% of the affected amount had been replenished.

How the attack happened, as far as we know

Binance has published early findings, and it has been careful to call them preliminary. The company said its initial investigation pointed to a compromised laptop belonging to a DevOps engineer, one of the staff who maintain the systems that keep the exchange running. From that machine, the attacker appears to have reached a service container and pushed through withdrawal transactions that were never properly authorized. BleepingComputer and The Week both reported this account.

Two details matter here. First, Binance said it found no evidence that wallet private keys had leaked. In plain terms, the attacker did not walk off with the master keys; instead they abused an internal pathway that was allowed to send funds. Second, a later review reportedly turned up additional points of breach beyond the laptop. That means the full root cause is still open, and the company has said it will release more technical detail once its investigation is complete.

For ordinary users, the takeaway is uncomfortable but useful. Nothing a customer did caused this. The weak point was inside the company. You cannot fix a vendor's internal security, but you can limit how much damage any single failure does to you, which is what the checklist further down is about.

Who is behind it

Within a day, two blockchain intelligence firms pointed at North Korea. TRM Labs attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, which it described as North Korea's elite hacking unit, and said it made that judgment "with high confidence." Chainalysis said the hack was likely the work of the same group. Those early attributions came from private companies, and the FBI initially said only that it was working with public and private partners to understand the scope of the incident, as Nextgov reported.

The bureau then went further. Reports from the Philippine Daily Inquirer, Yahoo Finance and The Hacker News say the FBI formally named North Korea and TraderTraitor as responsible for the theft and said the attackers were converting stolen assets mainly into ether. The FBI did not publicly name individual suspects.

This is not the first time. In February 2025 the FBI blamed the same North Korean activity, under the same TraderTraitor label, for the theft of about $1.5 billion from the Bybit exchange. NBC News and Security Affairs both covered that attribution at the time.

Is it really the biggest crypto theft ever?

Several outlets say so, and they are not guessing wildly. Al Jazeera described it as appearing to be the biggest crypto heist on record. Infosecurity Magazine called it the largest cryptocurrency heist in history. TRM Labs, in coverage carried by U.S. broadcasters, called it the largest crypto theft on record. The previous benchmark most people cite is the roughly $1.5 billion Bybit hack in 2025, which this incident exceeds several times over.

Keep one caveat in mind. These rankings are based on dollar value at the time of the theft, and crypto prices move constantly. The $6.3 billion number is also an estimate that could be revised as the investigation and on-chain tracing continue.

What is frozen and what has been covered

These are two separate ideas, and headlines often blur them.

Frozen refers to stolen assets that third parties locked before the attackers could move them further. Binance said it worked with blockchain partners to freeze about $3 billion. Al Jazeera reported that more than $2.3 billion of that was frozen by Tether and Circle, the companies that issue the USDT and USDC stablecoins. Freezing does not automatically return money to anyone; it stops the thief from spending it. Whether and how those funds get released back is a slower legal and technical process.

Covered refers to Binance putting its own money into its wallets so customer balances are fully backed again. The company reported this in stages. CNBC said on October 31 that Binance had restored backing for about 90% of the affected assets. By November 1, TechCrunch, CNN and Forbes were reporting about 95%. Reuters described the effort in dollar terms, saying Binance had covered about $6 billion by buying replacement tokens.

The money came from three pots, according to CNN and others: the Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU), Binance's futures insurance fund, and the company's corporate treasury. The remaining slice is made up of less liquid tokens that are harder to buy in size quickly, so Binance has said it will take longer.

Where withdrawals stand

Binance turned withdrawals back on starting Sunday, November 1. CoinDesk, CBS News and TechCrunch all described a phased approach with bitcoin in the first batch. Reports say the first phase covered roughly 15 assets or networks, including major coins such as ether, and that more would follow after testing. The exact list changes as each phase goes live, so the reliable place to check a specific coin and network is Binance's own announcements page or the withdrawal screen in the app.

Deposits were less affected. Binance said they were briefly paused and then running normally as of an update early on October 30.

Binance also promised a fuller proof-of-reserves report, the cryptographic check that lets customers confirm their balances are included in the company's holdings. Co-CEO Richard Teng said it would come by Monday, November 2, according to Crypto Briefing and other outlets. At the time of writing, publication of the full report could not be confirmed, and at least one report says the timeline slipped to November 3. Treat any reserves figures you see quoted before that document appears as unverified.

What South Korean exchanges did

The incident rippled into Korea quickly. Upbit posted a notice around 7:46 a.m. on October 30 warning users about sending digital assets to Binance and saying it could restrict such withdrawals if needed. Bithumb, Coinone and DigitalX followed with similar warnings, according to SBS and Korean business press. These were cautions, not a blanket ban. If you move coins between a Korean exchange and Binance, check both sides before you send anything.

What to check in your own account today

None of these steps would have stopped an attack inside Binance. They do reduce your exposure to the scams and account takeovers that tend to follow a big breach, when phishing messages pretending to be "refund" or "verification" notices spike.

  • Turn on the withdrawal address allowlist. Most big exchanges let you restrict withdrawals to addresses you have saved in advance. Review the list and delete anything you do not recognize.
  • Review two-factor authentication. An authenticator app or a hardware security key is safer than SMS codes. Make sure the method on file is one you still control.
  • Check active sessions and devices. Log out of browsers and phones you no longer use. Change your password if you reused it anywhere else.
  • Ignore unsolicited "compensation" links. Binance does not need you to click an email link or connect a wallet to restore your balance. Go to the app or type the address yourself.
  • Confirm the network before every withdrawal. Because withdrawals are reopening asset by asset, one coin can be live on one network and paused on another. Sending on a paused or wrong network can delay funds or lose them.
  • Download your records. Export a statement of your balances and recent transactions now. If anything looks wrong later, you will have a dated record to show support.
  • Think about concentration. This is not a recommendation, just a common risk principle: keeping everything on one platform means one outage affects all of it.

What is still unconfirmed

  • The final size of the theft. $6.3 billion is an estimate, and analysts' totals vary.
  • The complete root cause. The laptop explanation is preliminary, and additional breach points were reported.
  • When the last roughly 5% of affected assets will be replenished.
  • When every asset and network will be back to normal withdrawals.
  • The publication date and contents of the full proof-of-reserves report.
  • How much of the roughly $3 billion that was frozen can be returned, and how long that takes.
  • Whether Korean users suffered any direct losses. No figures have been published.

If you hold assets on Binance, do three things today. Turn on the withdrawal address allowlist and remove any address you do not recognize. Before moving any coin, open Binance's official announcements and confirm that the specific asset and network you plan to use are listed as open. Then save a copy of your current balance statement so you have a dated record while the recovery plays out.
