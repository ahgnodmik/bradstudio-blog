Anyone who chops a bunch of parsley by hand knows the routine. A few strokes of the knife, then a pause to scrape the scattered leaves back into a pile, then a few more strokes. The herbs drift toward the edge of the board, and some end up on the counter. NUO is a student design project that treats this as a problem of shape rather than technique: if the board itself keeps the pile together, you can just keep cutting.

NUO pairs a rocking knife with a wooden board that has a shallow hollow in the middle. It was featured by Yanko Design on October 31, 2026, and by the design blog FreeYork. Both describe it as the work of Münire Çalışır, a student at Burg Giebichenstein University of Art and Design Halle in Germany. It is a design concept, not a product you can buy.

What NUO is

The set has two parts that were designed together from the start.

  • The board is made of ash and has a shallow, recessed center. According to both write-ups, the hollow serves two purposes: it holds chopped ingredients in one place, and it guides the rocking motion of the blade.
  • The knife is stainless steel with a curved edge, meant to be rocked back and forth rather than lifted and dropped. Yanko Design describes it as starting life as a water-jet-cut sheet of stainless steel that is then pressed in the middle to give it a three-dimensional form, so the handle and blade come out as a single piece. FreeYork's headline calls it a pressed steel knife, which fits that description.

The idea is simple. On a flat board, every rocking stroke pushes food outward. On a curved board, gravity and the shape of the hollow push it back toward the middle. The curve of the blade and the curve of the board are matched, so the edge stays in contact with the wood through the whole stroke instead of riding up at the ends.

What the designer claims

The project materials, as quoted by both outlets, say the curvature of the cutting edge supports a natural rocking movement and that the weight of the knife reduces the effort needed to cut. Those are design intentions. Nobody outside the project has published hands-on testing, and Yanko Design was clear that its piece described the concept rather than a tested product. Until someone actually uses one for a few weeks, claims about comfort, speed or safety are unverified.

This is not a new idea, and that is fine

Rocking knives are old. The mezzaluna, Italian for "half moon," is a curved blade with a handle at each end, or sometimes a single handle on top. It has been used for chopping herbs and vegetables for a long time, and Wikipedia's entry notes that mezzalunas are sometimes sold with a shallow, dished board made for exactly this job. In French kitchens the same tool is called a hachoir.

You can already buy sets that combine a curved knife with a concave board. One widely sold example is the Cole & Mason Ashden set, which pairs a stainless steel mezzaluna with an acacia board that has a concave surface. Retailer listings say the curve keeps herbs in place and lets the mezzaluna nest into the board for storage. John Boos, the American butcher block maker, has also sold a gift set that combines a board with a dished area and a rocker knife.

So what does NUO add? Based on the two published descriptions, the difference is in how tightly the parts are matched. Most existing sets put a standard mezzaluna together with a board that suits it. NUO's knife profile and the board's hollow were developed as one form, and the single-piece pressed knife is part of that approach. Whether that produces a noticeably better result in daily use is exactly the thing nobody has tested yet.

The obvious limitation

FreeYork made a fair point: because the concave center is the whole idea, NUO is not a replacement for a large flat cutting board. You cannot slice a loaf of bread, break down a chicken or dice an onion efficiently in a shallow bowl. Think of it as a specialist tool for focused jobs where keeping a small pile together is the point, such as fresh herbs, garlic, chilies or a handful of nuts.

That is true of every dished herb board on the market, too. If you cook a lot of pesto, salsa verde or chimichurri, the specialist tool may earn its drawer space. If you chop herbs twice a month, a sharp chef's knife and a bench scraper do the job.

Price and availability

There is none to report. NUO is a student project, and neither Yanko Design nor FreeYork mentioned a price, a manufacturer, a production plan or a release date. No official product page or shop listing turned up in searches for this article. Student projects sometimes get picked up by manufacturers, but there is no public sign of that here. Dimensions and weight have not been published either.

If you want something like it now

The closest thing you can actually buy is a mezzaluna sold with a dished wooden board. A few practical things to check before you buy one:

  • Is the board really concave? Some mezzalunas are sold with a flat board, which loses the main benefit. Look for words like "concave," "dished" or "bowl" in the description, and look at side-on photos.
  • One handle or two? Two-handled mezzalunas keep both hands above the blade, which many cooks find reassuring. Single-handle designs can be more compact and easier to store.
  • Blade cover or storage. A curved blade does not sit well loose in a drawer. A sheath, or a board the knife nests into, keeps the edge protected and fingers safe.
  • Board care. Wooden boards generally should be hand washed, dried promptly and oiled now and then with food-safe mineral oil. Check the maker's instructions; dishwashers tend to warp and crack wood.
  • Sharpening. A curved blade is harder to sharpen on a standard whetstone or pull-through sharpener. Find out whether the maker offers advice or a sharpening service.
  • Size. Measure your drawer and your counter space. A dished board needs to be thick enough to hold the curve, so it can be bulkier than you expect.

Why a student project is worth a look

Design schools are where a lot of kitchen ideas get tested before industry adopts or ignores them. Burg Giebichenstein, based in Halle (Saale), is a German university of art and design with a long history in applied arts and industrial design. Projects like NUO are useful even when they never reach a shop, because they show which everyday frustrations designers think are worth solving. In this case the frustration is small and familiar, and the proposed answer leans on shape and material rather than gadgets or electronics.
