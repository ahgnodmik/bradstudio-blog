Titaner, the brand best known for machined titanium pens and pocket tools, has put a jet-flame lighter on Kickstarter, and the top version is carved from a real iron meteorite. It is called the Monolith. The headline number is eye-catching: roughly 30 meteorite pieces at $2,879 each. Most buyers, though, will end up with a titanium lighter, and anyone planning to carry one on a trip runs into a second problem: torch lighters are among the items airlines and aviation regulators refuse to carry at all. This piece covers what the Monolith actually is, what is still unconfirmed, and what the flying rules mean for owners.

How we checked: each price, date and specification below appears in at least two independent sources, including Yanko Design, Gear Patrol, HiConsumption, the Kickstarter tracker Kicktraq and Titaner's own product page. Airline rules come from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the published guidance of Korean Air and Asiana Airlines. Anything that appeared in only one place is labelled "unconfirmed."

What the Titaner Monolith is

The Monolith is a refillable butane lighter with a wind-resistant jet (sometimes called turbo) flame. The campaign went live on Kickstarter on 17 November 2026, according to Kicktraq and Yanko Design. Gear Patrol reports that the flame unit inside is an insert from the lighter maker Cricket, which means the gas chamber and flint can be swapped rather than the whole lighter being thrown away when something wears out.

Three design choices stand out in the coverage:

  • A removable copper heat sink. Titanium is a poor conductor of heat, so the area around the flame can stay hot. Copper spreads heat quickly. Titaner's product page and Gear Patrol both describe the copper part as a way to pull heat away from the burner.
  • A magnetic side slider. Sliding it exposes the refill valve so you can top up butane without removing the insert. Gear Patrol and HiConsumption both note that the slider doubles as a flathead driver for adjusting gas flow and flame height.
  • A choice of shell. The same internals sit inside either a titanium or a meteorite body, depending on the edition.

Dimensions and weight did not appear in any of the reports we found, so treat those as unconfirmed until the campaign page is updated.

Where the meteorite comes from

Muonionalusta is an iron-nickel meteorite found scattered across the Norrbotten region of northern Sweden, near the Finnish border. The first fragment turned up in 1906 near the village of Kitkiöjärvi, according to Wikipedia and the regional heritage site Heart of Lapland. More pieces have been recovered since.

The "4.5 billion years old" line in the marketing refers to when the metal formed, not when it landed. Both Wikipedia and Heart of Lapland put its formation at around 4.56 billion years ago, close to the birth of the solar system. Its arrival on Earth was far more recent; one auction listing puts the fall at around a million years ago, but we found that in only one source, so it is unconfirmed.

Why every piece looks different

Cut an iron meteorite, polish the face and etch it with a mild acid, and a crosshatch of long, thin bands appears. This is the Widmanstätten pattern. It forms when two iron-nickel minerals, kamacite and taenite, grow into each other as the metal cools extremely slowly inside a parent asteroid, over spans that factory metalworking cannot reproduce. Muonionalusta is classified as a fine octahedrite, a type known for tight, crisp banding. Because the bands change with the angle and position of each cut, no two Meteorite Edition shells will look the same, which is the main selling point Titaner leans on.

One practical note for collectors: iron meteorites can rust if left in damp conditions. The reports we read did not say whether Titaner applies a protective coating, so that is worth asking before pledging.

Shipping is the biggest gap. None of the coverage says which countries the campaign ships to, or whether the lighter leaves the factory with gas in it. That matters, because lighters and butane are regulated as dangerous goods in air freight, and couriers handle them differently from ordinary parcels. If you live outside the United States, check the shipping list on the reward tier before you commit, and ask the creator directly if your country is missing. Crowdfunding delivery dates also slip often, so treat March 2027 as a target rather than a promise.

Can you fly with a torch lighter?

In most cases, no, and that includes the Monolith because it uses a jet-flame insert. The rules below come from official guidance, but they vary by airline, route and country, so always confirm with your own carrier before you pack.

United States

The FAA's PackSafe guidance says torch lighters, which it also calls butane, blue-flame or jet-flame lighters, are not allowed in the cabin or in checked baggage. The agency's passenger FAQ adds that containers of lighter fluid or butane refill gas are forbidden in both carry-on and checked bags. An ordinary lighter is treated differently: the FAA allows one per passenger, carried in the cabin or on your person.

Korean airlines

Korean Air's restricted-items page says each passenger may carry one lighter or one book of safety matches on their person, that lighters cannot go in checked bags, and that torch and plasma lighters cannot be taken on board at all. It also says lighters found in checked luggage during X-ray screening are discarded. Asiana Airlines' guidance (in Korean) likewise limits passengers to one lighter in the cabin and bars lighters from checked bags. Both carriers say lighters are not allowed in either cabin or hold on flights departing from China, and Asiana lists flammable gas canisters such as butane among items banned from air transport. The Korean newspaper Segye Ilbo reported the same torch-lighter ban for both cabin and hold.

A simple packing routine

  • Check whether your lighter is a torch type. A straight blue flame or a hissing jet usually means it is.
  • Leave torch lighters and butane refills at home.
  • If you need a flame on the trip, bring one ordinary lighter in your pocket, not in your suitcase.
  • For connecting flights, check the rules at the transfer airport too; a second security screening may apply a stricter standard.
  • Buy refill gas at your destination rather than trying to fly with it.

If you want to bring an expensive lighter abroad, shipping it separately is not a guaranteed workaround. Many couriers refuse lighters or require special handling, so ask before you send anything.

Who the Monolith makes sense for

For most people this is a premium titanium jet lighter in the $149 to $159 range, with thoughtful touches like the copper heat sink and the tool-free refill slider. The Meteorite Edition is a different purchase: a collectible with a one-of-a-kind etched surface and a very small run. Either way, it is a lighter that stays at home when you travel by air.

If you are tempted, open the Kickstarter page and check three things before pledging: the current price of your tier, whether your country is on the shipping list, and how many Meteorite Edition slots remain. Then check your airline's restricted-items page before your next trip so the lighter does not end up in an airport bin.
