If you manage people at a workplace in Korea, whether a factory floor, a warehouse, a construction site or a foreign company's local office, a rule change from this month will land on your desk soon. On 4 November 2026 the Ministry of Employment and Labor (MOEL) issued a fully rewritten version of its guideline on workplace risk assessment. For the first time, skipping the assessment, or doing it without workers, comes with a fixed fine. This explainer covers what risk assessment is, what changed, how much the fines are, who is covered from when, and what to do before the first fines can apply on 1 January 2027.

What risk assessment means in Korea

Risk assessment is the process of finding hazards in a workplace, deciding how serious each one is, and putting measures in place to reduce them. It is meant to happen before people get hurt, not after. Under the Occupational Safety and Health Act, Korean employers already have a duty to carry it out. The MOEL guideline is the document that spells out how: when to do it, who must take part, and what records to keep.

The problem, according to the coverage of the reform, was that the duty existed on paper but had little direct penalty attached. Critics also pointed to "paper assessments": forms filled in at a desk while conditions on the floor stayed the same. Asia Economy described the new guideline as a follow-up to amendments to the Occupational Safety and Health Act and its enforcement decree and rules earlier this year. In plain terms, the law created the basis for fines, and the guideline now turns it into concrete, checkable steps.

What changed in how the assessment is done

Before work starts, not a month later

Under the old guideline, a new business had one month after it was established to begin its first assessment. That left a window in which work could run with no assessment at all. The revised guideline says the initial assessment must be finished before the first work begins. The same principle applies to ad hoc assessments, the ones triggered by events such as new equipment or a change in process: they should be done before that work starts. New sites and new machines are where unfamiliar hazards tend to show up, so the order now matches the risk.

"Carry out" every year, not just "review"

The regular assessment used to be described as a review every year. The new wording requires the assessment to be carried out at least once a year. The difference matters. A review can be read as skimming last year's paperwork. An assessment means looking at the workplace again.

Workers must take part

Workers who do a job every day usually know its hazards best. The revised guideline ties worker participation to fines. If an employer leaves workers out, or refuses a worker representative's request to take part, the fine runs from 1.5 million won for a first violation to 5 million won for a third. When the representative asks to join, the employer must agree on the schedule, method and scope of that participation.

Results must be shared and kept

An assessment that nobody on the floor hears about changes little. Employers now have to tell workers the schedule and results, and keep a written record for three years. Missing either step carries its own fine.

Contractors can assess together

Where a principal contractor and subcontractors share a site, the old rule had each side assess separately. The new guideline lets them do it separately or jointly. If they choose a joint assessment, the principal contractor, the subcontractor and the workers of both must all take part. On shared sites this can catch hazards that sit between two companies' areas of responsibility.

Who is covered, and from when

The phase-in is by size. Workplaces with 50 or more regular employees, and construction projects worth 5 billion won or more, face fines from 1 January 2027. Smaller workplaces and smaller construction projects follow on 1 January 2028.

The staggered dates apply to the fines only. The duty to assess applies to employers of every size now. If you run a 20-person office, you have an extra year before a fine can be issued, not an extra year before you need to start.

If your headcount moves around the 50 mark, plan as if you are over it. If you run several branches, check how headcount is counted for your situation; we could not confirm in two sources how branches are treated, so ask your regional labor office.

What the government says it will provide

MOEL said it will focus first on informing workplaces with 50 or more employees, since they are first in line for fines. Reports say the ministry plans to publish a workplace guidebook and a frequently asked questions explainer before the end of the year. Support for small workplaces, such as consulting, was also mentioned in coverage, but the details and dates are unconfirmed.

A practical plan for the next six weeks

None of this requires expensive software. It does require someone to own it. A simple plan:

  • This week: count your regular employees and confirm whether your fine date is 1 January 2027 or 1 January 2028. For construction, check the contract value.
  • Name an owner: decide who plans the assessment, who walks the site, and who signs off. Write it down so it survives staff changes.
  • Set up worker input: pick a method that fits your workplace, such as a short meeting, a survey or a joint walk-through. Keep sign-in sheets, photos or minutes as proof.
  • Find and rank hazards: break each job into steps and list what could go wrong, such as caught-in machinery, falls, slips, chemicals or noise. Look at past accidents and near misses.
  • Fix the biggest risks first: remove the hazard if you can, guard it with equipment if you cannot, and use work procedures and protective gear after that. Give each fix an owner and a deadline.
  • Share the results: post them where workers actually look, such as the notice board, the team chat or the pre-shift safety talk, and note when and how you shared them.
  • Store the records: keep them in one shared folder by year so they can be found for three years, even if the person who wrote them leaves.
  • Book next year: put the 2027 regular assessment on the calendar now, and add an assessment step before any new equipment or new site starts work.

If you are an employee

You have a stake in this too. Ask whether your workplace has shared its latest assessment schedule and results. If a task feels unsafe, raise it during the assessment; that is what the participation rule is for. A worker representative can formally ask the employer to be included.

What is still open

  • The exact release date of the ministry's guidebook and FAQ explainer: not yet announced.
  • How inspections and fines will be run in practice from 2027: not yet announced.
  • The detailed fine bands for failing to share schedules and results: unconfirmed beyond the reported range.

What to do now

Count your headcount today and write your fine date at the top of a one-page plan. Check whether you have an assessment from the past 12 months, whether workers took part, and whether the record is somewhere you can find it. If any of those is missing, schedule a full assessment with worker input in December, and book your first 2027 regular assessment for the first weeks of January.
