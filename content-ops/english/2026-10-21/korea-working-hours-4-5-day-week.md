# Korea's Long Hours and the 4.5-Day Week: What Foreign Workers Should Know

If you work in South Korea, you probably work more hours than most of your friends back home, and the rules are changing fast.

This guide sets out the numbers, the laws, and the reforms now under way. It is written for readers abroad and for foreign employees already in Korea. Each point ends with what it means for your own job.

## Korea still works about 100 hours more than the OECD average

OECD data reported in July 2026 put Korea's average annual working hours at 1,833 in 2025. The OECD average was 1,736 hours. That is a gap of 97 hours a year.

The trend is downward. Korean workers logged 1,865 hours in 2024, so 2025 brought a drop of 32 hours. Korea still ranked sixth-longest among the 36 OECD countries with data. Mexico was highest at 2,205 hours. Germany was lowest at 1,332 hours.

The gap with Germany is 501 hours. Divide that by an 8-hour day and you get roughly 63 working days a year.

Read these figures with care. Annual hours are an average across all employed people. They include part-timers and the self-employed. A country with many part-time jobs will show a lower average even if full-time staff work long days. Treat the number as a trend line, not a verdict on any one office.

## The 52-hour rule is a ceiling, not a target

Korea's Labor Standards Act sets a standard week of 40 hours, with 8 hours a day. Employer and employee can agree to up to 12 hours of overtime per week. Add the two and you get the famous 52-hour cap.

The cap arrived in stages. Firms with 300 or more employees came under it in July 2018. Firms with 5 to 49 employees followed in July 2021. Holiday work now counts inside the 12-hour overtime limit.

Overtime is not free. The law requires an extra 50% of ordinary wages for overtime, so you earn at least 1.5 times your normal hourly rate. Night work between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. carries its own 50% premium. The two can stack. One overtime hour worked at 11 p.m. can earn about 2 times the ordinary rate. Holiday work earns a 50% premium for the first 8 hours and 100% beyond that.

One exception matters a lot. Workplaces with 4 or fewer regular employees are exempt from the overtime premium and statutory annual leave rules. Minimum wage still applies to them. If you work at a very small firm, your contract is your main protection.

## The government wants the 1,700s by 2030

In December 2025, labor, business, and government signed a joint declaration on cutting actual working hours. The headline goal is to bring Korea's annual hours down to the 1,700s by 2030. That would put Korea roughly at the OECD average.

The declaration is a policy target, not a legal requirement. It set the direction for three reforms covered below: a 4.5-day week subsidy, a crackdown on all-inclusive wage contracts, and more flexible annual leave.

## The 4.5-day week is a subsidy, not a law

Headlines about a "4.5-day week" can mislead. No law forces Korean employers to adopt it.

The Ministry of Employment and Labor runs a pilot called the Work-Life Balance +4.5 Project. It launched in 2026. It pays small and mid-sized firms that cut working hours without cutting pay. Participation is voluntary. Management and workers must agree on the new schedule.

The key numbers:

- Subsidy: up to KRW 600,000 per worker per month whose hours are reduced
- Extra support: up to KRW 800,000 per month for each new hire during the transition
- Participation: 224 companies by the end of June 2026
- Target: 220 companies for the year, so the pilot reached 101.8% of its goal
- Models: firms can choose a 4.5-day week, a 38-hour week, or a 35-hour week

Most participants are small. The ministry reported that about two-thirds of them had fewer than 50 employees.

For a foreign employee, the takeaway is simple. Your employer may offer a shorter week, but it does not have to. Ask HR whether the company has joined the program. If it has, your new schedule and pay terms should be in writing.

## All-inclusive wage contracts face new limits

Many Korean employment contracts use an all-inclusive wage system (pogwal imgeum). Under this model, overtime, night, and holiday pay are bundled into a fixed monthly salary. The employee gets the same pay no matter how many extra hours they work. Critics call it a route to unpaid overtime.

On April 9, 2026, the Ministry of Employment and Labor began enforcing a guideline to prevent abuse of these contracts. Its main points:

- Contracts that lump base pay and overtime allowances together without separating them are prohibited in principle.
- Employers should itemize base pay and each allowance on wage ledgers and pay statements.
- Fixed overtime allowances are still allowed. If your actual overtime exceeds the hours the fixed allowance covers, the employer must pay the difference.

The ministry followed up with workplace inspections based on tips from workers.

Keep one limit in mind. The guideline is administrative guidance, not a statute. Several bills to restrict or ban all-inclusive wage agreements are still pending in the National Assembly. Until one passes, courts and inspectors rely on existing case law and this guideline.

## Annual leave in hourly units is coming

In May 2026, the National Assembly passed an amendment to the Labor Standards Act. It lets workers split their paid annual leave into hourly units. Employers must comply when a worker asks.

The change does not take effect immediately. It applies one year after promulgation, which means 2027. A presidential decree will set the exact units and procedures.

The amendment also bans dismissal or other unfair treatment of workers who request or take annual leave.

For you, this means a dentist visit or a school meeting will no longer cost a full or half day of leave. Watch for your company to update its rules of employment before the new provision kicks in.

## How Korea compares with other countries

Comparing working-hour laws is tricky. Some countries set a baseline, some set a ceiling, and some do both.

- France: The legal week is 35 hours. It is the point where overtime pay starts, not a hard limit. The ceilings are 48 hours in any single week and an average of 44 hours over 12 weeks.
- Japan: The standard week is 40 hours. Overtime is capped at 45 hours a month and 360 hours a year in principle. With a special agreement, the annual cap rises to 720 hours. Monthly overtime plus holiday work must stay under 100 hours. The average over 2 to 6 months must stay within 80 hours a month.
- Belgium: Since November 2022, full-time employees can request a four-day week. Total hours stay the same. Workers fit them into four longer days. Employers can refuse, but they must give reasons in writing.
- Brazil: On October 14, 2026, the Senate approved a constitutional amendment by 75 to 1. It cuts the maximum week from 44 to 40 hours with no pay cut and guarantees two paid rest days a week. Congress promulgated it on October 16, 2026. The cut is phased: 42 hours first, then 40 hours later.

The lesson for comparisons: Korea's 52 hours is a maximum with overtime included. France's 35 hours is a baseline before overtime. Comparing them directly gives the wrong answer. Belgium's four-day week is compressed, not shorter. Korea's 4.5-day pilot is different again, because it cuts total hours while protecting pay.

## What to check if you work in Korea

Use this list before your next pay day.

- Count your employer's headcount. If the workplace has 5 or more regular employees, overtime premiums and annual leave rules apply to you.
- Read your employment contract. Look for the words "all-inclusive wage" or "fixed overtime allowance." If your contract has either, track your real hours.
- Compare your hours with your pay. Overtime should pay at least 1.5 times your ordinary hourly wage. Night overtime should pay more.
- Check your pay statement. Employers must issue one, and it should list overtime, night, and holiday hours.
- Keep your own records. Save badge logs, emails, and messages that show when you started and finished work.
- Ask HR whether the company has joined the Work-Life Balance +4.5 Project, and ask how it will handle hourly annual leave from 2027.
- Call for help when something does not add up. The Ministry of Employment and Labor's customer center is 1350 from any phone in Korea. The Irish Embassy in Seoul and the Bucheon city foreign-resident guide both list English service on this line. Confirm the language options when you call. The Immigration Contact Center at 1345 offers multilingual help on visa and residence questions.

Open your contract today, search it for "overtime" and "inclusive," and write down your actual hours for the next two weeks. If the numbers do not match your pay, call 1350.
