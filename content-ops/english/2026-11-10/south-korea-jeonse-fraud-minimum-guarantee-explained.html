If you rent a home in South Korea, or are thinking about it, you will hear the word jeonse sooner or later, often alongside the word fraud. Thousands of tenants have lost large deposits in recent years when landlords could not or would not pay them back. On 13 November 2026 a new safety net takes effect: a "minimum guarantee" meant to ensure that recognised victims get back at least one-third of their deposit. Online pre-applications open on 10 November. This explainer covers how jeonse works, what went wrong, what the new program does, and what is still unclear, written for readers who did not grow up with the system.

Whether you qualify and how much you might receive depends on your own case, and only the official notice from the Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH) can settle that. Details are current as of 10 November 2026.

What jeonse is

In most countries, renting means paying every month. Jeonse works differently. The tenant hands the landlord a single large lump-sum deposit at the start of the lease, often a substantial share of the home's market value, and then pays little or no monthly rent. When the lease ends, typically after two years, the landlord returns the full deposit. The landlord's return comes from what they do with the money in the meantime: investing it, paying down a loan or putting it toward another property.

For tenants, jeonse can be cheaper than monthly rent over time, especially when the deposit is financed with a lower-interest loan. For landlords, it has served as a form of interest-free financing. The whole arrangement depends on one assumption: that the landlord will have the money to give back at the end.

Where it goes wrong

Problems arise when the deposit plus any mortgage on the property adds up to more than the home is worth, or when a landlord owns many units with borrowed money and cannot repay several tenants at once. In the worst cases, landlords deliberately structured deals so tenants could never be repaid. Some schemes involved people who had no legal right to lease the home at all, such as cases where the property was held by a trust company. Tenants in these situations often end up waiting for a court auction or public sale of the property, and if banks or other creditors rank ahead of them, the tenant may recover only a small part of the deposit, or nothing.

The new minimum guarantee, in plain terms

The program sets a floor. If a recognised victim's total recovery, such as money received from an auction or public sale, comes to less than one-third of the original deposit, the state covers the difference in cash. It does not add one-third on top of what someone already recovered. It only fills the gap up to that one-third line.

The legal basis is an amendment, passed earlier in 2026, to Korea's special act on support for jeonse fraud victims, with the enforcement decree revised in October according to Korean reports. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport oversees the policy, and LH handles applications, payments and settlement.

A worked example

The example used across Korean reports is a deposit of 100 million won with 10 million won already recovered. One-third of 100 million is about 33.33 million won. Subtracting the 10 million already recovered leaves about 23.33 million won as the potential top-up. The table below applies the same simple arithmetic to other cases. Who can apply, and who is excluded

The starting point is official recognition. Applicants must already have been designated as jeonse fraud victims under the special act, which involves criteria such as a deposit of 500 million won or less and having secured legal standing as a tenant through move-in registration and a fixed-date stamp on the lease. Simply not having your deposit returned is not enough. If you have not been designated yet, that application comes first.

Several reports list the same exclusions: people without South Korean nationality, some tenants with deposits above 500 million won, and people who bought the damaged home themselves. One report adds tenants who did not file a claim for distribution in the auction, but that point is unconfirmed.

A note for foreign residents: because reports list lack of Korean nationality as an exclusion, foreign tenants should not assume they qualify. If you are a foreign national affected by jeonse fraud, contact LH directly to ask how the rules apply to you, and consider getting advice from a legal aid service or a lawyer familiar with Korean tenancy law.

Victims whose auctions finished before the program started are reported to be eligible as well. If your case closed some time ago and you recovered less than one-third, it may be worth checking.

Advance payment for trust-fraud victims

Tenants who signed leases with someone who had no authority to lease the property, such as in trust-related scams, often face especially long legal processes. For them, the program offers advance payment and later settlement: they can receive the guaranteed amount before the property disposal is finished, with adjustments made later if money is recovered. The Herald Business reported that recipients must report and repay funds if grounds arise, such as later getting the deposit back; the details of that obligation should be confirmed in LH's notice. Other victims generally apply after the auction or public sale has concluded.

Documents and deadlines

Korean reports list the following documents. The list appeared in only some reports, so the official LH notice is the final word.

  • The decision designating you as a jeonse fraud victim
  • A copy of your lease contract
  • An abstract of your resident registration
  • The property's certified registry record
  • The auction distribution statement, if the auction has finished
  • Identification

Applicants who cannot apply in person because of illness or time abroad can reportedly appoint a representative; the required authorisation documents are unconfirmed.

The general deadline is three years from the date you were designated a victim, or one year from the end of the auction or public sale. People designated before the program began can apply until 13 November 2029, according to Korea.kr and news reports. Because the one-year auction rule might end sooner for some people, check both dates for your own case.

Practical tips if you rent in Korea

  • Before signing any jeonse lease, read the property's registry record yourself and compare existing mortgages with the deposit you are being asked for.
  • Complete move-in registration and obtain the fixed-date stamp on your lease promptly; these steps underpin your legal position as a tenant.
  • Ask whether deposit return guarantee insurance is available for the property, and read the conditions carefully before relying on it.
  • Keep copies of every contract, receipt and message with the landlord or agent in one place.

If you are a designated victim, open LH's Cheongyak Plus site today and read the minimum support notice before anything else. Pull out your victim decision and any distribution statement, compare what you have recovered with one-third of your deposit, and if there is a gap, gather the documents and apply this week or visit an LH regional headquarters from 13 November. If anything about your status is unclear, call the LH call centre on 1600-1004 or speak to a jeonse damage support centre or lawyer before you file.
