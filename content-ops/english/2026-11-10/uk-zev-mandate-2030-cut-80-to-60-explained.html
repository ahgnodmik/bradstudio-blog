If you are planning to buy a new car in Britain in the next few years, or you work for a company that sells cars there, one number matters more than most: the share of new cars each maker must sell as zero-emission. For 2030 that number is currently 80%. Reports published on 8 and 9 November 2026 say the government is preparing to cut it to around 60%. The government says no decision has been made. Here is what is known, what is not, and what it could mean for buyers and carmakers in the UK and across Europe.

How we checked: the figures below appear in at least two independent sources, including GOV.UK consultation material, the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT) and several trade and news outlets. Anything that appeared in only one place is marked "unconfirmed." The final policy has not been announced, so the 60% figure is reported, not official. Details are current as of 10 November 2026.

What the ZEV mandate is

The zero emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate is the UK's main tool for moving the new-car market away from petrol and diesel. It does not tell drivers what to buy. It tells manufacturers what proportion of their new-car sales in Great Britain must be zero-emission, which in practice means battery electric. Each year the required share rises. Makers that fall short can use various flexibilities, such as buying credits from other brands or borrowing against future years, and if they still miss the target they pay a charge for each car over the line.

The current trajectory for cars runs as follows:

YearRequired zero-emission share of new cars
202633%
202738%
202852%
202966%
203080%
2035100%

The steep climb after 2027 is the part the industry has been most worried about. Going from roughly a third of sales to four in five within four years requires demand, charging infrastructure and pricing to keep pace.

What the reports say

The Guardian reported, citing government sources, that ministers are expected to lower the 2030 target to around 60% and extend the flexibility mechanisms, which let makers carry over performance between years, out to 2034. AM Online, Energy Live News, Fleetpoint and a Reuters report republished by The Manila Times carried the same core figures. AM and EV Fleet World reported that a final decision could be taken at a meeting in Downing Street on Wednesday 11 November, with Louise Haigh, the First Secretary of State, expected to make the call.

A government spokesperson responded that the government fully supports the ZEV mandate and has not taken any decisions. That response was reported by both AM and Brit Brief. Until a formal announcement, the 60% figure should be treated as a likely outcome rather than settled policy.

EVXL wrote that the BBC had reported a cut to 50% was also on the table. We could not read the BBC article directly, so the 50% possibility remains unconfirmed.

What is not changing, according to the reports

Several outlets agree on two fixed points. The end of sales of new pure petrol and diesel cars in 2030 stays. The target of 100% zero-emission new cars by 2035 also stays. What is being adjusted is the slope between now and then. Since a set of changes in April 2025, full hybrids and plug-in hybrids can continue to be sold between 2030 and 2035, so the 2030 milestone already did not mean "electric only."

Why the government is reconsidering

The simplest answer is the gap between the target and real sales. According to SMMT data, battery electric cars made up 26.6% of new registrations from January to October 2026, below the 33% required this year. October on its own reached 32.0%, the highest monthly share of the year according to SMMT and electrive, but still under the line. SMMT chief executive Mike Hawes has said that the gap between demand and the mandate keeps widening, and the trade body has pushed for targets to be brought in line with what customers are actually buying. Trade unions have added to that pressure.

This review did not come out of nowhere. On 14 August 2026 the government opened a consultation on the mandate. According to the consultation documents and trade press coverage, it set out options to lower the 2030 car target to 70%, 60% or 50%, or to keep 80% while extending flexibilities to 2034. The reported 60% sits in the middle of that menu.

It is also not the first softening. In April 2025 the government cut the charge for each car short of the target from £15,000 to £12,000 and allowed hybrids to be sold until 2035.

On the other side, environmental groups and some Labour MPs have criticised the expected move as a step back on climate commitments. That debate is likely to continue after any announcement.

What it could mean for car buyers

For someone buying a car in the UK, the effects are indirect and harder to predict than the headline suggests. Nothing in the reported plan stops you buying an electric car, and nothing forces you to buy one.

  • Discounting pressure may ease. Part of the reason dealers have offered large discounts on electric cars is that makers needed to hit their quotas. A lower target could reduce the need to push volume through price cuts. Whether that actually happens, and by how much, is not something anyone can state with confidence yet.
  • Petrol choice may last a little longer in showrooms. With less pressure to limit combustion sales, some brands may keep more petrol and hybrid models on sale through the late 2020s. The 2030 end date for new pure petrol and diesel cars is reported to remain, though.
  • Government grants are separate. The Electric Car Grant is a different scheme from the mandate. Kia's EV4, for example, was reported by Fleet World and The EV Report to qualify for the top £3,750 grant from June 2026. Grant eligibility and amounts can change, so check the current list before you buy.
  • Running costs depend on your situation. Home charging, insurance, tax changes and resale values all affect what a car really costs you. A change in the mandate does not change those on its own.

What it could mean for carmakers

For manufacturers, a lower 2030 number mainly reduces compliance risk. Brands that have struggled to sell enough electric cars would face less exposure to per-car charges and less need to buy credits from rivals. Brands that have invested heavily in electric line-ups, and that have been selling credits, could see that advantage shrink. The reported extension of flexibilities to 2034 would give all makers more room to smooth out bad years.

Two things are still missing for planning purposes. First, the reports focus on 2030; it is not clear whether the interim targets for 2027 to 2029 will be redrawn. Second, it is not clear whether the per-car charge will change again. Both would matter more to a product planner than the 2030 headline.

The wider European picture

Readers in the EU should keep in mind that the UK mandate is a separate system from EU fleet CO2 rules. A UK decision does not change EU targets. It does, however, add to a broader conversation across Europe about how fast the electric transition can go when sales lag behind policy, and carmakers that sell in both markets will be planning around two sets of rules that may drift further apart.

What we still do not know

  • The final 2030 percentage. Around 60% is reported. The official figure is unconfirmed until the government publishes it.
  • Timing. Whether an announcement follows the reported 11 November meeting directly or comes with the formal consultation response has not been stated.
  • The 50% option. Reported second-hand from the BBC; unconfirmed.
  • Interim targets and charges. Whether the 2027 to 2029 steps and the £12,000 charge change has not been reported.

If you are close to ordering a car, do not rush or delay purely because of these reports. Compare the price you are offered today against two or three other dealers, check the current Electric Car Grant list on GOV.UK, and watch for the Department for Transport announcement after 11 November. When it comes, look for three numbers: the 2030 target, the end date for flexibilities, and the interim targets for 2027 to 2029. Those will tell you far more about future prices and model choice than any headline.
