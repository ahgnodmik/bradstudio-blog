A mother and her daughter went for a walk near their home in the Bella Coola Valley on British Columbia's central coast and did not come back. Officials believe they were killed by a grizzly bear, and possibly by more than one. This article sets out what authorities and multiple news outlets have confirmed so far, what remains open, and the practical steps that Parks Canada recommends for anyone hiking or camping in Canadian bear country.

Our thoughts are with the family and the Nuxalk and Bella Coola communities. This article avoids graphic detail on purpose. Facts below were reported by at least two outlets or come from official pages; anything we could find in only one place is marked unconfirmed. Details are current as of November 7, 2026, and may change as the investigation continues.

What happened

According to CBC News, CBS News and Backpacker, family members reported the pair missing after they did not return from a walk on Sunday, November 1. The BC Conservation Officer Service led the search. The mother was found on Monday, November 2, in the area of the Snooka trail system west of town, and her daughter was found on Tuesday afternoon, November 3, as reported by CBS News, CBC and Global News.

Family members identified them as Jennifer Balcarras, 48, and her daughter, Selah Reed. CBC and CBS give Selah's age as 20, while The Globe and Mail lists her as 19, so her exact age is unconfirmed. Relatives told CBC the two were extremely close and described them as "two peas in a pod."

More than one bear? What officials have and have not said

Inspector Jeff Tyre of the Conservation Officer Service said more than one grizzly may have been involved, according to NBC News (via the Associated Press) and CTV News. That is a possibility under investigation, not a finding. Officers were still trying to locate and capture bears as of the latest reports, and neither the number of animals nor the official cause of death has been formally confirmed. Every outlet we reviewed still describes this as a suspected attack.

Tyre also told The Globe and Mail that the valley holds a large grizzly population. Bella Coola sits on salmon rivers that draw bears down to the valley floor in the fall, which is part of why encounters near homes and short local trails are not unusual there. Whether seasonal conditions played a role in this case has not been addressed in any official statement we could find.

A second serious incident in the same valley in a year

This is not the first time the valley has made national news. On November 20, 2025, a grizzly attacked a group of schoolchildren and teachers on a walking trail near Bella Coola. The Associated Press, carried by Hawaii News Now and the Denver Gazette, reported 11 people injured. Conservation officers captured several bears in the weeks that followed, but Global News and Backpacker reported that the animal responsible was never identified before the search wound down for the winter.

The Globe and Mail reports that officers are looking at whether there is any connection between last year's attack and this one. No link has been announced. Treat any claim that it was "the same bear" as unconfirmed until officials say otherwise.

How the community is responding

CBC and The Globe and Mail report that the community put a curfew in place, asking residents to stay in after dark while the search goes on. Residents have been asked to stay away from the west section of the Snooka trail system, and conservation officers and RCMP officers are running night patrols. If you live in or are visiting the valley, follow local notices from the Nuxalk Nation, the RCMP and the Conservation Officer Service before anything you read online, including this article.

Bear safety in Canada: what Parks Canada actually recommends

Most of the millions of people who hike in Canada every year never have a problem with a bear. The guidance below is not about fear. It is a short list of habits that make a surprise meeting less likely and give you better options if one happens. It draws on Parks Canada's own bear-safety pages, which apply broadly across the country, not only inside national parks.

Before you go

  • Check closures and warnings. Parks Canada and BC Parks post trail closures and bear warnings online and at trailheads. A closure usually means bears are actively using the area. Respect it, even on a trail you know well.
  • Plan for daylight. Start early enough to finish well before dusk. Bears are often more active at dawn and dusk, and visibility is poorer.
  • Leave your plan with someone. Tell a friend which trail you are on and when you expect to be back. In this case, it was a family report that started the search.

On the trail

  • Go in a group. Parks Canada recommends hiking in a tight group, and says groups of four or more are best. Larger groups are more noticeable to bears.
  • Make noise. Call out, talk or clap, especially near rushing water, in thick brush, around berry patches, on windy days and around blind corners. Parks Canada notes that bear bells alone are not enough.
  • Keep your ears open. Parks Canada advises against wearing earbuds in bear country.
  • Control your dog. Dogs can provoke defensive behaviour in bears. In national parks, owners must keep pets under physical control at all times, which in practice means a leash.
  • Watch for signs. Fresh scat, tracks, dug-up ground or a carcass are reasons to turn back.

If you meet a bear

  • Stop, stay calm and do not run. Running can trigger a chase.
  • Get your bear spray ready. Remove the safety clip and know which way the nozzle points.
  • Back away slowly if the bear is not approaching, keeping it in view and giving it a clear way out.
  • Know that responses differ. Parks Canada explains that what you should do depends on whether a bear is acting defensively or approaching with interest, and the advice on playing dead versus fighting back is specific to each situation. Read the full guidance on the Parks Canada page for the area you are visiting before your trip; this article is not a substitute for it.

Bear spray: the rules visitors miss

Bear spray is a strong capsaicin-based deterrent designed for use on animals. Parks Canada recommends carrying it on the trail and says it must be within immediate reach, such as on a hip belt or chest strap, not buried in a pack. For use in its parks, Parks Canada also specifies that the product must be labelled for use on animals and must not exceed 500 ml.

You generally cannot fly with it. Parks Canada states that bear spray is not permitted on commercial aircraft, even in checked baggage. Some airport and airline guidance is worded less absolutely, but no source we found suggests carry-on is allowed, and many airlines refuse it in checked bags as well. The practical answer for most travellers is simple: buy or rent bear spray after you land. Outdoor shops in mountain towns sell it, and some, including outfitters in the Banff and Lake Louise area, rent it. If you are leaving by air, give unused cans to a friend or ask the shop about disposal rather than trying to pack them.

A few common-sense points make spray more useful:

  • Check the expiry date. Old cans can lose pressure.
  • Practise drawing it while wearing your pack, so the movement is automatic.
  • Never leave it in a hot car, where cans can be damaged.
  • Remember that wind and range matter. Spray is a close-range tool and is not a reason to approach wildlife.

What is still unknown

  • How many bears were involved: officials have raised the possibility of more than one, but this is unconfirmed.
  • Official cause of death: reports describe a suspected grizzly attack; no formal finding has been released.
  • Any link to the November 2025 school-group attack: being examined, not confirmed.
  • Whether any bears have been captured and what happens next: traps were reported, but outcomes have not been announced.
  • Selah Reed's age: reported as 19 by one outlet and 20 by others.
  • When the trail closure and curfew will lift: not announced.

What to do this week

If you have a hike or camping trip planned in British Columbia, Alberta or the Yukon, open the Parks Canada or BC Parks page for your destination tonight and check for bear warnings and closures. Add bear spray to your shopping list for your first stop after you land, not your packing list. On the trail, stay with your group, talk as you walk, keep the spray on your hip belt, and turn around if you see fresh signs. These habits will not make a trip risk-free, but they are the best tools hikers have.
