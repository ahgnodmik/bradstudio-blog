Two days after Americans voted, the headline is settled and the details are not. Major US networks have projected that Democrats will control both the House and the Senate when the new Congress meets in January, reversing the Republican hold on both chambers. For South Korea, a country whose exporters, carmakers, chipmakers and shipbuilders are deeply tied to Washington's trade decisions, the obvious question is whether anything changes for Seoul. The short answer from the analysis published so far: probably less than the size of the swing suggests, at least on tariffs.

This article reflects reporting available as of November 5, 2026 (Korea time). Final seat counts are unconfirmed, several races are still being counted, and the figures below may change. Nothing here is investment advice.

What has been projected so far

The midterm election took place on Tuesday, November 3, 2026. All 435 House seats were on the ballot, along with a share of the Senate. A party needs 218 seats for a House majority and 51 for a working Senate majority, since a 50-50 split is broken by the vice president, currently Republican JD Vance.

The House

NBC News and CBS News both projected a Democratic House majority on election night, and NBC reported that Democrats had passed the 218-seat threshold. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader, is widely expected to become Speaker, although that vote only happens once the new House convenes in January.

The final size of the majority is where the numbers diverge. Different outlets, counting at different moments, have published very different totals:

  • NBC News has projected that Democrats will end up with about 258 seats, a figure it describes as an estimate while its Decision Desk keeps calling individual races. GV Wire reported the same projection.
  • CBS News has estimated a final Democratic total somewhere between 251 and 268 seats.
  • CNBC's election coverage said Democrats were on course for a minimum of 225 seats once all races are complete.
  • Snapshot tallies of called races on November 4 ranged from the high 220s to the low 240s for Democrats, with dozens of districts still uncalled.

Treat any precise House number you see this week as provisional. The count of called seats and the projected final total are two different things, and headlines do not always say which one they mean.

The Senate

The Senate tipped on Wednesday. Forbes and TIME reported that the Associated Press called the chamber for Democrats after Josh Turek defeated Republican Ashley Hinson in Iowa. NBC's Senate tracker lists seven Republican-held seats flipping to Democrats: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas. In Maine, ABC News reported that five-term Republican Senator Susan Collins conceded to Democrat Troy Jackson.

NBC's tally put Democrats at 54 seats with two races still uncalled, and The Hill likewise counted seven Democratic pickups with Alaska and Mississippi outstanding. In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola held a narrow lead over Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in early counting, but the state counts absentee ballots for days and uses ranked-choice voting, so a result could take weeks. Mississippi was also described as too close to call.

Why this election got attention outside the US

Congress controls the federal budget, ratifies treaties, confirms senior officials and runs oversight hearings. A Congress run by the opposition party can slow or block a White House agenda, and that matters to every government that negotiates with Washington. ZDNet Korea noted that Democrats had not taken both chambers at once since 2006, the same benchmark the analysts at Inside Elections pointed to before the vote.

Governments abroad reacted quickly, and NPR, Al Jazeera and CBS each published roundups of those responses. For Korean readers, though, the more practical questions are about trade and markets.

The Korea angle: trade policy

The most detailed Korean read so far comes from KOTRA, the Korea Trade-Investment Promotion Agency, which published an analysis of the results that was covered by Newspim, Segye Ilbo, Aju Business Daily and The Herald Business. The main points, as reported across those outlets:

  • Tariffs are expected to stay. KOTRA's view is that current US tariff policy rests on presidential authority rather than new legislation, so a change in Congress does not by itself change the tariffs Korean exporters face.
  • Legislation faces a veto. Even if a Democratic Congress passes bills limiting the president's tariff powers, KOTRA expects such bills could be vetoed.
  • Legal basis has shifted, not disappeared. According to the Newspim and Segye coverage, KOTRA noted that the administration has continued to impose tariffs under Section 301 of the Trade Act and Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act.
  • Some sectors may benefit. Shipbuilding, semiconductors and power infrastructure were identified as areas with support from both parties, where new legislation could open opportunities for Korean companies.

That is a forecast from one agency, carried by several outlets, not a policy announcement. What bills the new Congress actually introduces after January, and whether any of them become law, is unconfirmed. The Korean government's official response was not found in the reporting reviewed for this article, and nothing specific has been reported on how the result might affect issues such as US Forces Korea, defense cost-sharing or the timeline of Korean investment pledges in the US.

The Korea angle: markets on November 4

Seoul's markets were open while American votes were being counted, which made for an unusually jumpy session. Both points below were reported by at least two outlets:

  • KOSPI: The benchmark index closed at 5,683.80, up 0.62%, according to News1, SBS Biz and Seoul Economic Daily. It swung by more than 400 points during the day before finishing higher. Seoul Economic Daily and other coverage linked the swings partly to sector moves, with chip stocks weak at one point and battery and green-energy shares gaining.
  • Won: The won closed at 1,341.5 per US dollar, weaker by 0.5 won, according to SBS Biz and Betanews.

Figures for net foreign selling that day differ widely between outlets, so they are left out here. It is also worth keeping in mind that a single trading day reflects many inputs, from US interest-rate expectations to company earnings, and the election's share of the move cannot be cleanly separated. This article makes no prediction about where Korean stocks or the won go next. It is not investment advice.

What is still unknown

  • Final seat counts. Both chambers have uncalled races. Final numbers depend on state counts and certification.
  • Alaska and Mississippi. Alaska's ranked-choice count could take weeks. Mississippi was reported as a cliffhanger.
  • Exact timing of the AP House call. Reports give slightly different times, so this is unconfirmed.
  • Actual trade legislation. KOTRA expects continuity, but what the new Congress proposes is not known yet.
  • Seoul's response. No official Korean government position or follow-up schedule has been reported.

What to do if your work touches US trade

If you export to the US, sell into American supply chains or manage a Korean company's US investment, a few practical steps make sense this week. Read KOTRA's own report on the election through its overseas market news service and find the section for your industry rather than relying on headlines. Pull out your US contracts and check who bears tariff costs if rates change in either direction. Note January 2027 in your calendar, when the new Congress convenes and committee chairs change, since that is when trade bills would start moving. And wait for the AP and state election offices to finish counting before quoting a final seat number to colleagues or clients.
