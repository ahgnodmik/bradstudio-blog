On Earth you tilt your head back and let a drop fall. In orbit, nothing falls, so a simple eye drop becomes an engineering problem.

A Korean research team says it has a fix. Scientists at KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology) and Seoul National University Bundang Hospital have built a wearable patch that pushes eye drops into the eye at the press of a button. The pressure comes from a reaction between vitamin C and baking soda. There is no battery and no pump.

One thing first. This is a research prototype, not a product. You cannot buy it. No price, maker or launch date has been announced. It has not been tested in space or in a clinical trial. Treat everything below as early lab work, not medical advice.

Why eye drops are hard in space

An ordinary eye drop relies on three things. Gravity pulls the drop down. A steady hand aims the bottle. The user holds the eye open long enough for the drop to land.

Microgravity removes the first one. Liquids in orbit are shaped mainly by surface tension, not weight. A drop squeezed from a bottle tends to cling to the tip instead of falling. A researcher at Michigan Medicine has pointed to a second risk: a floating astronaut may poke the bottle tip into the eye while trying to get a drop in.

Astronauts do need eye care. Spaceflight shifts body fluids toward the head. That shift is linked to changes in the eye and in vision, a condition NASA calls spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS). Medicine for SANS is still being studied, but any future eye treatment in orbit will need a reliable way to reach the eye.

Other teams are attacking the same problem from different angles:

  • Smaller drops. Nanodropper, a company in Rochester, Minnesota, sent its drop-shrinking bottle adapter to the International Space Station on NASA's SpaceX Crew-8 mission in 2024. Results from that test have not been published.
  • No drops at all. Hungarian astronaut Tibor Kapu tested a drug-free version of a solid eye insert on the Axiom-4 mission in 2025. The insert was developed by Spinsplit with Budapest University of Technology and Economics and Semmelweis University, as a possible replacement for frequent drops.
  • Pressure instead of gravity. That is the Korean approach.

How the patch works

The team was led by Professor Jeong Ki-hun of KAIST's Department of Bio and Brain Engineering and Professor Woo Se-joon of the ophthalmology department at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. Their paper appeared online on October 7, 2026, in the journal Sensors and Actuators B: Chemical. KAIST announced the work on October 21.

The patch is flexible, so it can sit on the skin around the eye. Inside are three key parts.

1. A chemical gas generator

One chamber holds ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and sodium bicarbonate (baking soda). When the user presses the patch, the two meet water and react. The reaction releases carbon dioxide. The gas raises the pressure inside the patch.

2. A gas-only filter

The reaction liquid must not touch the medicine. So the team placed a filter between the two sections. Gas passes through it. Liquid does not. The pressure reaches the eye drop, but the vitamin C and baking soda solution stays behind.

3. Microfluidic channels

Tiny channels carry the drop from its reservoir to the eye. The gas pressure pushes the liquid along these channels. Gravity plays no part, so the direction the patch faces should not matter.

The design is clever because the ingredients are cheap and familiar. Vitamin C and baking soda are kitchen-cupboard chemicals. Using them as a power source avoids electronics on the face.

What has been tested, and what has not

QuestionStatus as reported
Does it deliver drops in any orientation?Tested on the ground by rotating the patch relative to Earth's gravity. The team reports consistent delivery across orientations.
Does it work on a person?The team reports a wearing evaluation with delivery comparable to conventional drops. Participant numbers and conditions were not reported.
Does it work in real microgravity?Not tested yet.
Does it work with real medicines?Not tested yet. Planned.
Is it safe for repeated use and long storage?Not tested yet. Planned.
Is it approved or for sale?No. No regulatory filing has been announced.

The orientation test is the core result. It shows the patch does not depend on gravity to move liquid. It does not show the patch works in orbit. Parabolic flights, the space station or long-duration missions are a different environment, and the researchers say that validation is still ahead.

The human wearing results come from the team's own announcement. Press coverage did not give the number of volunteers, the test liquid or error ranges. That is not enough to say the patch matches a bottle in real use.

The team's stated next steps:

  • Experiments with real eye drop medicines
  • Tests under simulated space conditions
  • Checks on long-term storage stability and compatibility with different eye drop formulas

Who else could use it

The researchers also see a use on the ground. Many people struggle to put in their own drops. Examples include people with hand tremors from Parkinson's disease and older adults with weak grip or poor aim. A patch that sits in place and fires on a press could help them.

That use is still a hope, not a result. No patient study has been reported. If you or a family member find eye drops hard, the practical step today is to ask an eye doctor or pharmacist. They can show a better technique or suggest a bottle aid. Do not skip or change prescribed drops, such as glaucoma medicine, without talking to your doctor.

Where this fits in Korea's space push

Korea is building up its space program fast. The Korea AeroSpace Administration (KASA, Ujuhanggongcheong) opened on May 27, 2024, with headquarters in Sacheon, South Gyeongsang Province. At its launch, the government set goals of a lunar lander by 2032 and a Mars landing by 2045.

Crewed flights and long missions raise medical questions, and eye health is one of them. The KAIST patch is a university and hospital project. No KASA role in this research has been reported. It is best read as one early contribution from Korean labs to space medicine, not part of a national astronaut program.
