If a giant data center moved in next to your town, would there still be enough water and power for everyone else?

That question is now front-page news in Spain. Amazon, Microsoft and Blackstone are building huge server campuses in Aragon, a dry region in the northeast. NPR ran a long feature on the area on Oct. 22. Korea is asking a similar question, but with a different emphasis. This piece covers what is confirmed in Spain, why locals are pushing back, and how Korea's new rules compare.

The money flowing into Aragon

Amazon Web Services (AWS) is the biggest player. In May 2024, AWS said it would spend €15.7 billion on data centers in Aragon. Reuters and Iberian Property reported the same figure.

In March 2026, AWS added €18 billion more. The announcement came at Mobile World Congress in Barcelona. Its total commitment in Aragon is now €33.7 billion. The Spanish government (La Moncloa) welcomed it as a historic investment.

AWS sites are spread across all three provinces of Aragon: Zaragoza, Huesca and Teruel. NPR described the AWS pledge as close to $40 billion. That matches the euro figure after conversion.

Other companies followed:

  • Microsoft applied in 2024 to build a data center campus near Zaragoza. The reported value was €6.69 billion ($7.16 billion).
  • Blackstone, the private equity firm, plans a campus on 224 hectares in Calatorao, Zaragoza province. Phase one is reported at €7.5 billion. The finished campus would have 300 MW of power capacity.
  • Meta has pursued a separate data center in Talavera de la Reina, Toledo province, in central Spain. The regional government fast-tracked it as a priority project in 2023.

All these numbers are corporate pledges. None of the companies has published a firm spending timeline.

Why water is the flashpoint

Aragon is dry. Droughts are common. AFP reporting carried by The Local said residents are asked to save water in summer.

Data centers need water to cool servers. They also need a lot of electricity. That puts them in direct competition with farms and homes.

The 48% request

The most cited number is 48%. A joint investigation by The Guardian and SourceMaterial found that Amazon asked the regional government for a 48% increase in water permits at its three existing Aragon data centers. Amazon cited hotter weather and more frequent heat waves linked to climate change. Environmental Health News and other outlets carried the findings.

It is not confirmed whether the regional government approved the increase. Actual water use per site is mostly not public. Published figures mix permit limits, company statements and planning estimates.

Meta's Talavera plan

Meta's Toledo project also drew scrutiny. A technical report made public in 2023 put its need at about 660 million liters of water a year. The water would come from the Tagus river basin. We could not confirm whether the plan changed later.

Residents organize

Local opposition has a name. The citizens' group Tu Nube Seca Mi Río ("Your Cloud Dries My River") is the best known. Its spokesperson, Aurora Gómez, has said exact figures are hard to get, but the volumes are clearly huge. The group argues that big permits keep coming while farmers look for drought aid.

The industry says data centers bring clear gains in GDP and jobs. Aragon's regional president, Jorge Azcón, has said Aragon is becoming "the Virginia of southern Europe." Northern Virginia is the world's largest data center cluster.

Madrid's answer: an 80% renewables rule

Spain's central government stepped in this summer. On Aug. 25, the cabinet approved urgent processing of a draft royal decree on data centers. Reuters, pv magazine, idealista and DCD reported the details.

The main points of the draft:

  • New data centers of 1 MW or more must source at least 80% of their electricity from additional renewable generation.
  • New renewable capacity must match new demand, with compliance checked hour by hour.
  • Sites must meet water and energy efficiency standards.
  • Operators face EU data control and cybersecurity requirements.
  • Sites that fail to comply face rising grid surcharges and could lose grid access.
  • Existing facilities are not affected. Projects already in development get a grace period.

The industry pushed back hard. The trade association SpainDC warned that up to 90% of planned investment could be lost. Digital Transformation Minister Óscar López ruled out a moratorium on new data centers.

The decree is still a draft. Reports differ on how strict the hourly matching will be and how long the grace period lasts. The final text may change.

How Korea is handling the same question

Korea faces the same core problem. Where should data centers go, and how do they win power and local consent? In Korea, the fight is mostly about the grid and health fears, not water.

The Naver Yongin case

The landmark case is Naver in 2019. In 2017, Naver announced a second data center in Gongse-dong, Yongin, Gyeonggi Province. The planned investment was about 540 billion won. Residents protested over electromagnetic waves, power lines and pollution from diesel backup generators. Naver dropped the Yongin plan on June 13, 2019. It picked Sejong as its new site that October. The Korea Times and Korean outlets covered the reversal.

The pattern did not end there. Korea JoongAng Daily has described data centers as a new NIMBY target. Residents in places like Gimpo have hung banners opposing new projects. Concerns include high-voltage lines and fire risk.

The AI data center special act

On May 7, 2026, the National Assembly passed the Special Act on the Promotion of the AI Data Center Industry (AI deiteo senteo teukbyeolbeop). The Ministry of Science and ICT leads the policy. The law merged six bills with bipartisan support.

  • AI data centers outside the Seoul metropolitan area are exempt from the power system impact assessment, within a size limit.
  • Permits move through a one-stop window.
  • A "timeout" rule treats a permit as granted if officials miss the deadline.
  • A clause on direct LNG power purchase deals was dropped at the last minute.

The law takes effect in February 2027. The size threshold for the exemption depends on an enforcement decree that is not yet out. Civil society groups say the act lacks basic environmental rules, such as a renewable energy requirement. Some Korean media also say it lacks a real plan for power supply.

Spain and Korea, side by side

The two countries are moving in opposite directions. Spain is adding conditions: renewables, efficiency and grid penalties. Korea is removing hurdles to push data centers out of the capital region. Both face the same trade-off. Faster permits can mean less time for residents to weigh in.

What is still unknown

  • Actual water use at each Spanish site.
  • Whether Amazon's 48% permit increase was approved.
  • Construction progress at the Microsoft and Meta sites.
  • The final wording of Spain's decree.
  • The exemption threshold in Korea's enforcement decree.

What you can do

If you live in Korea and want to know whether a data center is planned near you, check your city or county office notices (gosi). Watch for power system impact assessment announcements too. Comment windows can be short, so note the deadline the day a notice goes up. If you live in Spain, follow your regional government's water permit filings and the public consultation on the data center decree.
