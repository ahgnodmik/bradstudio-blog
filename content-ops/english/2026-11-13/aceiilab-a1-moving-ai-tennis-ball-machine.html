If you practise tennis alone, you know the limits of a standard ball machine: it sits in one spot, so after a few minutes you can read where every ball is coming from. AceiiLab, a young startup founded by former DJI engineers, is selling a machine that tries to fix that by driving itself around the court. The AceiiLab A1, first launched on Kickstarter as the Aceii One, uses cameras to watch the player and the ball, then moves and changes the point it feeds from. This explainer covers how it works, how it differs from fixed ball machines and rally robots, what it costs, and which claims still rest only on the maker's own material.

How we checked: the specifications below appear in at least two independent reports, including New Atlas, Notebookcheck, Homecrux, Gadget Flow, CyberGuy, TennisNerd, Yanko Design and 36Kr. Anything carried by only one outlet is marked "unconfirmed." We found no independent hands-on review, so performance claims come from AceiiLab and are attributed accordingly. Details are current as of 12 November 2026.

What the AceiiLab A1 is

The product appeared on Kickstarter in September 2025 under the name Aceii One, and Notebookcheck, Homecrux and Gadgets & Wearables covered the launch at the time. The company later consolidated its branding as AceiiLab and renamed the machine A1, according to TennisNerd and 36Kr. Some retailers still list it under the old name, so search for both if you are comparing prices.

The campaign passed its funding goal. Kicktraq recorded 604 backers and roughly HK$5.55 million in pledges, while 36Kr described total funding of more than US$820,000. The two figures were captured at different times and in different currencies, so the final total is unconfirmed.

On the company side, 36Kr Japan and Dealroom both reported that AceiiLab was founded in December 2024, that its founding team came from drone maker DJI, and that it raised about 10 million yuan in angel funding. Reports place the company in Shenzhen or describe it as a Hong Kong startup, so we do not state a headquarters here.

How it works

Two features set the A1 apart: vision and mobility.

  • Vision: Notebookcheck reported two AI-driven stereo cameras that track the player's position on court. Homecrux described them as two vertically stacked 1080p cameras running at 60 frames per second. 36Kr said the stereo vision system recognises the ball, the player and the ball's flight path.
  • Mobility: Wheels and motors under the body let the machine reposition itself during a session. Outlets describe the drive layout differently, so we do not detail it.
  • Varied feeding: According to 36Kr, the machine tracks where your shots land and uses that to vary its next feed, and it can shift between spots on its own side, such as near the baseline or the service line.
  • Video review: Notebookcheck reported that the machine records your strokes so you can watch them on a phone after practice. A structured coaching programme in the app is mentioned in promotional material, but we could not confirm its content in a second source.

How it compares with other options

A conventional ball machine is parked on one side of the court. You set speed, spin, interval and direction, and some models randomise left and right. The starting point never moves. The A1's pitch is that the origin of the ball changes, which is closer to what a real opponent does.

At the other end of the spectrum are rally robots such as Acemate, which try to return the ball you hit. Notebookcheck pointed out that the Aceii One is not that kind of machine. It feeds fresh balls from its hopper rather than hitting your shot back. Think of it as sitting between a fixed feeder and a true rally partner.

The speed and interval numbers match across sources. Gadget Flow's spec sheet and CyberGuy both list a top speed of 80 mph, and CyberGuy said the interval can be set from 0.5 to 15 seconds. Gadget Flow and another product write-up agree on the 120-ball capacity and say the machine works on hard, clay and grass courts. Spin and angle are adjustable, but we found no published spin figures.

Portability is a major selling point. New Atlas said the A1 folds into a wheeled, suitcase-style form, and TechBuzz Ireland compared it to carry-on luggage. Yanko Design wrote that it fits in a car's trunk, but we could not confirm its weight or folded dimensions in a second source, and whether it fits will depend on your car.

Price and availability

Pricing has moved around. During the Kickstarter campaign, New Atlas reported a backer price of US$1,099, while Homecrux listed a super early bird price of US$1,199, which suggests tiered pledge levels. New Atlas also said the planned retail price was US$2,099, a figure we did not find elsewhere. In November 2026, TennisNerd reported a US store list price of US$2,599 with a Black Friday price of US$1,999. That too comes from a single outlet, so treat it as unconfirmed and check the official store before buying. TennisNerd itself described its article as a preview based on manufacturer information rather than a hands-on test.

Shipping appears to have started. 36Kr and Dealroom both reported that overseas volume deliveries had begun, and TennisNerd said the A1 is on sale through the US store. Plans for online sales in mainland China appear in only one report and remain unconfirmed.

Who it suits, and who can skip it

The A1 makes the most sense for players who already have solid basic strokes and want practice that feels less predictable: moving to the ball, adjusting to feeds from different spots, and reviewing footage afterwards. Club players who practise alone several times a week, or coaches who want a second feeder, are the obvious audience.

If you are still grooving a forehand, a fixed ball machine may do the job for much less money. Repetition from one spot is exactly what beginners need. If what you really want is live exchanges, a rally robot or a hitting partner is a different category altogether.

Practical questions matter at this price. Ask how long the battery lasts in moving mode on a real court, since only one report gave a figure. Ask who handles repairs in your country, because a wheeled machine with cameras and motors has more parts that can fail than a basic feeder. Check whether your club allows motorised equipment on its courts, and whether the machine needs a flat surface to navigate reliably. If you plan to fly with it, confirm the airline's rules for lithium batteries before you book.

Before you order, compare the official AceiiLab store price against at least one retailer listing under the Aceii One name, ask the seller in writing about the warranty, repair route and return window in your country, and wait for at least one independent hands-on review if you can. If you need a machine this season, borrow or rent a fixed ball machine for a few sessions first so you know how much you would really use the moving features.
