If you hold Korean shares or get paid in won, the last two trading days have been hard to read. The KOSPI jumped more than 6% on Tuesday 24 November, then gave all of it back on Wednesday 25 November, closing at 4,697.51. On Thursday 26 November the Bank of Korea's Monetary Policy Board meets to set the Base Rate, currently 3.00%. This piece sets out where the KOSPI stands after the 25 November close, what the Bank of Korea is expected to decide, and what each outcome could mean for foreign investors and the won. This is not investment advice.

KOSPI after the 25 November close: the numbers

The KOSPI closed at 4,697.51 on Wednesday 25 November, down 308.52 points, or 6.16%, from the previous session. Seoul Economic Daily, Korea Daily and JKN all reported the same close. One day earlier, on Tuesday 24 November, the index had risen 6.32% to 5,006.03, according to Korea JoongAng Daily and MarketScreener. In two sessions the benchmark went from below 4,710 to above 5,000 and back under 4,700.

The two moves had the same engine: large semiconductor stocks. Tuesday's rally was led by chipmakers after reports that Jensen Huang had returned to Nvidia. Wednesday's fall followed a weak session for US artificial intelligence and chip shares overnight. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix, which carry a heavy weight in the index, fell sharply on Wednesday, Korea Daily and Businesskorea reported.

Who sold: foreign investors and the KOSPI

Foreign investors were net sellers of about 1.9 trillion won of Korean shares on 25 November. The exact number varies by outlet. Newsclaim and Businesskorea reported 1.8993 trillion won, while a Yonhap report carried by Nate gave 1.9527 trillion won. The gap comes from different counting points during the day, not from a disagreement about direction.

That matters because foreign money tends to move the KOSPI's largest names first. When overseas funds cut exposure to Korean chipmakers, the index falls faster than the broader market. When they buy back, as they did on Tuesday, the index can rise just as quickly. Two 6% swings in a row are a sign of how concentrated the market's direction has become.

The won: why it barely moved

You might expect a 6% stock market drop with heavy foreign selling to hit the currency hard. It did not. The won closed at 1,343.2 per US dollar on 25 November in Seoul, only 0.8 won weaker than Tuesday's close of 1,342.4, according to Seoul Finance, News1 and Segye Ilbo.

Korean reports point to exporters. Month-end dollar selling by exporters, especially semiconductor companies converting overseas revenue into won, absorbed much of the demand from foreigners leaving the stock market. News1 described the rate as rising only slightly despite the stock market plunge.

For anyone sending money in or out of Korea, the practical point is this: the won has been sitting in a narrow band in the low 1,340s, even through two days of extreme equity swings. A rate decision is one of the few scheduled events that could push it out of that band.

Bank of Korea rate decision on 26 November: what is expected

At the time of writing, the decision has not been announced. The Monetary Policy Board meets on Thursday 26 November, and the result is usually released in the morning, Korea time. Check the Bank of Korea's website or a major outlet for the confirmed result before acting on anything here.

Here is the background. The Bank of Korea raised the Base Rate by 25 basis points in July and again on 27 August 2026, taking it from 2.75% to 3.00%, according to the Bank's own August decision notice, Seoul Economic Daily and The Asia Business Daily. On 22 October the board held at 3.00% in a unanimous vote, The Korea Times and KED Global reported, while leaving the door open to a move in November.

Most forecasters now expect a 25-basis-point hike to 3.25%. DBS said it expects that outcome, and an Aju Press poll of economists put the year-end rate at 3.25% despite the stock market volatility. UPI described the bank as weighing another increase. A minority of economists see a hold, citing weak household consumption and the stock market's sharp swings. The case for a hike rests on inflation that has stayed above the bank's 2% target and on strong semiconductor exports.

The board also publishes its members' projections for the Base Rate six months ahead. That chart will tell investors whether a 3.25% rate is the end of the cycle or a step toward something higher. Several forecasters see a peak around 3.50%, according to KED Global and other previews. Treat that as a forecast, not a plan.

What each outcome could mean for foreign investors

None of the following is a prediction. These are the textbook channels through which a rate decision usually reaches markets.

  • A 25-basis-point hike to 3.25%, as expected: markets have largely priced this in. The bigger signal will come from the projection chart and the governor's press conference. A clear hint that this is the last hike could ease pressure on rate-sensitive stocks. A hint of more to come could support the won but weigh on equities.
  • A hold at 3.00%: this would surprise most of the market. A hold can weaken a currency at the margin, because the gap between Korean and US yields stays wider. It could also be read as the bank responding to the stock market's turbulence, which some investors would welcome.
  • A larger move: no preview we found expects one. If it happens, expect sharp moves in both bonds and the won.

For foreign investors who measure returns in dollars, euros or yen, the currency side matters as much as the share price. A stronger won adds to returns when you convert back. A weaker won subtracts from them. That is why a central bank decision can matter to a foreign holder of Korean shares even if the KOSPI itself does not move much on the day.

What residents and expats in Korea should watch

If you live in Korea and borrow in won, a hike usually flows into variable-rate mortgages and credit lines over the following weeks, not overnight. Banks adjust their reference rates on their own schedules. If you hold won deposits, new fixed-term deposit rates tend to move first.

If you are paid in won and send part of your salary abroad, the decision may nudge the exchange rate. Splitting a large transfer into two or three smaller ones over a few weeks is a simple way to avoid converting everything at a single bad rate. It does not guarantee a better rate. It spreads the timing risk.

What to do now

On Thursday morning, look up the confirmed Base Rate on the Bank of Korea's English website and read the board's projection chart, not only the headline number. Then check the won's closing rate that afternoon against the 1,343.2 close on 25 November. If you hold Korean shares, write down your reason for owning each position before the market opens, so a third straight day of large swings does not push you into a decision you have not thought through.
