If your business ships Korean-made goods to the United States, or your job depends on a company that does, you need to know whether Washington's newest tariffs raise your costs. On Wednesday 25 November 2026, the Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) proposed new Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners, including South Korea, over what it calls structural excess capacity in manufacturing. This explainer covers what was announced, how much Korea is exposed, why Seoul says its 15% cap still holds, and what happens between now and the end of January 2027.

What the US announced on 25 November

USTR published proposed tariff actions against 14 economies at the end of an investigation it opened in March 2026. CNBC, Reuters (via US News and Investing.com), Al Jazeera and the law firm Baker McKenzie all reported the announcement on the same day. The investigation looked at whether governments promote factory output that runs ahead of real demand, through subsidies and industrial policy, and whether that hurts US commerce.

The 14 economies named are China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam. Mexico and Norway, which were part of the original investigation, were left out for now, Reuters reported.

These are proposals, not tariffs in force. Nothing is collected at the US border until USTR finishes a comment and hearing process and publishes a final action.

Why Section 301 and why now

The administration is using Section 301 after the US Supreme Court struck down the broad tariffs it had imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, CNBC reported. Section 301 lets USTR act against foreign practices it finds unreasonable or discriminatory, but it requires an investigation, public comment and hearings. That is why the process runs into 2027.

Korea has already been through one round of this. In July 2026 USTR finalised a separate Section 301 tariff of 12.5% on Korean goods, in an investigation linked to forced labour, the Financial News and other Korean outlets reported. Seoul and the Korea International Trade Association had asked for the duty to be dropped or cut to 10%. USTR kept it at 12.5%.

Korea's exposure: the 15% cap

The number Korean exporters care about is 15%. Under the trade deal Seoul reached with Washington last year, Korea agreed to a large investment package in the United States, reported at 350 billion dollars, in exchange for tariffs being lowered from 25% to 15%. The Korean government has argued since then that any new Section 301 duties must stay within that 15% ceiling, a position Industry Minister Kim Jung-kwan repeated in Washington, according to The Korea Times.

The 25 November proposal appears to respect that line. The Asia Business Daily, Financial News and several Korean wire reports said Korea's combined rate would stay within 15%. With 12.5% already in place from the forced-labour action, Korean reports put the room left for the new excess-capacity duty at about 2.5 percentage points.

There is a catch. USTR's proposal for Korea includes an alternative rate of up to 25% if it finds Korea's consultations and commitments unsatisfactory, according to trade advisers and the Kyunghyang Shinmun. In practice, the 15% figure depends on Seoul continuing to deliver on its side of the deal.

Which Korean policies the US cited

USTR pointed to sectors including automobiles, semiconductors and petrochemicals, Korean reports said. According to Kyunghyang Shinmun, the findings also named government programmes that support semiconductors and artificial intelligence as part of the unfair-trade case. USTR head Jamieson Greer cited Korean government support and infrastructure plans as grounds for the excess-capacity rate, according to Korean broadcast and wire reports carried by Nate.

The Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources has said it rejects the overcapacity allegations. In a submission earlier this year it pointed to its own efforts to cut excess capacity, including restructuring of the petrochemical industry, The Korea Times reported.

What is not yet clear

  • Product coverage: USTR has asked for comments on which products should be included or removed, and whether rates should be adjusted for existing Section 301 and Section 232 duties, according to Baker McKenzie. Some reports said semiconductors, cars, steel and aluminium are left out because they fall under separate tariffs; we could not confirm that in two independent sources, so treat it as unconfirmed.
  • A separate digital probe: one report raised the risk that a different Section 301 investigation into Korea's digital regulations could add duties and test the 15% ceiling. This is unconfirmed.
  • Final timing: Korean reports quoted a senior US official as saying actual collection could slip beyond January. A precise effective date has not been published.

How the rest of the world reacted

China's Foreign Ministry called overcapacity "a false issue" but did not announce retaliation, according to CNBC and Al Jazeera. The Coalition for a Prosperous America, a US group that backs tariffs, welcomed the proposals. Japan and the EU, both named in the proposal, have their own trade deals with Washington and can make their case in the same comment process.

Who in Korea should pay attention

Exporters of finished manufactured goods to the United States are the obvious group. Suppliers deeper in the chain should watch as well, because a US buyer facing a higher landed cost often asks its suppliers to share it. Automobiles, semiconductors and petrochemicals are the sectors named in Korean coverage of the findings. If you work for a Korean company with US sales, expect internal guidance on pricing and contracts over the next two months.

For consumers in Korea the direct effect is small. These are US import duties paid on goods entering the United States. Indirect effects, through exporters' profits, hiring and the won, are harder to predict and depend on the final rates.

Timeline from here

  • Now to 5 January 2027: written comments and requests to appear at hearings are due.
  • From 25 January 2027: public hearings begin; rebuttal comments follow, according to USTR's notice.
  • After the hearings: USTR publishes final rates, product lists and an effective date. No new excess-capacity duty applies before then.

What to do now

If you export to the United States, pull your top 20 product codes this week and ask your customs broker to map them against USTR's proposed lists once the Federal Register notice is published. Note 5 January 2027 as the deadline if your industry association plans to file comments, and send them your data before mid-December so they have time to use it. If you sign new US contracts before February, add a clause on who pays any new duty. Then check the final notice after the January hearings before changing prices.
