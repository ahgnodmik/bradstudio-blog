If you live in Korea and your phone has never buzzed with a snow warning, that changes this winter. Korea's 2026–27 winter disaster plan expands heavy snow alerts to all 227 city, county and district areas and raises the number of cold-weather shelters to about 66,000. This explainer covers what the plan changes, how Korea's emergency alerts work, how to read them if you do not read Korean, and a short checklist for residents and expats before the first big freeze.

Korea's 2026–27 winter disaster plan at a glance

The Ministry of the Interior and Safety announced its comprehensive winter natural disaster plan on 11 November 2026. The official response period runs from 15 November 2026 to 15 March 2027. For that whole period the government keeps the heavy snow and cold wave crisis alert at the lowest level, "Attention," so that central and local authorities stay on a standing 24-hour watch. The Korea Herald's business edition, Asia Business Daily and several Korean outlets reported the same dates.

The Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) expects temperatures this winter to be similar to or above normal, with precipitation similar to or above normal. A mild forecast does not rule out sharp cold snaps or heavy snow on individual days, so the plan is built around short, intense events.

Heavy snow alerts now cover all 227 districts

Last winter the KMA tested heavy snow alerts in part of the country, including the capital region. From this winter it sends them nationwide. Reports from Kyunghyang Shinmun and News1, published on 26 November, said the nationwide rollout began on 23 November. Earlier coverage had given different start dates, so we use the date in the most recent reports.

When a heavy snow alert is sent

The KMA sends the alert directly, district by district, in two situations:

  • Traffic risk: 5 cm or more of new snow builds up within one hour.
  • Structural collapse risk: 20 cm or more of new snow within 24 hours, plus 3 cm or more per hour on top of that.

Gangwon Province and Ulleung County in North Gyeongsang Province, where heavy snow is routine, use a higher 24-hour threshold of 30 cm. The snow alert is sent as a safety guidance message, the lowest alert tier, so it arrives without a loud warning tone. That makes it easy to miss if your phone is in a bag.

How emergency alerts work in Korea

Korean alerts arrive by cell broadcast. Every compatible phone connected to towers in the affected area receives the message, whether or not you are a Korean citizen and whatever mobile carrier you use. You do not need to sign up, and the message does not use your data allowance.

The three-tier structure and the rule that critical alerts cannot be blocked are described by HuffPost Korea, Health Kyunghyang and the government's own policy portal, Korea.kr (all in Korean). The exact volume rules have been revised over the years, so we describe the tones in relative terms only.

Reading alerts if you do not read Korean

Alerts are written mainly in Korean. Since early 2025, nationally issued alerts also carry a short English keyword for the type of event, such as "Earthquake," and its scale, according to The Korea Times and the Busan Metropolitan City English news page. That tells you what is happening but not what to do.

For the full message in another language, install the government's Emergency Ready app (안전디딤돌 in Korean). It is run by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety. The Korea Immigration Service's English notice lists alerts in English, Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese and Thai, plus shelter locations and safety guides. A Busan city page describes a wider set of 22 languages; we could not confirm that figure with the ministry, so treat it as unconfirmed. The app relies on mobile data, while cell broadcast does not, so expect the app version to arrive a little later.

Cold-weather shelters: about 66,000 this winter

The number of designated cold-weather shelters rises from about 53,000 last winter to about 66,000. The extra sites come mainly from agreements with banks, post offices and other private facilities, on top of familiar places such as senior centres and community service centres. The Korea Herald, MTN and other Korean outlets reported the same figures.

Opening hours and who may use each shelter vary by site. Local governments publish their own lists, and many have added convenience stores, local bank branches and community halls on top of the national count. Hours can also change once a severe cold snap hits, so check again on the day rather than relying on a list you saved weeks ago. If a shelter is a bank branch, check whether it is open outside banking hours.

Resident evacuation support teams in winter

Teams of local residents, including neighbourhood heads and volunteer disaster prevention groups, have helped with evacuations during summer downpours. This winter they will be used for heavy snow for the first time. They identify people who would struggle to leave on their own, such as older or disabled residents in greenhouse-style homes or isolated mountain villages, and go door to door when risk rises. About 5,100 people have been designated as priority evacuees.

Cold-related illness: last winter's numbers

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) counted 364 people treated in emergency rooms for cold-related illness last winter, with 14 estimated deaths. Hypothermia accounted for most cases. A notable share of cases happened at or near home, not only outdoors. News1 and the KDCA's own release (both in Korean) give the same totals. This is general information, not medical advice.

Questions residents and expats ask

Do foreigners receive Korean emergency alerts?

Yes. Cell broadcast reaches every compatible phone in the area, including phones on prepaid or foreign-resident plans. Whether a roaming phone from abroad shows them depends on the handset and its settings, so short-term visitors should install Emergency Ready as a backup.

Why did I not hear the snow alert?

Heavy snow alerts are sent as safety guidance messages, which use your normal notification sound. If you mute messages, you may miss them. Check that emergency and public safety alerts are switched on in your phone's settings.

Who do I call in an emergency?

Dial 119 for fire and medical emergencies and 112 for police. For non-emergency questions in English, the Korea Travel Hotline at 1330 and, in Seoul, the 120 Dasan Call Center can help.

How do I find the nearest shelter?

Check your city, county or district office website, ask at your local community service centre, or use the shelter map inside Emergency Ready.

Your winter checklist for this weekend

  • Open your phone's notification settings and confirm that emergency alerts and public safety alerts are switched on.
  • Install Emergency Ready and set your preferred language.
  • Find the cold-weather shelter nearest your home and your workplace, and share both with your household.
  • Insulate your water meter box with old clothes or foam, and on very cold nights leave a tap running slightly to stop pipes freezing.
  • Save 119, 112 and 1330 in your contacts, and call any older neighbour or relative who lives alone to check they know where to go.
