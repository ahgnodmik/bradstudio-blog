If your company was waiting on Nvidia-based AI servers for a data centre in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Indonesia, those machines may not arrive any time soon. On 26 November 2026 the Financial Times reported that Nvidia had told its server-building partners to stop shipping to all four countries. The trigger is a new US export rule on remote access to advanced chips that took effect a week earlier. This piece separates what is reported from what is confirmed, explains the rule behind the Nvidia Southeast Asia shipment halt, and lists the open questions that will decide what happens next.

This article is for general information only and is not investment advice.

What the Nvidia Southeast Asia shipment halt report says

According to the FT, which cited three people familiar with the matter, Nvidia instructed manufacturing partners to hold back AI servers bound for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. Server makers including Dell and Super Micro then told customers in those countries that Nvidia would not allow their orders to ship. The report was carried by Reuters via US News, and by TechCrunch, Tom's Hardware, Quartz, Heise and Tech Times.

Three caveats matter here:

  • Reuters could not verify the FT report. Reuters said so explicitly when it relayed the story.
  • Nvidia did not comment. Neither did Dell nor Super Micro when asked, according to Reuters and Tom's Hardware.
  • The stop came from Nvidia, not from a US government order. The reports describe a company decision taken to comply with the new rule, not a direct instruction from Washington.

What is being held back is described as finished AI servers, the racks a data centre plugs in and runs, rather than loose GPUs. Which models are covered has not been made public. The FT's sources said it was unclear whether the pause is temporary while Nvidia and its partners work out how to comply.

The US remote-access rule behind the halt

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) released an interim final rule titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" on 16 November 2026. It took effect on 19 November. Law firm analyses from Gibson Dunn, Greenberg Traurig, K&L Gates and Willkie Farr all describe the same core pieces.

Five countries now need a licence

Exports, re-exports and in-country transfers of covered advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now require a US licence. BIS treats these destinations as carrying a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. Brazil is covered by the rule but was not among the four countries in the FT report on Nvidia.

Remote access is now in scope

The rule targets a gap that older controls missed. A Chinese company did not need to import a chip to use it. It could rent compute in a data centre abroad and run its workloads remotely. The new rule requires a licence to supply advanced chips to data centres anywhere that provide large amounts of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other restricted destinations. Several law firm notes give the threshold as 12 million aggregate TPP (total processing performance).

An exception for US-headquartered companies

Qualifying transactions with US-headquartered end users are carved out, subject to monthly reporting. In practice that means large US cloud providers can keep building in the region on easier terms than local operators. Analysts quoted by Asia Times and others have described this as a two-tier system.

The AI Diffusion Rule is gone

The same interim final rule formally rescinded the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule. Greenberg Traurig and Nixon Peabody both flagged this.

The Megaspeed blacklisting

A second move followed within days. On 23 November, BIS added Singapore-based Megaspeed and six related entities to the Entity List, the US trade blacklist. Semafor broke the story, and Tech Times, Benzinga and Technology.org reported the details. The seven entities are located in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand. Reports name an Alibaba-linked Singapore company, Apex, among them. Exports of US-origin items to listed entities now need a licence, with a presumption of denial.

Several outlets reported that this was the first Entity List addition in more than a year. The rule was placed on public inspection on 23 November and scheduled for Federal Register publication on 25 November, according to Unite.AI and Technology.org.

How Malaysia and Singapore have responded

Malaysia took a hard line. On 18 November, trade minister Johari Abdul Ghani told data centre operators that they must follow US rules if they want to keep operating in Malaysia, and that those unwilling to comply should not do business there. The Star and Malaysiakini both reported the remarks. He said the government had not seen documentary evidence that data centre tenants had re-exported chips, that Megaspeed's local unit was under investigation, and that Malaysia was seeking clarification from Washington on how the rule treats compute services as opposed to physical chips.

Singapore said it was studying the rule and engaging with affected companies, according to The Star, Asia News Network and The Korea Times. Officials said they would seek "trusted access" to advanced chips for legitimate Singapore businesses while not allowing export controls to be circumvented.

We did not find an official public response from Thailand or Indonesia in two independent sources, so we do not report one.

What it means for data centre projects in the region

Malaysia, especially the Johor area, and Singapore have attracted large data centre investment plans over the past two years. The new rule changes the economics for operators that are not US-headquartered: they need licences, must know who their end customers are, and face uncertainty over timing.

Financing felt it first. Yahoo Finance and TokenPost, both citing Bloomberg, reported that lenders paused a loan of about US$480 million to a Singapore cloud company that planned to buy Nvidia GPUs for a Malaysian facility linked in reports to ByteDance. One other report put the figure at about US$500 million, so treat the exact amount as approximate.

For anyone following the companies involved, the useful questions are practical ones. How quickly does BIS issue licences? Do US hyperscalers absorb demand that local operators can no longer serve? Does the hold extend to other server brands? None of these has a public answer yet.

What is still unknown

  • Restart date: No timetable for resuming shipments has been reported.
  • Products covered: The specific server and GPU models affected have not been disclosed.
  • Licence guidance: How long licence reviews will take, and how many will be approved, is not known.
  • Grace period for existing data centres: Some reports mention a 90-day window for facilities already providing compute. We could not confirm the exact cut-off date in two sources, so it is unconfirmed.
  • Nvidia's position: Nvidia has not publicly confirmed the halt.

Questions readers are asking

Did the US government ban Nvidia from shipping to Southeast Asia?

No. The US now requires licences for covered chips to five countries. The reported shipment stop is Nvidia's own decision while it works out compliance.

Is Brazil affected?

Brazil is one of the five licence countries under the rule. It was not named in the FT report on Nvidia's server halt.

Can US cloud companies still serve customers in Singapore and Malaysia?

Qualifying US-headquartered companies are excluded from the new licence requirement, subject to reporting. Each company's position depends on its own compliance setup.

What to watch next

The first public signal of any change will most likely come from official documents rather than company statements. Check the BIS press release page at bis.gov and search the Federal Register for "remote access advanced semiconductors" for licence guidance or amendments. If you work with a data centre operator in the region, ask it now for its licence plan and expected delivery dates in writing.
