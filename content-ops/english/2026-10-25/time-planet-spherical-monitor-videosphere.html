Almost every monitor on a desk is a black rectangle, and if you want something that looks like a space-age prop instead, your options are thin.

The Time Planet is one of them. It is a round monitor sold online by a small brand called iRetroLab. Its shape copies the JVC Videosphere, a helmet-shaped TV from 1970. It is a real product with a store page, not a concept render. But the seller shares very little about itself. This guide covers what the product is, where the design comes from, what it costs, and what to check before you pay.

What the Time Planet is

The US design site Core77 introduced the product. Its headline called it a spherical computer monitor that pays homage to a 1970 JVC design. Tom's Hardware Italia covered the same product as a monitor shaped like a sphere. The seller is iRetroLab, also written as Retro Lab. The product name is The Time Planet.

The shape is the whole point. A round body holds a screen on its front face. A stand holds the ball up from below. The silhouette is close to the original Videosphere. The big difference is the job. The original was a TV. The Time Planet is a display you connect to a computer.

Reported specs (unconfirmed)

Core77 gave a few details. We found them in that one article only, so treat each as unconfirmed.

  • Screen: 9.6 inches, reportedly a Samsung or LG panel of the kind used in iPads.
  • Body: 3D printed.
  • Stand: includes a mechanical flip clock that runs on AA batteries.

The iRetroLab store listing mentions three colors: yellow, orange and white. It also says custom-made units ship within 15 days. Both details come from the store alone, so they are unconfirmed too.

The gadget site The Awesomer reported that Retro Lab also showed a version, made with a partner named KAI, that holds a small computer such as a Mac mini. We could not confirm whether that is the same product or a separate model.

The original: JVC Videosphere, 1970

The Videosphere was a cathode-ray tube (CRT) TV made by the Japanese company JVC. Production ran from 1970 into the early 1980s. Wikipedia and the Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum in New York give the same period.

Cooper Hewitt holds a Videosphere in its collection. The museum describes it as a plastic and acrylic object that captured the excitement of space exploration. Many people read the shape as an astronaut's helmet, a nod to the Apollo 11 moon landing in 1969. Another common reading links it to the 1968 film "2001: A Space Odyssey."

Size matters here. A 9.6-inch screen is less than half the diagonal of a typical office monitor. It is too small for spreadsheets or long documents. It fits better as a second screen for a clock, music controls or system stats.

What to check before you buy

1. Who you are buying from

Core77 noted that the company has no track record and does not say where it is based. That is a real risk. A seller with no public address makes returns and repairs harder. Before you order, email the seller. Ask where units ship from and how returns work. A clear, fast reply is a good sign. No reply is an answer too.

2. The final price, with shipping and import costs

The reported price and the store price already differ by about $80. Shipping adds more. If you live in Korea or another country outside the seller's base, import duty and VAT can apply. In Korea, the Korea Customs Service (Gwanse-cheong) handles this. Check its current rules for personal imports before you pay. No official Korean retailer has been found, so overseas direct purchase (jikgu) is the realistic route.

3. Ports and power

Public sources do not list the input ports. They do not say which cable connects to a laptop. They do not list the power adapter voltage. Ask the seller for all three. If you live in Korea, confirm the adapter supports 220V.

4. Repairs and payment protection

A 3D printed body and a reused tablet panel may be hard to fix locally. Ask about warranty terms in writing. Pay with a credit card, and learn your card issuer's dispute process for overseas purchases before you need it.

5. The alternative: a real Videosphere

If you want the original look, used Videospheres turn up for sale. They are CRT TVs more than 50 years old. Analog broadcast reception is gone in many countries, so you may need converters or input modifications. Condition and prices vary a lot.

Next step

Start with the product photos in the Core77 article to see if the shape is for you. If you are ready to order, send the seller one email first: ask about ports, power, shipping origin and returns. Order only after you have those answers in writing.
