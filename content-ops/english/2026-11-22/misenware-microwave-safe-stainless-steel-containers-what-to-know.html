For decades the rule has been simple: no metal in the microwave. So when the American cookware brand Misen started selling stainless steel food storage containers that it says can go straight into the microwave, plenty of people did a double take. This piece walks through what Misen actually claims, the conditions it attaches, what the sets cost, and why the containers already in your cupboard still do not belong in the microwave.

What Misenware is

Misenware is a line of food storage containers made by Misen, a brand known mainly for its knives and pans. The container bodies are 304 stainless steel, a common food-grade alloy. The lids are tempered glass with a silicone rim and a small steam vent. There are three sizes, and all three share the same opening, so one lid fits every container.

Misen lists the capacities as roughly 700 mL for the small, 1,100 mL for the medium and 1,500 mL for the large. Those are the manufacturer's figures; we did not find an independent measurement.

The line first appeared on Kickstarter in June 2026. Two crowdfunding trackers, Kicktraq and TCF, both put the campaign above 18,000 backers and above $2.6 million raised against a $25,000 goal. Their exact totals differ slightly, so the final figure is unconfirmed. The campaign closed in late July, and the containers are now sold as sets on Misen's own store.

Why Misen says its steel is microwave-safe

Misen does not credit a special coating or an exotic alloy. Its explanation is about shape and thickness. According to the company, the containers use thick 304 stainless steel with rounded corners, a design meant to reflect microwave energy safely rather than let it concentrate in one spot.

That lines up with the basic physics of why metal sparks. Microwaves push electrons around on a metal surface, and charge crowds into points and sharp edges. Where the charge bunches up, the electric field gets strong enough to break down the surrounding air, and you see an arc. Live Science and NPR have both described the classic example: a flat sheet of foil may sit quietly, while the same foil crumpled into a ball starts sparking almost at once. A fork, with its tines, is far more likely to arc than a smooth spoon. Remove the sharp features and you remove the most likely arcing points. That is the idea behind Misen's design.

One detail does not quite add up. Misen's own pages describe the body thickness two different ways, as 1.0 mm in one place and 0.8 mm in another. We could not resolve which is correct, so treat the exact thickness as unconfirmed.

The conditions Misen attaches

This is the part that matters most. Misen does not say "put it in any microwave for as long as you like." Its product pages and quickstart guide set out specific limits:

  • Power and time: up to 5 minutes at a time in an 1100W microwave, or up to 10 minutes at a time at 1000W.
  • Ovens to avoid: do not use it in 1200W microwaves or in 915 MHz microwaves.
  • One at a time: microwave only one container per run.
  • Never empty: running an empty container can damage the microwave, Misen says.
  • No contact: keep the container away from the oven's walls, any metal rack and any metal turntable support.
  • Nothing else metal: no cutlery or foil in the oven with it.
  • Vent open: leave the lid's steam vent open while heating.

Misen says it lab-tested the containers up to 1100W. It has done only limited testing at 1200W and chose to set 1100W as the ceiling. Read the list the other way round and it becomes a set of warnings: step outside these conditions and the company itself is not promising you will avoid sparks.

Misen also points out that the vent is a simple opening, not a pressure valve, and that the containers are not fully airtight, so they are a poor fit for keeping dry goods for long periods. With hot food inside and the vent closed, a vacuum can form under the glass lid, making it hard to open and putting stress on the glass.

A few independent review sites, including Prudent Reviews and On Better Living, report heating food in Misenware under the stated conditions without sparking. Those are individual hands-on reviews, not certified laboratory tests. We did not find any public record of third-party safety certification for microwave use, so that point is unconfirmed.

Why ordinary metal containers are still off-limits

It would be easy to read the Misenware story as "metal is fine in the microwave now." It is not, and every safety authority we checked says the same.

The Environment, Health and Safety office at the University of California, Berkeley tells staff not to put aluminum foil, metal containers, metal utensils, metal objects or anything with metal or foil trim in a microwave oven. Its reasoning is direct: these items can arc, damage the oven and create a fire or burn hazard.

In South Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety has long warned against metal containers and aluminum foil in the microwave, noting that microwaves concentrate on sharp metal edges and cause sparks. Samsung Electronics Service and LG Electronics give the same advice in their Korean support pages, and both specifically flag dishes with gold or silver trim as a sparking risk.

Misen's conditions are a claim about one product that the company designed, measured and tested itself. They tell you nothing about a stainless steel lunch box, travel mug or saucepan from another maker. Even something that looks smooth and rounded may have a rolled rim, a welded seam or a handle joint that becomes a hot spot. If your microwave's own manual forbids metal containers, follow the manual.

Checks before you heat anything

Before you press start

  • Look for a microwave-safe mark on both the body and the lid.
  • Remove foil lids, wire twist-ties, staples and metal handles from takeout packaging.
  • Take the spoon out of the soup bowl.
  • Wipe food residue off the oven walls; Samsung notes that baked-on residue can spark too.
  • Find your microwave's wattage on the label inside the door or in the manual.

If you see sparks or flames

  • Stop the microwave immediately.
  • If something has caught fire, keep the door shut. Fire and Rescue NSW and other fire services advise turning the oven off, unplugging it if you can reach the plug safely, and letting the fire smother.
  • If the fire does not go out or starts to spread, leave the room and call the emergency services.
  • If the oven sparks with nothing metal inside, stop using it and have it inspected.

Should you buy Misenware?

It depends on how you cook. If you like storing leftovers in steel and want to skip transferring food into a separate dish to reheat, Misenware is built for exactly that, provided your microwave sits at or below 1100W and you are comfortable following the company's rules every time. If your microwave is more powerful, or you need airtight storage for liquids on the go, a microwave-safe glass container may suit you better.

Whatever you decide, start with what you already own. Check your microwave's wattage today, and move any ordinary stainless steel containers and gold-rimmed plates to a shelf away from the microwave so nobody reaches for them by mistake.
