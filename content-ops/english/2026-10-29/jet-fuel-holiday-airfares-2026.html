If you have not booked your Thanksgiving or Christmas flight yet, you are about to pay more for it than in any holiday season in the past decade.

That is the message from the travel app Hopper, whose 2026 holiday travel index puts the average US domestic round trip at about $402 for Thanksgiving and about $452 for Christmas. Both are the highest holiday fares the company has tracked in roughly ten years. The main reason is not mysterious. Jet fuel in the United States costs about twice what it did a year ago. Here is what the numbers say, where they come from, and what a traveler can still do about it.

Put those fares in household terms. A family of four flying round trip at the Thanksgiving average spends about $1,608 on tickets alone. At the Christmas average the same trip costs about $1,808. Bags, seats, rental cars and hotels come on top.

Why jet fuel is the headline cause

Fuel is usually one of the two largest costs for an airline, next to labor. When it doubles, carriers have only a few levers. They can raise fares, cut capacity, add fees, or absorb the hit and post weaker profits. In 2026 they have pulled most of those levers at once.

The fuel shock traces back to the war involving Iran earlier this year and the near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz. NPR reported in April that jet fuel prices had roughly doubled from prewar levels as Middle Eastern supply struggled to reach the market. Industry coverage of US Energy Information Administration (EIA) data likewise described jet fuel prices doubling in March, and noted that US refiners raised jet fuel output in response.

On October 23, NPR ran a story on fuel and holiday fares that changed during the day. The first version said the Argus US Jet Fuel Index had topped $4.75 a gallon that week, close to an all-time high. A later version said the index averaged $4.90 a gallon on Thursday, the highest single-day average Argus has recorded. Both versions agree on one point: fuel costs roughly twice what it did a year ago. Hopper economist Hayley Berg told NPR that fares are at a ten-year high because of the high cost of jet fuel.

Is it really a record?

That depends on the yardstick. The $4.90 record comes from one outlet citing one price index. An analysis based on EIA Gulf Coast spot prices found prices more than double a year earlier but still below the April 2022 peak. So "about double last year" is solid, while "all-time record" should be read as unconfirmed.

Less cheap capacity, too

Fuel is not the only factor. Spirit Airlines, the largest US ultra-low-cost carrier, stopped flying on May 2, 2026. NPR member stations and Fox reported that the airline wound down immediately after talks on federal support failed, canceling all flights. Spirit had filed for bankruptcy twice since 2024, and its planned merger with JetBlue had been blocked in court.

When a low-fare carrier leaves, the cheapest seats on many routes go with it. CNN coverage cited by Gray TV stations noted that experts point to fewer low-cost options alongside fuel. Rival airlines have also trimmed less profitable routes and raised bag fees and other charges, which lifts the total cost of a trip even when the base fare looks familiar.

What the inflation data shows

The September consumer price index, released October 14, showed airline fares up 22.4% from a year earlier, according to Travel Extra and Travel And Tour World. Headline inflation for the same month was 3.7%. In other words, flying got more expensive about six times faster than prices in general. Gasoline was up sharply too, which points to the same energy shock.

One caution. A separate article put the airfare increase at 23.4%, citing CNBC. The 22.4% figure appears in more sources, so it is used here, but the exact number should be checked against the Bureau of Labor Statistics release.

Will prices come down closer to the holidays?

Hopper does not expect them to. Its advice, as reported by Fox stations and The Points Party, is to book flights and hotels before Halloween, because it expects prices to climb as the holidays approach. The one exception it flagged was big-city hotels, which can drop in the last day or two before a holiday.

Berg also told NPR that holiday travelers rarely change their plans much, even when prices jump. Demand that does not bend is exactly why airlines can pass fuel costs through during these weeks.

Practical ways to soften the hit

  • Price the whole trip, not the fare. Add checked bags, seat selection and airport parking before comparing two itineraries. A lower base fare can end up costing more.
  • Be flexible by a day. Hopper says the Monday or Tuesday before Christmas tends to be cheaper. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is one of the most expensive days to fly home.
  • Check nearby airports. A second airport within driving distance can change the price, especially on routes Spirit used to serve.
  • Look at refund terms. If you buy early to avoid rising fares, a ticket that allows changes for a credit limits the damage if your plans shift.
  • Set fare alerts now. Alerts cost nothing and show you whether the price you see today is a high or a dip.
  • Use points where they stretch. When cash fares are high, award prices on some routes do not rise as fast. Compare before you pay cash.

What is still unconfirmed

  • The claim that US jet fuel hit an all-time high ($4.90 a gallon, Argus) rests on a single NPR report. Other measures put prices below the 2022 peak.
  • The exact September airfare CPI figure (22.4% versus 23.4%) has not been checked against the BLS release itself.
  • No one knows when shipping through the Strait of Hormuz will fully normalize, or how quickly fuel prices would ease afterward.

Your next step: if you still need a Thanksgiving or Christmas flight, search your route today, set a fare alert on the two best options, and book the one with acceptable change terms before Halloween instead of waiting for a drop that Hopper does not expect.
