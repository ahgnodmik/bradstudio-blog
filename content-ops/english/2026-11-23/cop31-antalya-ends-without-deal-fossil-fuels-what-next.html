The UN climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, was supposed to wrap up on Friday 20 November 2026. It ran into the weekend instead, and on Sunday 22 November it ended with no overarching agreement at all. For the first time since the Copenhagen summit in 2009, a COP closed without adopting its main political text. This piece explains what actually failed, which countries stood where on fossil fuels, why a handful of governments can sink a deal backed by most of the room, what the money fight over adaptation was about, and where South Korea fits in.

What failed in Antalya: the cover decision

Every COP produces two kinds of output. There are dozens of technical decisions on specific agenda items, such as transparency reporting, adaptation indicators or the just transition. Then there is usually a "cover decision," a single political text that sums up what the conference wants to say to the world. It is the document headlines are written about.

At COP31, it was the cover decision that died. At the closing plenary, COP31 President Murat Kurum, Türkiye's Minister of Environment, Urbanization and Climate Change, told delegates the text had not been agreed, according to CBS News, CBC and the Korean daily Kyunghyang Shinmun. Australian broadcaster ABC and CBC both described the meeting as the longest UN climate summit on record.

Some separate agenda-item decisions were reported as adopted alongside the failed cover text. Reports differ on exactly which ones, so the definitive list will only be clear once the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) publishes the official decisions. Until then, treat any claim about a specific item being adopted as unconfirmed.

The fossil fuel fight: who stood where

The breaking point was one phrase: "transition away from fossil fuels." That language was agreed at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, and many countries wanted Antalya to build on it. An early presidency draft did point in that direction. Later drafts dropped the reference, according to Al Jazeera and Argus Media.

Here is how the camps lined up, using only countries named in at least two independent reports:

  • For keeping fossil fuel language: the European Union, whose negotiating team was led by Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra, said it could not back a cover decision that left fossil fuels out (Al Jazeera, NPR). Panama formally objected to the final draft (CBC, Al Jazeera). NPR and CNBC also reported opposition from the United Kingdom and climate-vulnerable island states including the Marshall Islands and Tuvalu.
  • Against including it: NPR, CNN and the Korean outlets News1 and iNews24 reported that Saudi Arabia and Russia opposed fossil fuel transition wording in the final text.
  • Trying to broker it: the Turkish presidency under Kurum and Australia's Chris Bowen, Minister for Climate Change and Energy, who served as President of Negotiations under an unusual split arrangement between the two countries.

The EU has said that roughly 130 countries supported explicit fossil fuel language. That number comes from the EU side and has not been independently verified, so we treat it as unconfirmed.

Why a few countries can block everyone

This is the part that confuses most readers. If a large majority wanted the fossil fuel language, why did it not simply pass?

The answer goes back to the 1990s. The UNFCCC never formally adopted the voting rule in its rules of procedure, because countries could not agree on it. In practice, every COP decision is taken by consensus. Consensus does not mean every country cheers. It means no country lodges a formal objection. One determined objection can stop a text.

That cuts both ways. In Antalya, oil producers could block a text with fossil fuel language, and the EU and its allies could block a text without it. With red lines on both sides, no version could pass. Defenders of consensus argue that climate action requires deep changes to national energy systems, and countries are more likely to implement what they agreed to themselves. Critics say it pushes every text toward the lowest common denominator. Antalya is now Exhibit A in that debate.

Does the collapse cancel earlier commitments?

No. The Paris Agreement is a separate treaty and remains in force. Each country's nationally determined contribution (NDC), its national emissions target under Paris, still stands. The COP28 language on transitioning away from fossil fuels has not been erased either. CBC and Korea's Herald Business both made this point. What Antalya failed to do was reaffirm and strengthen that language.

The money question: tripling adaptation finance

Adaptation finance pays for poorer countries to protect themselves against floods, droughts, heatwaves and sea-level rise. At COP30 in Belém, Brazil, in 2025, countries agreed to triple adaptation finance by 2035. The problem is that the target was set without a firm baseline.

Tripling only means something once you know the starting number. A low base year makes the target cheap. A higher one makes it expensive. Carbon Pulse and the think tank ODI both reported that countries in Antalya were still split on how to count the tripling. The G77 and China pushed for formal work on a baseline and tracking method. The EU and some other developed-country groups preferred to handle it in the broader finance negotiations. The baseline remains undecided.

What comes next: COP32 in Addis Ababa

The next summit, COP32, is set for 2027 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, with the African Group holding the presidency. Ethiopia's hosting bid was widely reported in November 2025. We could not confirm exact dates in two independent sources, so check the UNFCCC calendar before planning around it.

Both unresolved fights move there. An African presidency is likely to keep adaptation finance near the top of the agenda. Whether fossil fuel language returns, and whether anyone can bridge the gap between oil producers and the EU-led bloc, is open. The consensus rule will not change.

Where South Korea fits in

South Korea's delegation was led by Kim Sung-whan, Minister of Climate, Energy and Environment. On 17 November, Kim and Australia's Chris Bowen signed a memorandum of understanding on joint decarbonisation pathways, according to Electronic Times (in Korean), the Korean government's policy briefing service (in Korean) and Australia's Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water. It covers six areas:

  • Electrification and grid expansion
  • Technology cooperation and capacity building
  • Investment conditions for clean energy and industrial decarbonisation
  • Climate adaptation and resilience
  • Support for implementing NDCs
  • Civil society and youth participation

An MOU sets direction rather than money. No specific projects or funding amounts had been announced as of 23 November.

Korea's own target is unaffected by the Antalya result. In November 2025, the government confirmed its 2035 NDC as a cut of 53 to 61 percent in net emissions from 2018 levels, after approval by the Presidential Commission on Carbon Neutrality and Green Growth and the Cabinet (Newsis and YTN, both in Korean). That is a 13 to 21 percentage point step up from the previous 2030 target of 40 percent. Some environmental groups have argued the 53 percent floor is not consistent with a 1.5C pathway. We could not confirm, in two independent sources, what position Korea took on the final draft in the closing plenary, so that remains unconfirmed.

How to follow this yourself

  • Check which document failed. When a headline says "talks collapse," ask whether it means the cover decision or a specific agenda item.
  • Look for named countries. Under consensus, a single named objector matters more than a vague "some nations."
  • Ask for the baseline. Any "double" or "triple" finance pledge is only meaningful with a base year and a base amount.
  • Watch the UNFCCC decisions page. The official COP31 decision list will settle which separate decisions passed.
  • Track national follow-through. For Korea, the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment's press releases will show whether the Australia MOU turns into concrete projects.
