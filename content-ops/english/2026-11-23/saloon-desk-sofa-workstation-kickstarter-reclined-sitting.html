Plenty of people who work from home end up on the couch by mid-afternoon, laptop on their knees and neck bent forward. A German designer thinks the couch is not the problem; the missing desk is. His Saloon Desk is a steel frame that stands over a sofa or armchair and carries a full workstation, monitors included. The Kickstarter campaign has passed its goal, early-bird pledges start at €499, and first deliveries are planned for February 2027. This piece covers what has been confirmed about the product, what is still missing, and how far the "reclining is better for your back" pitch actually holds up.

What the Saloon Desk is

The Saloon Desk comes from Paul Weatherstone, a product designer based in Kiel, Germany. It was covered in November by the industrial design site Core77 and by New Atlas. Both describe the same basic layout: heavy steel feet sit on the floor on either side of your existing seat, a frame bridges over it, and two work surfaces reach in from the left and right until they meet in front of you.

To get up, you push the two halves outward, like the swinging doors of a Wild West saloon. That is where the name comes from. Nothing bolts to the sofa, so in principle the frame can be moved to a different chair.

New Atlas reports that each work surface can be adjusted for height and angle on its own. Monitors attach through standard VESA mounts. The maker says the frame can hold two monitors or one large screen alongside a laptop; treat that as a manufacturer claim until independent reviews exist. Core77 and New Atlas both say Weatherstone went through 27 prototypes over about three and a half years before launching.

The back-health claim, and where it comes from

According to Core77, Weatherstone started the project after reading research suggesting that leaning back is easier on the spine than sitting upright in an office chair. That is his account of his motivation. It is not a finding about his product, and nobody has published a study of people using a Saloon Desk.

The study most often cited for this idea was presented at the 2006 annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA) by a team led by Waseem Bashir. According to the RSNA abstract and coverage in AuntMinnie, Diagnostic Imaging and ScienceDaily, the team scanned 22 adults with no history of back pain or surgery in an upright MRI machine. They compared a slouched posture, a 90-degree upright posture and a 135-degree reclined posture. The abstract concluded that the 135-degree trunk-to-thigh angle placed the least strain on the lumbar spine.

Whether this is the exact research Weatherstone read is unconfirmed. Either way, the study has clear limits:

  • It involved 22 healthy volunteers, a small group with no back problems.
  • It captured short scans in each position, not months of desk work.
  • It measured disc height and movement on MRI, not pain, injury or productivity.
  • It did not test a sofa, an armchair or any workstation product.

So "reclining reduced measured disc strain in a small imaging study" is a fair summary. "Working reclined will protect your back" goes further than the evidence. If you already have back pain, a clinician is a better starting point than new furniture.

What workplace safety agencies actually say

OSHA

The US Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) publishes a computer workstation guide that lists reclined sitting as one of several neutral postures, alongside upright sitting and standing. In OSHA's description, a reclined posture keeps the torso and neck straight with an angle of roughly 105 to 120 degrees between thighs and torso. That is noticeably more upright than the 135 degrees in the RSNA study. OSHA also says the back should be fully supported, with lumbar support, whether you sit upright or lean back slightly.

On screens, OSHA places the monitor directly in front of you, with the top line of text at or below eye level, at a distance of roughly 20 to 40 inches (50 to 100 cm). Its guide also notes that staying in one posture or sitting still for long periods is not healthy, and it suggests getting up and walking for a few minutes from time to time.

CCOHS

The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS) is even less prescriptive. It says there is no single body position recommended for sitting. A good position, in its words, is one that lets you change posture frequently and naturally within an acceptable range. CCOHS also warns that shifting position alone may not offset the effects of sitting for long stretches.

For monitors, CCOHS advises keeping the top of the screen at or below eye level, with the line of sight angled 15 to 30 degrees below horizontal. Its diagram shows a viewing distance of about 40 to 74 cm.

Put the two agencies side by side and the message is consistent. Leaning back is acceptable within limits. Support for the back matters. The bigger point is not to stay in any one position for too long.

Why leaning back makes desk setup harder

There is a practical reason a reclined posture is hard to get right on an ordinary couch. When you lean back, your eyes and hands move back with you. A laptop left on your knees or a coffee table stays where it is. You end up pushing your head forward to read the screen or stretching your arms to type, which undoes much of what leaning back was supposed to achieve.

That is the problem the Saloon Desk is designed around. Bringing the screen and keyboard toward a reclined body is the whole point of the swing-out surfaces and monitor arms. Whether the product does it well is something only hands-on reviews can show, and none were available at the time of writing.

Before you back it, or before you rearrange your couch

  • Measure your seat. Note the width of the sofa or chair, the seat height and the armrest height. The frame has to straddle it, and official dimensions were not published in the sources we found.
  • Plan for weight. At 70 to 75 lb, this is not something you move around casually.
  • Check delivery terms. Confirm your country is on the shipping list and budget for shipping and import charges.
  • Try cheap fixes first. A cushion behind the lower back, a footrest if your feet do not reach the floor, a laptop stand plus a separate keyboard, and a timer to remind you to get up can cover a lot of the same ground.
  • Judge it as a workspace. If it solves a real space problem in your home, it may be worth a look. If the main reason is back health, the evidence does not yet support that.

The simplest next step costs nothing. Tomorrow, work for an hour slightly reclined with your screen raised and pulled closer, then take a photo of yourself from the side. If your head is jutting forward, adjust the screen before you think about buying anything.
