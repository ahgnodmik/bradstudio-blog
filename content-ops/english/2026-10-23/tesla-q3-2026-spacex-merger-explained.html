If you read two headlines about Tesla this week, one said it beat estimates and the other said profit collapsed, and both were right.

Tesla reported third-quarter 2026 results after the US market closed on October 21. The day before, Elon Musk said on SpaceX's own earnings call that a Tesla–SpaceX merger would be "a good idea." The two events landed back to back. Many readers mixed them together. This guide pulls them apart. It covers what the numbers say, what a merger would actually need, and why the story matters in South Korea. It is not investment advice.

The numbers: two true headlines

Start with the figure accountants use. Tesla's GAAP net income was $787 million, or 22 cents a share. A year earlier it was about $1.37 billion, or 39 cents a share. That is a drop of roughly 43%.

Now the figure Wall Street watches. Adjusted earnings per share came in at 48 cents. That strips out items such as stock-based compensation and some investment gains and losses. Analysts polled by LSEG expected 43 cents. Zacks and FactSet had 45 cents. So Tesla beat the forecast. It still earned less than the 50 cents of a year ago.

Revenue was flat at about $28.05 billion. The same quarter last year brought in roughly $28.1 billion. Against the LSEG estimate of $27.9 billion, revenue was a small beat. Against Tesla's own compiled consensus, it was a small miss. Your verdict depends on which yardstick you pick.

The weakest line was operating income. It fell 56% to $718 million. That is an operating margin of 2.6%. The reason is spending. Operating expenses rose 39% as Tesla poured money into artificial intelligence, robots and new products.

Some lines grew:

  • Services and other revenue rose 33% to $4.6 billion.
  • Free cash flow was positive at $1.53 billion.
  • Tesla said it started production of its Optimus humanoid robot at its Fremont, California factory.

Some lines shrank:

  • Energy generation and storage revenue fell 16% to $2.8 billion.
  • Capital spending jumped about 110% year over year to $4.73 billion in the quarter.

One more detail matters for the cash story. Tesla's quarterly filing (Form 10-Q) now guides 2026 capital spending at "in excess of $20 billion." Earlier this year it said more than $25 billion. The change appeared in the 10-Q, not in the shareholder letter. Some analysts read it as a real pullback. Others see a timing shift. Shares slipped about 3% in after-hours trading.

Deliveries beat, storage missed

Tesla published delivery numbers on October 2. It delivered 486,532 vehicles in the quarter. The company-compiled analyst consensus was 461,974. That is a beat of about 5%.

Year over year, deliveries fell about 2.1%. The comparison is tough. In Q3 2025, US buyers rushed to purchase before the $7,500 federal EV tax credit expired at the end of September.

Energy storage deployments reached 13.7 GWh. That missed the company-compiled consensus of 15.9 GWh by about 14%. It also sat below the record of 14.2 GWh set in Q4 2025.

The pattern is clear. Cars held up better than feared. Storage cooled. Profit shrank because spending on future bets rose much faster than sales.

What Musk actually said about SpaceX

The merger comments did not come from Tesla's call. They came on October 20, during the first earnings call of SpaceX as a public company.

Musk made four points:

  • The boards of Tesla and SpaceX each formed a special committee of independent directors to study a combination.
  • The committees were formed at the request of shareholders.
  • He is recused from those discussions because he is a conflicted party. He runs both companies.
  • He thinks a merger would be "a good idea," but treating it as certain "would be overstating it."

CNBC, NBC News and others reported that the committees have not agreed on any terms. There is no guarantee they will.

On Tesla's own call the next day, Musk mentioned the topic in his opening remarks. He added nothing new. Tesla took no merger questions. Analysts focused on Optimus timing and spending.

SpaceX shares rose more than 8% after its results and the merger comments. Reported estimates put the combined value at roughly $3.5 trillion. Treat that figure as a rough estimate. It would still be the largest corporate combination on record.

What a merger would require

This is where the story gets concrete. A deal has several gates.

Gate 1: the special committees

Each committee must negotiate terms. That means an exchange ratio, a deal structure, and whether Tesla holders get a premium. None of this exists yet. The committee members have not been named publicly.

Gate 2: Tesla shareholders

Musk said a deal would need approval from 50% of all Tesla shares outstanding. Not 50% of the shares that vote. All of them. He called that a high bar.

The difference is big. Under this rule, a share that does not vote works like a "no" vote. Turnout becomes as important as opinion. Fortune argued that the biggest obstacle to the deal is not Musk but Tesla's shareholders.

Earlier reports said a share-swap structure might need a two-thirds vote. That figure is unconfirmed. The real threshold will only be clear once a deal is filed.

Gate 3: SpaceX shareholders

This gate looks easier. Musk owns roughly a fifth of Tesla. At SpaceX, his super-voting shares give him more than 80% of the voting power. Approval on the SpaceX side is largely in his hands. On the Tesla side it is not.

Gate 4: a separate meeting

Tesla's annual meeting is set for December 3, 2026. Its proxy, filed October 19, contains no merger proposal. Any vote would need a separate meeting later. A report that the companies want to announce a deal in the first half of 2027 is unconfirmed.

Critics have raised a governance point too. Musk recused himself from the committee talks. He then endorsed the deal in public before any terms existed. Some commentators say that puts pressure on the committees. Expect that argument to come back if a proposal arrives.

Why South Koreans are watching

Tesla has been the most-held foreign stock among South Korean individual investors for years. Data from the Korea Securities Depository (Hanguk Yetak Gyeoljewon) has repeatedly put it at the top. Nvidia briefly overtook it in mid-2024. Tesla regained first place weeks later. In August 2025, Bloomberg's analysis of depository data found Korean individuals still held about $21.9 billion of Tesla shares. That was far ahead of any other US stock.

Korean retail investors who buy US shares are often called "Seohak Gaemi" (Western ants). For them, the merger question is practical. If a proposal reaches a vote, their shares count toward the total. Under the 50%-of-all-shares rule Musk described, an unvoted share counts against the deal. Korean holders usually vote foreign shares through their brokerage and the depository. The process takes time and has deadlines.

More recent holding figures for 2026 exist, but each came from a single report. So this article does not cite a current dollar total.

Your next step

If you own Tesla shares, check your brokerage now for how it handles proxy votes on US stocks. Then watch for two documents: any merger agreement filed with the SEC, and a notice of a special shareholder meeting. Those filings, not headlines, will set the real terms and the real vote threshold.
