If you deliver food in Korea on a motorbike, or you are thinking of picking up delivery shifts this winter, 3 December 2026 is a date to circle. From that Thursday, anyone who wants to start working as a delivery rider must first complete a traffic safety course, and riders who were already on the road get no more grace period for commercial insurance. Platforms such as Baemin and Coupang Eats have to check both before they hand out work. This explainer covers what the rules say, who they apply to, what is still undecided, and what to do before the deadline.

The legal basis is the Act on the Development of Living Logistics Services Industry. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) has rolled the rules out in two stages, June and December.

Why Korea is doing this

Motorbike delivery exploded in Korea during the pandemic and never really shrank. Many riders work part-time, switch between apps, and ride bikes insured under ordinary private policies. A private policy assumes commuting and personal errands. Riding for pay is a different risk, and a rider who crashes while on a paid job with only private cover may find the claim limited. That gap leaves both riders and the people they hit exposed.

Rather than trying to police every rider on the street, the law puts the check at the point where every rider has to pass: the contract with a platform or a local delivery agency. No verified insurance, no contract. From December, no completed training, no contract for newcomers either.

Rule one: commercial insurance for every rider

What counts as valid cover

The enforcement rules set a floor. A qualifying policy must pay unlimited compensation for bodily injury to victims and up to 20 million won for property damage. Policies from commercial insurers qualify, as do mutual aid products from the Delivery Service Mutual Aid Association, which MOLIT said were on average 16% to 32% cheaper than comparable insurer products when they launched.

What happens on 3 December

New riders have needed this cover since 3 June. Riders who were already working at that point were given six months to switch, and that window closes on 3 December. From then on, a delivery business cannot sign or keep a contract with a rider who lacks qualifying cover, according to the government's policy briefing. Platforms confirm cover through an information system lookup or by asking for documents, and they have to recheck it periodically while the policy runs.

Rule two: safety training before your first delivery

From 3 December, a person who wants to become a delivery worker must complete traffic safety training run by MOLIT or a provincial or metropolitan government. Recent reports put the course at three hours. Earlier coverage of the draft rules described it as four hours or more, so treat the exact length as unconfirmed until the final notice is out. Reported topics include how two-wheelers handle, traffic law and crash prevention.

Based on the government's description and the revision notes in the national law database, the training duty applies to people entering the job, not to riders already working. We found no official statement that existing riders must also take the course.

There is a practical catch. A report on 19 November said that with about two weeks to go, the way the training will be delivered and which institutions will run it were still open questions. Some platforms already run their own rider safety courses, but whether those will count as the legal requirement is unconfirmed.

The money side: discounts for safer riders

Since 21 September, the Delivery Service Mutual Aid Association has offered larger discounts on its 30-day commercial policies. Completing traffic safety training, installing a digital tachograph (a trip recorder known locally as DTG), and fitting a front license plate each earn 5%. Before the change, training and DTG earned 3% each and the front plate 1.5%. Combined, the maximum discount is 11%. The rates apply to new and renewed contracts, and existing policyholders can add the rider to qualify.

Fines for platforms

The law obliges certified delivery platforms and their local agencies to check insurance and training, and it provides for fines when they do not. Reports on the draft decree mentioned fines of 3 million to 5 million won depending on the number of violations. The final amounts in the enforcement decree are unconfirmed in the sources we checked.

What to do now

If you already ride for a platform

  • Open your policy document and look for wording that says it covers paid transport or delivery.
  • Check that bodily injury cover is unlimited and property damage cover is at least 20 million won.
  • Make sure the plate number on the policy matches the bike you ride today.
  • If anything is missing, arrange new cover by 2 December so there is no gap.
  • Update your insurance details in every delivery app you use, not just your main one.

If you plan to start in December

  • Get insurance quotes from at least two providers, including the mutual aid association.
  • Watch MOLIT and your city or provincial government for the training sign-up notice.
  • Save your training completion record and policy as PDFs; platforms will ask for both.
  • Book training early, because the first week of December is likely to be busy.

If you are a foreign resident

The rules do not carve out exceptions by nationality, so the same insurance and training requirements apply. Whether a given visa allows delivery work at all is a separate question for the immigration office, and it is worth checking before you sign any rider contract.

What is still undecided

  • How and where to apply for the training course
  • Whether the course is three hours or longer in the final rules
  • Whether platform-run courses will count
  • The final fine amounts for platforms that skip checks

The single most useful thing to do today takes five minutes: find your insurance certificate, confirm it covers paid delivery with the required limits, and fix it before 3 December if it does not.
