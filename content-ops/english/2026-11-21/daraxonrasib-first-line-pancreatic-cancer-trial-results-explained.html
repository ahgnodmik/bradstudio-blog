Headlines this week said a pill made pancreatic tumours disappear. The real story is more careful than that, and still worth understanding. Revolution Medicines released updated early-stage results for daraxonrasib, its oral RAS inhibitor, given alongside standard chemotherapy to people starting treatment for metastatic pancreatic cancer. This piece walks through what the numbers mean, how they relate to the drug's US approval in August, what the trial cannot tell us yet, and what patients and families can sensibly do next.

This article is general information, not medical advice. Anyone with pancreatic cancer should discuss treatment decisions, including clinical trials, with their own oncologist.

What was announced about daraxonrasib in first-line pancreatic cancer

On 19 November 2026, Revolution Medicines published updated data from RMC-GI-102, an open-label phase 1/2 study, timed to a presentation at the ENA 2026 cancer symposium. The data were covered by trade outlets including OncLive and Targeted Oncology, and then picked up by general news media.

The cohort in focus included 61 people with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma who had not yet received treatment for their metastatic disease. Each took daraxonrasib at 200 mg once a day together with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, a standard chemotherapy pairing often shortened to GnP. The data cutoff was 28 September 2026, and median follow-up was 18.3 months.

The "one in twelve tumours disappeared" phrasing that appeared in some coverage is a translation of that 8.2% complete response figure. It describes five people, not a general outcome.

Why the results are promising but not proof

The most important detail is the study design. Everyone in this cohort received the same combination. There was no group in the same study receiving chemotherapy alone. Without that comparison, nobody can say from this trial how much of the benefit comes from adding daraxonrasib, and how much reflects who was enrolled, where they were treated, or how closely they were followed.

Comparing these figures with older chemotherapy trials is an indirect comparison. Commentators quoted in coverage of the results made this point, and it is standard caution for any single-arm study. Sixty-one patients is also a small group. The numbers have already moved once: an earlier readout from the same program, based on 40 treated patients, reported a 58% response rate. Figures can shift again as more patients are followed for longer.

A complete response is a meaningful result, but it is not the same as a cure. It means tumours could not be seen on scans at the time. Small amounts of cancer can remain below the level imaging can detect, which is why people in complete response continue to be monitored. How long these responses last is still being tracked.

How daraxonrasib works

RAS proteins act like switches that tell cells to grow. In healthy cells the switch flicks on briefly and then off again. Mutations in RAS genes, especially KRAS, can jam the switch in the "on" position, and such mutations are found in the large majority of pancreatic adenocarcinomas.

For decades RAS was considered almost impossible to drug because its surface offers few places for a medicine to bind. Some newer drugs target one specific mutation. Daraxonrasib takes a broader approach. It is described as a RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor, designed to block several forms of RAS while they are in their active state. That breadth matters in pancreatic cancer, where the exact mutation varies from patient to patient.

What the FDA approved in August, and what it did not

On 26 August 2026, the US Food and Drug Administration approved daraxonrasib under the brand name Rasonque. The approval covers adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma whose disease progressed after at least one prior systemic therapy. The recommended dose is 300 mg once daily, taken on its own rather than with chemotherapy.

That decision rested on RASolute 302, a randomized phase 3 trial in previously treated patients. Median overall survival was 13.2 months with daraxonrasib compared with 6.7 months with chemotherapy chosen by the treating doctor, with a hazard ratio of 0.40. Results were presented at the ASCO annual meeting in 2026 and published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

The first-line combination discussed this week is not part of that approval. In September 2026 the FDA granted it Breakthrough Therapy designation, which is a route to faster review, not a green light for use. The confirmatory study is RASolute 303, a randomized phase 3 trial comparing three arms: daraxonrasib alone, daraxonrasib plus GnP, and GnP alone. No date for its results has been announced.

Side effects reported so far

In RASolute 302, the most common side effects with daraxonrasib were:

  • Rash, the most frequent problem and the most common reason for lowering the dose
  • Diarrhea
  • Stomatitis, meaning painful sores or inflammation in the mouth
  • Nausea and vomiting

Serious treatment-related side effects were reported less often with daraxonrasib than with chemotherapy in that trial. Individual risk is a question only a treating team can answer, because it depends on a person's overall health and other medicines.

Where things stand outside the United States

Access varies by country. In South Korea, for example, approval by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety has not been announced, and Korean medical press reports describe the timing of approval and insurance coverage as undecided. Korean outlets Medical Times and The Korea Economic Daily (both in Korean) have reported that RASolute 303 is running at Korean hospitals, but the number of sites and whether they are still recruiting are unconfirmed. Reports of European regulatory review exist, but we could not confirm a European approval, so treat that as unconfirmed.

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the hardest cancers to treat. The American Cancer Society puts five-year relative survival in the United States at about 13%, and Korea's National Cancer Information Center reports 17.0% for people diagnosed in 2019 to 2023. That context explains the attention, and also why careful reading of each new result matters.

What patients and families can do now

  • Read headlines against the design. Check how many patients were studied, whether there was a comparison group, and which trial phase the result comes from.
  • Ask about RAS testing. Find out whether the tumour has been tested for RAS mutations and whether a copy of the report is available.
  • Look up trials before your appointment. Searching ClinicalTrials.gov for "daraxonrasib" and "pancreatic", filtered by country, shows registered studies and listed sites. Listed recruitment status can lag, so confirm with the site.
  • Bring specific questions. Whether you might qualify for RASolute 303, what happens to current treatment if you enrol, and what side effects to watch for.
  • Do not stop current treatment on the strength of a news story. Changes should be made with your oncologist.
  • Be wary of unofficial sources of the drug. Medicines bought outside regulated channels can be counterfeit or unsafe, and this article does not offer guidance on obtaining the drug that way.

The practical next step is simple: write down your diagnosis date, stage, treatments so far and any genetic test results, then take that list and the questions above to your next oncology appointment.
