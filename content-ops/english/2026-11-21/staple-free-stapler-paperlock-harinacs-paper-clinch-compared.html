If you have ever pried a bent staple out of a stack before feeding it to the shredder, a stapler that uses no metal at all sounds like a small gift. That is roughly how Yanko Design framed PaperLock on 20 November 2026, recommending it as a stocking stuffer. The idea is not new, though. Japanese stationery makers have sold staple-free staplers for well over a decade. This piece explains how these tools hold paper together, what PaperLock actually offers, how it compares with Kokuyo's Harinacs and the PLUS Paper Clinch, and when you are better off sticking with ordinary staples.

What PaperLock is, and what we could confirm

PaperLock is a desk tool that binds paper without consuming anything. Press the lever and a small blade inside the body cuts through the stack; the cut section folds back through the paper and locks the sheets to one another. Nothing metal is left behind.

Two numbers appear consistently across independent coverage. Yanko Design and Trend Hunter both list the price as $35 and the capacity as five sheets of copy paper. It is sold through the Yanko Design online shop. The November article was not its debut: Yanko Design first covered the same product on 20 August 2026 under a headline calling it a "$35 desk tool."

Some things remain unconfirmed. Neither the articles nor the shop page name the manufacturer or designer, and we could not find shipping terms for buyers outside the US. We also found no sales figures, so any claim that it is a "popular" gift has no support. One more trap for searchers: an unrelated comb-binding product from around 2009 also used the name Paperlock. It has nothing to do with this desk tool.

How a stapler with no staples holds paper together

There are two main approaches on the market, and they make very different trade-offs.

The cut-and-fold method

Most staple-free staplers, PaperLock included, punch a small tongue and a matching slit through every sheet at once. The tongue folds over and tucks into the slit, so each page is threaded onto the same tab of paper. Think of it as tying the stack together with a strip cut from the stack itself. According to Kokuyo's own corporate history, the original Harinacs from 2009 worked this way, cutting two slits and folding them back. Cult Pens and Staples Canada describe the PLUS Paper Clinch in similar terms: it cuts and folds the sheets, then tucks a flap through the cut.

The press method

Kokuyo later added Harinacs Press, which makes no hole at all. Serrated metal teeth squeeze the corner hard enough that the fibres of neighbouring sheets mesh and the crimped ridges catch on each other. The result is a clean corner with only an embossed mark. The catch is strength. Gizmodo, covering the press model in 2014, pointed out that the bond is not permanent, and a firm tug will separate the pages.

The real limits

Capacity is small. Every product here tops out at five to ten sheets of standard copy paper. Cutting through a thicker stack takes more force, and a folded tab has to be long enough to pass through every sheet. Heavier paper reduces the usable count further.

The hold is weaker than metal. A staple's legs clinch behind the stack and grip dozens of sheets. A paper joint relies on friction and a folded tab. Kokuyo itself notes that its staple-free products do not hold as firmly as a metal staple. Damp paper or heavy page-turning can loosen the join.

Removal leaves a mark. Yanko Design's August article admits that separating PaperLock-bound pages without tearing takes more care than pulling out a staple. Cut-style tools leave a slit; press-style tools leave a crease.

Where staple-free binding makes sense

The clearest win is at the shredder and the recycling bin. Yanko Design and Trend Hunter both highlight that PaperLock-bound documents can be shredded or recycled without removing anything first, and Cult Pens says the same of the PLUS model. Core77 has offered a fair counterpoint: many modern office shredders handle staples without complaint, so check your own machine's manual before counting this as a major saving.

  • Good fits: meeting handouts of a few pages, receipts, short worksheets, notes you will discard within days.
  • Poor fits: contracts, archived records, thick reports, anything carried around and flipped often.
  • Classrooms: no loose staples on the floor, but cut-style tools still contain a small blade, so try the mechanism in person before handing one to young children.
  • Shared offices: a desk-style model by the printer is easier for many hands than a compact one.

How to choose one

Start by counting. Look at what you bound last week and note how many sheets most stacks had. If almost everything is five sheets or fewer, a compact model will do. If six to ten sheets is common, look for a desk model rated for ten.

Next, decide whether a hole matters. If the document must stay pristine, a press-style tool avoids cuts, at the cost of a weaker hold. If the papers will be handled and carried, a cut-style tool holds better.

Finally, compare the total cost. These tools have no refills, so the purchase price is essentially the lifetime cost. For PaperLock, check the shop's shipping countries and fees before checkout, since those were not stated in the coverage we reviewed. Existing models from Kokuyo and PLUS are already stocked by mainstream retailers and are worth pricing alongside it.

A quick test before you commit: fold the corner of three scrap sheets and press hard with your thumb. Feeling how little that holds tells you why the cut-and-tab design exists, and helps you judge whether your paperwork needs it.
