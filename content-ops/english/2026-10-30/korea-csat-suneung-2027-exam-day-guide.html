If you live in South Korea, or are visiting in mid-November, one Thursday will feel different from every other day of the year: the morning commute starts late, police cars give students rides, and for 25 minutes in the afternoon planes stop landing.

That day is the College Scholastic Ability Test, known in Korea as the suneung. The test for 2027 university admissions takes place on Thursday, November 19, 2026. Scores reach students on Friday, December 11, 2026. Here is what the exam is, how the day runs, and which nationwide measures the government has announced for this year.

What the suneung is

The suneung is Korea's national university entrance exam. It is run by the Korea Institute for Curriculum and Evaluation (KICE) under the Ministry of Education. Universities use the scores for admissions, and many early-admission offers also require a minimum suneung grade. A single day of testing carries years of preparation, which is why the whole country adjusts around it.

The 2027 exam has extra weight. It is the last suneung under the current structure, which has used common plus elective sections in Korean and math since the 2022 exam. Admissions reforms take effect from the 2028 cycle, so this year's test-takers are the final group on the current system.

KICE reported that 551,864 people applied to sit the exam, about 2,300 fewer than last year. Korean outlets also reported that the number of repeat test-takers, students who already graduated and are trying again, is the highest in more than two decades.

How the exam day runs

Students must be seated in their assigned room by 8:10 a.m. After that, doors close. The day is split into five periods.

The English section opens with a listening test played over speakers in every exam room. That is the reason for the most famous measure of the day.

What changes across the country

The Ministry of Education presented this year's support plan at a Cabinet meeting on October 20, 2026. Korea JoongAng Daily, the government's Korea.kr portal and several Korean newspapers reported the same measures.

Later office hours

The government is asking public offices and private companies to push their start time to 10 a.m. or later on exam day. The goal is to keep roads and trains clear while students travel. This is a request for cooperation, not a legal order, so check with your employer.

Flights and military drills paused during listening

From 1:10 to 1:35 p.m., airplane and helicopter takeoffs and landings will be adjusted so aircraft noise does not reach exam rooms. Military training such as artillery fire and tank movement will also stop during that window. If you are flying in or out of Korea that afternoon, expect schedule changes, and check with your airline in advance.

More trains, fewer cars near schools

  • Subway: trains in the Seoul metropolitan area will run more often during the student commute, from 6 a.m. to 8:10 a.m.
  • Vehicle control: cars cannot enter the area within 200 meters of an exam site. Parents driving students should plan to drop them off and let them walk the rest of the way.
  • Emergency rides: police and other agencies will station vehicles along routes to help students who are running late.
  • Quiet zones: buses and trains are asked to slow down and avoid honking near exam sites, and construction sites are asked to limit noise.

Provincial and metropolitan education offices are also running safety inspections of all exam sites before the day.

What it means if you live in or visit Korea

Even if you have no student in the family, the day touches daily life. A few practical tips:

  • Commuting: if your workplace shifts hours, the 10 a.m. start helps you too. If it does not, leave early and avoid streets around high schools, which are the usual exam sites.
  • Driving: expect blocked roads near schools in the early morning, before the 8:10 a.m. entry deadline. Use navigation apps and allow extra time.
  • Flying: if your flight is scheduled near the early afternoon, confirm the departure or arrival time with your airline a day ahead.
  • Noise: if you live near a school, keep things quiet during the day, especially around the listening test. Neighbors notice.
  • Cheering: you may see younger students gathering outside schools early in the morning to cheer on seniors. Taffy and sticky rice cakes are traditional gifts, because "sticking" is a play on passing the test.

For test-takers and parents

If you or your child is sitting the exam, KICE's official guidelines are the final word. Common-sense preparation still goes a long way.

  • Check the admission ticket and exam site as soon as you receive them. Take a photo of the ticket in case it gets lost.
  • Bring a physical photo ID. Confirm which IDs KICE accepts before the day.
  • Use an analog watch. Smartwatches and electronic devices are not allowed. Check the battery.
  • Visit the site in advance and time the trip from home, including the walk from the 200-meter drop-off point.
  • Fix your wake-up time now so your body is alert by 8:40 a.m. Keep meals familiar on exam day.

What is still unconfirmed

  • Stock market and bank hours: in previous years, Korea's markets and banks opened later on exam day. We did not find this year's schedule confirmed by two sources.
  • Number of exam sites: one English-language report gave a figure, but we could not confirm it with a second source.
  • Exact flight changes: airlines will publish specific schedule adjustments closer to the day.
  • Exam difficulty: unknown until after the test. Answer keys and grade cutoffs follow later.

Put November 19 in your calendar now. If you work in Korea, ask your employer whether start times will shift. If you are flying that day, check your flight time with your airline. If you have a student in the family, do a test run to the exam site this weekend and set the alarm for exam-day timing.
