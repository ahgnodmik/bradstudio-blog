China's Tiangong space station has only ever hosted Chinese crews. That is set to change with Shenzhou-24, which is scheduled to carry Pakistani astronaut Muhammad Zeeshan Ali to the station. He would be the first foreign astronaut to visit Tiangong and the first Pakistani to reach orbit. This explainer covers who he is, what the mission plans to do, how Tiangong compares with the International Space Station, and why Korean readers may recall a similar moment in 2008 with Yi So-yeon.

Shenzhou-24 launch: scheduled for 25 November, liftoff not yet confirmed

The China Manned Space Agency (CMSA) said at a news conference on 24 November that Shenzhou-24 would launch at 8:18 a.m. Beijing time on Wednesday 25 November. That is 9:18 a.m. in Seoul and 00:18 UTC. The spacecraft will fly on a Long March-2F rocket from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in Gansu Province, and is planned to dock with Tianhe, the station's core module, about three and a half hours after launch. People's Daily, Newspim and News1 reported the same schedule.

Status at the time of writing: launch unconfirmed. We found no report confirming liftoff, orbit or docking. Crewed launches can slip for weather or technical reasons. Check CMSA or major news outlets for the result before you share this article as fact.

Who is Muhammad Zeeshan Ali?

Ali is a 40-year-old former Pakistan Air Force pilot, according to Gulf News and Korean reports on the crew announcement. On Shenzhou-24 he is the payload specialist. That role is about running science experiments, not flying the spacecraft.

CMSA spokesperson Zhang Jingbo described Ali as the first foreign astronaut to visit Tiangong and the first international crew member in China's human spaceflight programme, as reported by Chinese state media and Al Jazeera.

Dawn, the South China Morning Post, CGTN and Arab News all reported the April selection and the two names. Both candidates are military pilots. Only one could fly; reports describe the other as a backup.

What Ali will do on Tiangong, and how long he stays

Ali's visit is short. He is expected to spend about a week on the station. He will not return on Shenzhou-24. Instead he will come home on Shenzhou-23 with two of its crew already aboard, Zhang Zhiyuan and Lai Ka-ying, according to Dawn and Korean reporting. Lai is from Hong Kong. The Chinese crew of Shenzhou-24 is planned to stay much longer; mission listings show a planned duration of about 180 days. An exact return date for Ali has not been announced.

His work centres on experiments designed in Pakistan, drawn from proposals by Pakistani universities after SUPARCO invited submissions. Reports describe the work as covering biology and biotechnology in microgravity. Several outlets put the number of experiments at four; CMSA has not published a detailed list, so we treat the count as unconfirmed.

Tiangong vs the ISS: why a foreign visitor matters

Tiangong and the International Space Station (ISS) are the two crewed stations in orbit. They grew out of very different politics.

International Space StationTiangong
OperatorPartnership of the US, Russia, Europe, Japan and CanadaChina
Crew nationalitiesAstronauts from many countries over two decadesChinese only, until Shenzhou-24
China's roleExcluded since the US Wolf Amendment of 2011Owner and operator
FuturePlanned retirement around 2030Opening to foreign partners

Since 2011 a US law known as the Wolf Amendment has barred NASA from spending funds on bilateral cooperation with China. That effectively kept China off the ISS and pushed it to build its own station, according to analyses from Aerospace Corporation and Physics Today. With the ISS heading toward retirement, Tiangong may become one of the few places in orbit open to astronauts from countries outside the ISS partnership. Pakistan is the first to take up that offer.

The format of Ali's trip, a short stay by a payload specialist from a partner country, has a long history. Soviet and Russian programmes flew guest researchers from many countries to Salyut, Mir and later the ISS. One of them was from Korea.

Korea's own first astronaut: Yi So-yeon in 2008

For Korean readers, Ali's flight has a clear parallel. On 8 April 2008, bioengineer Yi So-yeon launched from Baikonur, Kazakhstan, on the Russian Soyuz TMA-12 with two Russian cosmonauts. She became the first Korean in space. The Soyuz docked with the ISS on 10 April, and she landed on 19 April aboard Soyuz TMA-11, according to Britannica, Space.com and The Korea Times.

Like Ali, Yi flew as a short-stay specialist rather than a pilot. She carried out 18 science experiments for the Korea Aerospace Research Institute (KARI). The two flights share a pattern: a country without its own crewed spacecraft buys or negotiates a seat with a spacefaring partner, sends a scientist-astronaut for a short stay, and brings home experiment results and a national first.

There is a key difference. Yi's seat came through a Russian Soyuz to a multinational station. Ali's comes through China alone, to a station only China runs. No Korean has visited Tiangong, and Korea has announced no plan to send one.

What to watch next

  • Launch and docking. Look for CMSA confirmation of liftoff on 25 November and docking with Tianhe a few hours later.
  • The handover. Watch for Shenzhou-24's crew joining Shenzhou-23 on board, and for news of the Shenzhou-23 return date, which also sets Ali's return.
  • Experiment details. Pakistan's SUPARCO and partner universities may publish the experiment list once the work starts.
  • Next foreign visitors. China has said it is open to more international astronauts. Any second country announced would show whether Pakistan's flight is a one-off or the start of a pattern.

If you follow space news from Korea, set an alert for "Shenzhou-24" and check the launch result on the morning of 25 November, Seoul time, before reading any further coverage as confirmed.
