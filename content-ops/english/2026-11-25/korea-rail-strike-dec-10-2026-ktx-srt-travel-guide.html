If you have a KTX ticket, a former SRT booking or a daily commute on a Korail-run subway line in December, the Korean Railway Workers' Union strike planned for 10 December is the one date to watch. The union says it will walk out at 9 a.m. that Thursday and stay out indefinitely unless its demands are met. Nothing is final yet. This guide explains what the Korea rail strike would mean for KTX, SRT and capital-area subway riders, how many trains Korail expects to keep running, how refunds have worked in past strikes, and which alternatives to line up now.

Korea rail strike on 10 December: what has been announced

The union announced the plan at a press conference outside Seoul Station on 11 November. It said the strike would begin at 9 a.m. on 10 December and would have no set end date. The Korea Herald, The Korea Times and several Korean dailies carried the same date and time.

The strike follows a vote of union members in early November. Of 24,691 registered members, 21,634 voted, and 16,783 of them, or 77.58%, backed industrial action, according to Korean press reports of the result.

The union's stated demands are:

  • More staff in safety-related jobs, filled without delay.
  • A promotion system the union describes as fair to frontline workers.
  • Better working conditions, including allowances and rest facilities.

Korail, officially the Korea Railroad Corporation, has said it will keep negotiating to avoid a full stoppage. As of 24 November, no deal had been reported.

The union had planned to start a two-week work-to-rule campaign on 23 November. After five cars of a 21-car freight train came off the tracks at Sojeongni Station in Sejong on 21 November, it pushed that campaign back to 26 November so crews could focus on recovery. Korail restored all services from the first train on 22 November. Herald Business, The Financial News, Pressian and Aju Business Daily all reported the delay. Under a work-to-rule campaign, staff follow every procedure to the letter, so trains can leave late or run at longer intervals even before any strike.

Why this Korail strike could hit KTX and SRT harder than before

In past strikes, SRT high-speed trains out of Suseo Station kept running because a separate company, SR Corporation, operated them. Travellers who lost a KTX seat could often switch to an SRT train.

That safety valve is gone. Korail took over SR's high-speed operations on 1 September 2026, and trains formerly sold as SRT now run under the KTX brand. The Korea Herald and The Korea Times both noted that this means a strike could affect high-speed trains from Suseo as well as Korail's other services. Korean outlets including Newspim described this as the first major strike threat since the two operators were brought together.

If you booked an "SRT" journey through Suseo or Dongtan, treat it as exposed to the strike in the same way as a KTX from Seoul Station.

KTX operating rate during the strike: what Korail expects

Korean labour law requires essential railway functions to keep running at a minimum level during a strike, so a walkout never means zero trains. The question is how many.

Korail's own projection is that it can keep about 75.2% of all trains running by using staff from the newly integrated high-speed division and other replacement workers. Green Economy reported that figure, and Newspim reported that Korail's chief executive told lawmakers the company would keep at least 75% of trains running. For comparison, Korean press reports put the operating rate during the week-long strike in December 2024 at 66.8%.

Two cautions apply:

  • It is an average. A 75% network-wide rate still means roughly one train in four is cancelled. Cuts are not spread evenly. In past strikes Korail protected rush-hour commuter services and trunk routes more than off-peak and regional trains.
  • Reports differ on the split. Newsway reported a separate projection of 56.9% for Korail's conventional operations and around 70% for the former SR services. We treat the split by service as unconfirmed until Korail publishes a timetable.

Which Seoul subway lines a Korail strike affects

Many foreign residents are surprised that a "rail" strike reaches the subway. Korail operates several commuter lines in the capital area. During the December 2024 strike, The Korea JoongAng Daily reported disruption on Lines 1, 3 and 4 and on the Gyeongchun, Gyeongui-Jungang, Suin-Bundang and Seohae lines. The Korea Times and Yonhap-based reports also named Lines 1, 3 and 4.

Seoul's Lines 1 to 8 are run by a different company, Seoul Metro. Lines 1, 3 and 4 are shared: Seoul Metro runs the central section and Korail runs the suburban sections and many through trains. Seoul Metro's own union had planned a separate strike for 25 November. Its management and unions reached a deal in the early hours of 25 November, after talks through the night, and Lines 1 to 8 ran normally from the first train, according to The Hankook Ilbo, Munhwa Ilbo and Aju Business Daily. That dispute is over; the Korail dispute is not.

KTX refund rules if your train is cancelled

Korail has not published a refund policy for this strike yet. Its practice in recent strikes has been consistent, though, and is the best guide to what to expect.

  • Cancelled trains: full refund, no fee. In December 2025, Korail said tickets for trains cancelled because of the strike would be refunded in full, automatically, with no need to apply (Newsis). The same no-penalty rule was reported in 2019, 2022 and 2024.
  • Notice by text and app. Cancelled trains are marked as suspended on the Korail Talk app and the Korail website, and ticket holders receive a text message and push notification (Newsis, December 2025).
  • Fee waivers during the notice period. In 2022 and 2024, Korail waived cancellation and change fees for all trains during the strike notice period, not only for trains that were cut (MTN and other Korean reports). Whether it will do so this time is unconfirmed.
  • Sales limits. Before the planned December 2025 strike, Korail stopped selling tickets for trains it expected to cancel. Watch for the same step this year: if a train you want disappears from sale, it may be on the cut list.

Practical point for foreign residents: make sure the phone number on your Korail account can receive Korean text messages. If you booked through a travel agency or a rail pass, ask the seller how they will pass on cancellation notices.

Will the rail strike actually happen?

Nobody can say yet. History suggests it is not a done deal either way.

  • In December 2024, the union struck for about a week, and the operating rate fell to 66.8%.
  • In December 2025, the union called off a strike set for 11 December after a provisional agreement, then announced a new strike for 23 December and paused that one too, hours before it was due to start (Newsis, SBS Biz, The Korea JoongAng Daily).

Last-minute deals are common in Korean rail disputes. They are not guaranteed. Plan as if the strike will happen, and be pleasantly surprised if it does not.

What to do this week

  • List every train you have booked between 10 December and the end of the month.
  • For trips you cannot move, book a refundable backup bus or flight now; you can cancel it if the strike is called off.
  • Turn on Korail Talk notifications and check the "operation suspended" status the evening before you travel.
  • If you commute on Line 1, 3 or 4, the Gyeongui-Jungang or the Suin-Bundang line, check a bus route to work this weekend, before you need it.
  • Check the news on 9 December. Deals in past disputes have come in the final hours.
