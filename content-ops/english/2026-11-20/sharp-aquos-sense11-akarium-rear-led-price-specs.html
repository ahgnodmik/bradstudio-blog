Most of us have a habit of flipping our phone face down when we want a break from it, and then flipping it back every few minutes in case we missed something. Sharp's new AQUOS sense11, announced in Japan on 17 November 2026, tries to solve that small daily annoyance with a strip of light on the back. Sharp calls the feature Akarium. This piece covers what the phone is, how the rear light works, what it costs in Japan, what is still unconfirmed, and whether you can realistically get one outside Japan.

The short version

  • Announced: 17 November 2026, by Sharp in Japan.
  • On sale: 26 November 2026, in Japan only.
  • Where: NTT Docomo, au, SoftBank, Rakuten Mobile and cable operator J:COM, plus a SIM-free model sold by Sharp.
  • SIM-free price: ¥94,600 for 8GB RAM and 128GB storage, ¥119,900 for 12GB and 256GB. Both prices include Japanese consumption tax.
  • Headline feature: Akarium, a light next to the rear camera unit that signals calls and notifications while the phone lies screen-down.
  • Global release: none announced. Sharp has not said anything about the US, the UK or other markets.

What Akarium actually does

Akarium is a light-and-sound notification feature. In its press release, Sharp describes it as a way to let you know about incoming calls and alerts through soft light and sound, paired with recorded nature sounds meant to create a calm mood. The idea is simple. When the screen faces the table, the back of the phone is the only surface you can see. A small glow tells you that something arrived without pulling you into the screen.

The feature is not new to Sharp's lineup. It debuted on the AQUOS R11, the company's flagship announced in June 2026. Japanese coverage of the R11 framed Akarium as a tool for taking short breaks from the phone while still catching the alerts that matter. The sense11 brings the same concept down to Sharp's best-selling mid-range line, which is the more interesting move. It reached the mid-range about five months after the R11 was announced.

There is one detail we could not pin down. Sharp has not published, in material we could confirm twice, whether you can assign different colours to different apps or limit the light to specific contacts on the sense11. Treat those settings as unconfirmed until the user manual or support pages go live after launch.

How the light is built, and why it differs from the R11

The more technical story is about thickness. According to both ITmedia Mobile and Keitai Watch, the R11 uses LEDs that emit light upward from the circuit board toward the back panel. That needs vertical space. The sense11 is only about 7.6mm thick, so Sharp switched to side-emitting LEDs mounted on a flexible board. Their light travels sideways into a light guide plate, which redirects it out through the back.

A light guide plate is a clear panel that carries light from its edge and spreads it across a surface. Light bounces along inside the plate, and tiny patterns on its surface bend some of it outward. The same principle lights up laptop keyboard backlights and the backlight units of thin LCD monitors. Pairing it with side-emitting LEDs lets a designer keep the light source flat while still shaping where the glow appears.

The trade-off is coverage. Reports note that the illuminated area on the R11 is larger. In other words, Sharp gave up a little glow to keep the sense11 slim. For a light whose job is to say "something happened," that seems like a reasonable compromise.

Rear notification lights are having a moment

Sharp is not alone here. Nothing has built its brand around the Glyph lights on the back of its phones, and several outlets have reported that Google put a notification light called HiLight into the camera bar of its Pixel 11 Pro models. The old front-facing notification LED largely disappeared from Android phones years ago. The new wave lives on the back, and it assumes you will put your phone face down on purpose.

The biggest physical change is the body. Japanese outlets led with the 7.6mm thickness and 157g weight, and it is easy to see why. The chip, by contrast, carries over unchanged from last year's sense10. Sharp put its effort into memory, which now starts at 8GB with a new 12GB tier, and into the slimmer chassis.

One footnote on updates: SoftBank's product page says the update count is calculated from the model's release date. If you buy late in the phone's life, you may get fewer of those four OS upgrades.

Price: a noticeably bigger bill

The SIM-free sense11 starts at ¥94,600 and tops out at ¥119,900, tax included. Japanese site garumax and the international outlet gagadget both described the increase over the previous generation as large. For a line that built its reputation on being a sensible, affordable everyday phone, that is a meaningful shift.

We have deliberately not converted those prices into dollars, pounds or Korean won. There is no official overseas price, and any conversion depends on the exchange rate on the day you check. Carrier prices in Japan also vary with each operator's discount programmes, so the SIM-free figures are the cleanest comparison point.

Can you buy one outside Japan?

Not officially. Both gagadget and Notebookcheck reported that Sharp has not announced a release in the US, the UK or elsewhere outside Japan. We found no announcement for South Korea either.

Importing a Japanese unit is possible, but it puts several checks on you rather than on a retailer:

  • Network bands: Compare the phone's supported LTE and 5G bands with the ones your carrier relies on. Sharp's product page and the Japanese carriers' spec sheets list them. Carrier and SIM-free versions may differ, so check the exact model you plan to buy.
  • Import tax: At roughly ¥94,600 and up, the phone is likely to exceed typical personal-import duty-free thresholds. In South Korea, for example, the general duty-free limit for personal-use goods shipped from Japan is $150, and once you go over, tax applies to the full value. Use your local customs calculator before you order.
  • Radio certification: Many countries require wireless devices to be certified. South Korea, for instance, exempts a single unit brought in for personal use rather than resale, according to a legal interpretation published by the Ministry of Government Legislation. Rules for reselling later are stricter and worth checking with the regulator first.
  • Repairs and warranty: No overseas service network has been announced. A cracked screen could mean shipping the phone back to Japan or relying on an independent repair shop.

Who should care

If you live in Japan and want a light, slim Android phone with long software support, the sense11 is an easy one to shortlist. The real questions are whether the price jump is worth it over discounted older models, and whether you will actually use Akarium.

If you live elsewhere, the sense11 is mostly a signal. It shows that rear notification lights are moving from flagships into mid-range phones, and that slim, light designs are coming back as a selling point. You can get some of the same benefit today without new hardware. Set up Do Not Disturb with a short list of priority contacts, allow repeated calls to ring through, and look in your accessibility settings for a flash-on-notification option. Those three changes make leaving your phone face down a lot less stressful.
