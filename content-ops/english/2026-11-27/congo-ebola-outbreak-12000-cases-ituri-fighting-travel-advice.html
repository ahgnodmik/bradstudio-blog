If you have a trip to Central or East Africa coming up, the Congo Ebola outbreak passing 12,000 confirmed cases is the news to read before you book anything else. Congolese government data released on Thursday 26 November 2026 put confirmed infections at 12,021 and deaths at 5,904, according to Reuters and AP. In the same week, fighting in Ituri province forced health workers to flee and trapped some in a hospital under crossfire. This guide sets out what is confirmed, why the fighting matters, and what travellers should check. It is not medical advice; for decisions about your own health, speak to a travel clinic or your doctor.

Congo Ebola outbreak: the latest confirmed figures

The outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo virus, one of the rarer species of Ebola. Reuters and AP both reported on 26 November that there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment for this species. That is a key difference from the Zaire species behind several earlier Congolese outbreaks, where an approved vaccine exists.

The World Health Organization's latest bulletin, published on 20 November, gave a slightly lower count: 11,613 confirmed cases and 5,678 deaths as of 17 November. The gap reflects different reporting cut-off dates, not a contradiction. WHO said Ituri remains the most affected province.

CBC and Reuters described the outbreak as the second-largest on record, behind only the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic. WHO has said it is the largest Ebola outbreak ever reported in DR Congo. Officials and reporters have also warned that the true toll may be higher than the confirmed count, because cases in areas teams cannot reach are less likely to be tested.

How fighting in Ituri is disrupting the Ebola response

Fighting between Congolese government forces and a rebel group called the Convention for the Popular Revolution (CRP) intensified north of Bunia, the provincial capital, during the week of 23 November, AP reported. UN News and AP gave a consistent account of what happened to responders:

  • Akwe: about a dozen people, including two WHO staff and Congolese health officials, spent three days trapped in a hospital in Akwe under intense crossfire. UN peacekeepers evacuated them to Bunia on Tuesday 24 November.
  • Mongbwalu: WHO temporarily moved its team in Mongbwalu, the mining town where the outbreak was first detected in May, to Bunia after fighting cut road access.
  • On the road: one humanitarian team came under fire while returning to Bunia and sheltered at a UN peacekeeping base before being escorted out.

WHO's Catherine Smallwood said the conflict had blocked teams from reaching some communities, disrupted the follow-up of people exposed to the virus and left some treatment centres short-staffed, according to AP and UN News. Blocked roads also make it harder to deliver supplies, move patients and send teams to carry out safe burials.

Contact tracing is the backbone of Ebola control. When teams cannot visit exposed people every day for 21 days, new cases can appear outside the known chains of transmission. That is why aid groups treat security as a public health issue in this outbreak, not a separate story. The CRP said on Tuesday it was suspending its advance toward Bunia, and the government said its forces had pushed the rebels back, but residents described the situation as volatile on Wednesday, according to AP.

Has Ebola spread beyond DR Congo?

Yes, but in limited numbers so far. Uganda reported cases early in the outbreak, which is why WHO and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) referred to DR Congo and Uganda together from May. Kenya confirmed an imported case on 6 October, and WHO counted nine confirmed cases there as of 17 November, including two deaths, all linked to one cluster. Nigeria confirmed one imported case in Abuja on 20 October; the patient was evacuated to Switzerland, and WHO and SciDev reported that no secondary infections were recorded in Nigeria.

WHO's 20 November bulletin reported no new cases in Kenya or Nigeria since its previous update and rated the risk as very high in DR Congo and high in Kenya and Nigeria. WHO determined in May 2026 that the epidemic is a public health emergency of international concern, and that status has been kept under review since.

Ebola travel advice: what travellers should know

Check official travel notices before you book

Advice changes quickly in an outbreak, and different sources can show different versions on the same day. In May, CDC issued travel health notices for DR Congo and Uganda, according to its health advisory. CDC's travel notice pages have since listed the hardest-hit provinces, Ituri and Nord-Kivu, under its highest "avoid all travel" level, with exceptions for humanitarian and emergency response work. We could not confirm the current level from a second source, so check the CDC travel notices page directly. UK, Korean, Canadian and other governments publish their own travel advice, and that advice can differ.

WHO's own position is that countries should not impose general travel or trade bans on DR Congo or Uganda, while confirmed cases and their contacts should not travel internationally except for medical evacuation. Some governments have still introduced entry rules. CDC said in May that the US had suspended entry for foreign nationals who had been in DR Congo, Uganda or South Sudan in the previous 21 days, with enhanced screening for returning travellers. Whether those rules are unchanged today is unconfirmed, so check before you fly.

Practical steps for anyone travelling in the region

  • Read your insurance policy. Many policies exclude destinations under a government "do not travel" warning. Check medical evacuation cover in writing before departure.
  • Register with your embassy. Most governments run a traveller registration service so they can contact you if security or health conditions change.
  • Follow basic precautions. Ebola spreads through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a sick person or someone who has died of the disease. Avoid healthcare facilities treating Ebola patients unless you work there, avoid funerals and burial rituals, and wash your hands often.
  • Expect screening. Temperature checks and health questionnaires are common at airports and land borders in the region. Allow extra time.
  • Know the 21-day window. Health authorities generally monitor people for 21 days after possible exposure. If you develop fever, weakness, vomiting, diarrhoea or unexplained bleeding within 21 days of leaving an affected area, call a doctor or health line before going in person and tell them where you travelled.

Transit and nearby destinations

Passing through a major airport hub in East Africa is not the same as visiting Ituri. Risk is concentrated where transmission is active. Still, airlines can change schedules at short notice, and border rules can tighten quickly. Book flexible fares where you can and keep copies of your itinerary to show screening staff if asked.

What to watch next

  • Whether the fighting around Bunia eases enough for teams to return to Mongbwalu and Akwe.
  • WHO's next situation report, which will show whether the case count is levelling off or rising faster.
  • Any decision on Kenya and Nigeria, which WHO said were counting down 42 days, two maximum incubation periods, without new cases.
  • Updates to CDC and your own government's travel notices.

If you are due to travel to DR Congo, Uganda or neighbouring countries in the next few weeks, do three things today: open your government's official travel advice page for each country on your route, ask your insurer in writing whether your trip is covered, and book a travel clinic appointment to discuss current precautions. Then set a reminder to check those pages again 48 hours before departure.
