The SpaceX Starmind FCC approval sounds like a licence to launch a million computers into orbit tomorrow. It is not. On Wednesday 25 November 2026 the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) authorised SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for an orbital data-center network called Starmind, but it granted the application only in part, capped the altitude, held back key debris questions and set deadlines that run to 2040. This guide explains what was approved, what was left out, who objected and what to watch next.

What the FCC approved for Starmind

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr announced the decision, and CBS News, CNBC, TechCrunch, Engadget and Via Satellite all reported it on 25 November. The headline number is real: SpaceX may deploy up to one million satellites under this licence. Several outlets, including CBS News and Gizmodo, described it as the first authorisation of its kind in the United States.

The satellites are meant to work as computing modules rather than pure communications relays. The idea is to run artificial intelligence workloads in orbit, where solar power is plentiful and there is no local opposition over land, water or grid capacity. According to Spaceflight Now and other trade coverage, the satellites are designed to pass data to each other mainly by laser links, including links to SpaceX's existing Starlink network.

The key word in every serious report is "partial." CNBC and Engadget both stressed that the order grants the application in part, defers other parts and attaches conditions. So the approval opens a door, but SpaceX still has to come back to the regulator before it can use the whole building.

The conditions: altitude cap and the 600 km line

A lower ceiling than SpaceX asked for

When the FCC accepted the filing for public comment in February, its notice described a system operating between 500 km and 2,000 km, as Broadband Breakfast and Benton Institute reported. The final order allows shells only up to 1,002 km, according to Spaceflight Now and Tech Times. The authorised orbits are split into six altitude bands. Reports disagree on the lowest band: most say 500 km, while at least one outlet says 550 km, so the exact floor is unconfirmed.

Debris rules split the constellation in two

The most important condition is about space debris. The FCC found SpaceX's debris mitigation plan acceptable only for satellites below 600 km, as Spaceflight Now, Hardware Busters and Tech Times all reported. Two of the six bands sit under that line. The other four, running up to 1,002 km, cannot be used until SpaceX files a final debris plan as a modification and the FCC approves it.

The 600 km line matters because of physics. At lower altitudes, thin atmospheric drag pulls dead satellites down within a few years. Higher up, a failed spacecraft can stay in orbit far longer, so the risk of collisions and debris cascades grows. The regulator has effectively said: build low first, and prove your plan before going higher.

The disposal waiver is still pending

SpaceX also asked for a waiver from the FCC's rules on how satellites must be disposed of at the end of their missions. CBS News, Engadget and Trendblog reported that the FCC deferred that request rather than ruling on it. One outlet described the request as covering disposal into heliocentric orbits, meaning sending satellites away from Earth instead of burning them up in the atmosphere. Earlier coverage in other outlets described the decision as a refusal, so the precise outcome should be read as deferred until the order text says otherwise.

Deadlines: from 2033 to 2040

FCC licences come with deployment milestones. Spaceflight Now, CBS News and several trade outlets reported that the first Starmind satellite must be operating by 25 November 2033, and the full constellation must be deployed by 25 November 2040. Missing a milestone can shrink or cancel the authorisation.

Reports of an interim milestone are less consistent. Spaceflight Now reported that half of the constellation must be in orbit by 25 November 2038, while other outlets cite a different intermediate target and date. Treat the mid-point deadline as unconfirmed until the order is widely quoted.

SpaceX's own timeline is faster. In its IPO filing, the company said it expects to begin deploying orbital AI compute satellites as early as 2028, according to CBS News and TechTarget. No launch date for the first Starmind satellite has been announced.

Who objected to the SpaceX satellite plan, and why

Amazon, which runs the Kuiper broadband constellation, satellite operator Viasat and the American Astronomical Society (AAS) all asked the FCC to deny the application, according to CNBC, Gizmodo and Hardware Busters. The FCC turned those petitions down.

  • Orbital debris and congestion. A million additional spacecraft would multiply close approaches and collision-avoidance manoeuvres for every operator in low Earth orbit.
  • Radio interference. Competitors argued the system could crowd spectrum other networks depend on.
  • Night-sky impact. Astronomers warned that bright, numerous satellites could damage ground-based observations that took decades of public investment to build.
  • Environmental effects. Objectors raised questions about launches and atmospheric re-entry at this scale.

Whether any objector will seek reconsideration or a court review has not been announced, so that path is unconfirmed.

Why SpaceX wants data centers in orbit

The business logic ties back to AI. SpaceX announced on 2 February 2026 that it was acquiring Elon Musk's AI company xAI, as AP and Engadget reported. The pitch is that orbit offers near-constant solar power and no fights over land or cooling water, two constraints that are slowing data-center construction on the ground.

The risks are just as real. Coverage of SpaceX's IPO filing by The Next Web and TechTarget noted that the company itself warned the orbital data-center plan relies on complex, unproven technology and may never become commercially viable. Cooling hardware in a vacuum, repairing failed chips and moving large volumes of data back to Earth are all open engineering problems. An FCC licence does not solve any of them.

The FCC has also said it is reviewing other orbital data-center proposals through its normal procedures, according to reports on the decision. Starmind may be the first, but it is unlikely to be the only one.

What happens next

  • A debris filing for the upper bands. SpaceX must submit a final debris mitigation plan before any satellite goes above 600 km. Its timing is unconfirmed.
  • The deferred disposal waiver. The FCC still has to decide on SpaceX's end-of-life disposal request.
  • First launches. The company points to 2028 at the earliest; the regulator's hard deadline is 2033.
  • Responses from objectors. Amazon, Viasat and the AAS have not announced next steps.
  • Competing applications. Other orbital data-center filings are under review at the FCC.

What this means for you

If you follow space policy, astronomy or AI infrastructure, the practical point is that this decision is the start of a long review rather than the end of one. The licence is large on paper and narrow in practice: low orbits only, debris questions still open, and seven years before the first hard deadline. If you work in satellite operations or space insurance, expect debris and conjunction data rules to become a bigger part of every filing.

To follow the story yourself, check the FCC Space Bureau's public notices on fcc.gov and search the filing system for "SpaceX" and "Starmind." The next document to watch for is the modification application with SpaceX's final debris plan. When it appears, the real argument over the upper 400 km of this constellation will begin.
