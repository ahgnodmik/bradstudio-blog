In the space of four days, a string of bomb threats rippled through European airspace. Planes were diverted, a fighter jet shadowed a passenger flight into Budapest, an airport near Warsaw shut its doors for a few hours, and an Aer Lingus jet bound for Rome ended up at a French air base in Normandy. Every search came back empty. If you are flying in or out of Europe in the coming weeks, you probably have two questions: what actually happened, and what happens to you if your flight is the next one caught up in it.

This article reflects reporting available as of November 6, 2026. Each fact below was checked against at least two independent outlets. Who sent the threats, and whether the incidents are linked, is unconfirmed. Flight counts differ between outlets and may change. Nothing here is legal advice.

A timeline of the threats

Monday, November 2: Luton to Sofia, via Budapest

The first incident in this run involved Wizz Air flight W64302 from London Luton to Sofia, Bulgaria. Euronews, Gulf News and Cyprus Mail all reported that the aircraft diverted to Budapest after a bomb threat was received mid-flight. Hungarian Gripen fighters escorted the plane in, according to Gulf News and Aviation24. A search of the aircraft and passengers found nothing suspicious. At the time it looked like a one-off.

Tuesday, November 3: the first wave and the Modlin closure

The next day, threats hit several flights at once. The most visible case was a LOT Polish Airlines flight from London Heathrow to Warsaw Chopin. After the alert, the Boeing 737 MAX landed instead at Warsaw Modlin, the smaller airport north of the city. Modlin closed while the aircraft and luggage were checked, then announced it had returned to normal operations. LOT said the security inspection was completed and nothing suspicious was found. The Jerusalem Post, Asharq Al-Awsat and France 24 all carried versions of this account.

The same day, an SAS flight from Copenhagen to Warsaw was also reported as targeted, and landed at Chopin to be checked. Outlets including CBC and Global News named LOT, SAS, Wizz Air and Ryanair among the airlines affected that week. Early counts for November 3 varied: Euronews first reported four flights, while Al Jazeera and The Globe and Mail later said at least seven flights and one cruise ship had been targeted.

Thursday, November 5: a larger second wave

Two days later came a bigger round. The Globe and Mail and Ireland's RTÉ both reported that at least 27 flights received hoax threats that day. AP put the figure at more than a dozen, and CNN's live coverage cited at least 14 planes at one point, which shows how quickly the totals moved as the day went on.

The best-documented case was Aer Lingus flight EI402 from Dublin to Rome. The Irish Times, TheJournal.ie and Al Jazeera reported that it was diverted to the Évreux air base in Normandy. French authorities said 151 passengers and six crew were moved to safety, and the threat was later declared a hoax. Irish outlets also reported a threat involving a flight at Cork Airport the same morning. Some aircraft that day were escorted by fighter jets, according to The Globe and Mail and CNN.

Al Jazeera listed flights touching France, Italy, Bulgaria, Spain, Denmark, Poland, Romania, Ireland, the United Kingdom and the Czech Republic, among others. Britain's transport secretary, Heidi Alexander, called them malicious hoax threats, according to AP and CNN.

Who is behind it? Nobody knows yet

This is the part where the reporting is thinnest, and where it pays to be careful. No government has publicly blamed any country, group or individual. EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it was too early to conclude whether the incidents were linked, and that any threat against civilians was unacceptable, according to Euronews and Eunews.

Some analysts have raised the possibility of a state-backed hybrid campaign, and The Globe and Mail and CNN both noted that European officials have accused Russia of hybrid attacks in other contexts. The same coverage says Russia denies such activity, and analysts quoted in it also pointed to other explanations, including individuals acting alone and copycats. There is no confirmed attribution, and this article does not take a side.

On how the threats arrived, Prague airport said the threats it received about four flights came by anonymous email, and the Czech investigation was taken over by the national counter-terrorism unit, according to The Globe and Mail and CBC. Whether every threat across Europe came the same way is unconfirmed. No arrests had been reported at the time of writing.

Your rights if a bomb threat disrupts your flight

For travellers, the most practical question is what an airline owes you. The main rulebook is EU Regulation 261/2004, usually shortened to EU261. It covers flights departing from an EU airport on any airline, and flights arriving in the EU on an EU-based airline. The UK has its own near-identical version, often called UK261.

Cash compensation: probably not

EU261 normally entitles you to fixed compensation of 250 to 600 euros, depending on distance, when you arrive three or more hours late or your flight is cancelled at short notice. But the airline does not have to pay that compensation if the disruption was caused by extraordinary circumstances that could not have been avoided even with all reasonable measures. Passenger-rights firm AirHelp and France's The Connexion both describe bomb threats and similar security risks as falling into that category. In practice, expect the airline to decline a compensation claim for a delay caused by one of these hoaxes.

Duty of care: still yes

The exemption covers the cash payment, not the airline's duty to look after you. AirHelp, The Connexion and Ryanair's own EU261 help pages all describe a right to care that continues when the delay is long enough to qualify. That usually means:

  • Meals and refreshments in reasonable relation to the waiting time.
  • A hotel room, plus transport between the airport and the hotel, if you are stuck overnight.
  • Two free phone calls, emails or similar messages.

If your flight is cancelled, you are also normally entitled to choose between a refund and rerouting to your final destination. Airlines word the details differently, and Ryanair's help pages note that care obligations stop once you opt for a refund or a much later rerouting. Read the airline's terms carefully and, if in doubt, check the regulation text on EUR-Lex.

If you were diverted to another airport

A diversion like the one to Modlin or Évreux raises a separate issue: you land somewhere you did not plan to be. Generally, if the airline lands you at a different airport serving the same city or region, it is expected to cover the cost of getting you to the airport you booked, or to a nearby destination you agree on. If you land somewhere much further away, the airline should rebook or reroute you. Because the threats in this case were security events, the airline may argue that its obligations are limited, so keep records and ask in writing.

What to do if you are flying this month

  • Turn on airline app notifications. Diversions and gate changes tend to be pushed through the app before they reach the departures board.
  • Keep your boarding pass and every receipt. If the airline does not provide meals or a hotel on the spot, you can usually claim reasonable costs afterwards, but only with proof.
  • Ask for care at the desk, not later. Meal vouchers and hotel rooms are easiest to get while you are still at the airport.
  • Do not count on compensation. For a security-related delay, a 250 to 600 euro payout is unlikely. Focus on rerouting, refunds and care.
  • Check your travel insurance policy. Some policies cover missed connections or extra accommodation. Read the wording yourself, since coverage varies widely.
  • Follow official sources. Airport and airline statements are more reliable than social media posts during an incident.

What we still don't know

  • Perpetrator and motive: unconfirmed. No arrests reported.
  • Whether the incidents are linked: unconfirmed, per the EU transport commissioner.
  • Exact numbers: counts for November 3 range from four to at least seven flights; for November 5, from more than a dozen to at least 27.
  • The cruise ship: one ship was reported as targeted on November 3, but its operator and route are unconfirmed.
  • How every threat was sent: email is confirmed for Prague, unconfirmed elsewhere.
  • New security measures: most airports and airlines have not published details.

If you have a European flight booked before the end of the month, take five minutes today. Save your airline's customer service contact in your phone, switch on flight alerts, and photograph your booking confirmation. If your flight is disrupted, ask the airline staff on the spot for rerouting, meals and, if needed, a hotel, and keep every receipt.
