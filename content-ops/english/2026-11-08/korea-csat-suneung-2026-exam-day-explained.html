On Thursday, November 19, 2026, South Korea will rearrange an ordinary working day around a single exam. Offices will open late, the stock market will start an hour behind schedule, extra subway trains will run before dawn, and for about half an hour in the early afternoon, planes will be kept off runways so that no engine noise reaches a classroom. The reason is the College Scholastic Ability Test, known in Korean as the suneung. If you have seen headlines about South Korea "shutting down" for a test and wondered what actually happens, this explainer walks through the day, the scoring and what comes after.

How we checked: dates and figures below were confirmed by at least two independent sources, mostly Korean government releases and major Korean news outlets. Items that were reported for previous years but not yet confirmed for 2026 are labeled. Details are current as of November 8, 2026, and official notices can change.

What the CSAT is

The CSAT is South Korea's national university entrance exam. It is set and administered by the Korea Institute for Curriculum and Evaluation (KICE), a government-funded research institute, under the Ministry of Education, which coordinates the nationwide support measures. Universities use CSAT results heavily in their regular admissions round, and many students and families treat it as the single most important day of secondary school.

A naming quirk confuses many readers. The exam held in November 2026 is officially the "2027 academic year" CSAT, because it decides admission for the school year that starts in March 2027. Korean coverage also describes this as the last CSAT in the current format with elective options in Korean and math, before a reformed exam begins for the 2028 academic year.

The rise in repeat test-takers, students who already graduated from high school and are sitting the exam again, has been one of the main talking points this year. Current high school seniors registered for the exam fell to 355,391, while graduates rose to 173,706, according to KICE figures reported by several outlets.

How the country slows down

On October 20, 2026, the Ministry of Education reported its exam-day support plan to the Cabinet. Most of the measures have been part of exam day for years, and the 2026 version follows the same pattern.

Later office hours

The government has asked public offices and private businesses to have employees start work at 10 a.m. or later, so that the morning rush does not collide with students heading to test centers. This is a request for cooperation, not a legal order, but government agencies follow it and many large companies do too.

A later stock market

The Korea Exchange announced on November 5 that regular trading on the KOSPI, KOSDAQ and KONEX markets will open at 10 a.m. and close at 4:30 p.m. on exam day, one hour later than usual at both ends. The pre-market session will not run. Metro Seoul and Kyunghyang Shinmun both reported the change.

More trains, closed streets near schools

Subways in the greater Seoul area will run more frequently between 6 a.m. and 8:10 a.m. Vehicles will be blocked from entering the area within 200 meters of test centers, so parents driving their children have to drop them off before that point and let them walk the rest of the way. Students must be in their seats by 8:10 a.m.

Police on standby

The government plan places emergency transport vehicles from police stations and other public agencies along routes students use. In past years, Korean and international media have shown officers carrying late students to test centers on police motorcycles and in patrol cars, and police set up stations near major intersections to help anyone stuck in traffic. The size of the 2026 police deployment had not been announced as of this writing, so we are not quoting a number.

Planes on hold during the listening test

The third period of the exam is English, and it opens with a listening section in which audio is played over classroom speakers. The government plan schedules the listening test for 1:10 p.m. to 1:35 p.m. and says takeoffs and landings of aircraft will be adjusted during that window, while military training such as artillery fire pauses. Drivers near test centers are asked to slow down and avoid honking, and construction sites are asked to keep noise down.

For the November 2025 exam, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport kept flights from taking off or landing nationwide from 1:05 p.m. to 1:40 p.m., a 35-minute window that added five minutes on either side of the listening section, with exceptions for emergencies. Aircraft already in the air were held at higher altitude. The exact flight window for 2026 had not been formally published when we checked, so expect a similar arrangement but treat the precise times as unconfirmed until the transport ministry announces them.

How the CSAT is scored

CSAT results are not reported as a simple percentage. Students receive three kinds of numbers, and universities weigh them differently.

  • Standard score. The raw score adjusted for how hard that section was relative to everyone who sat it. It lets universities compare students who chose different elective subjects.
  • Percentile. The share of test-takers who scored below the student.
  • Grade (1 to 9). For relatively graded sections such as Korean, math and the inquiry subjects, grade 1 goes to roughly the top 4% of test-takers, with wider bands below.

Two sections work differently. English has been graded on an absolute scale since the 2018 academic year: a raw score of 90 or more out of 100 earns grade 1, 80 to 89 grade 2, and so on in ten-point steps. Korean history has been absolutely graded since the 2017 academic year and is mandatory for all test-takers. For these sections, how many other students did well has no effect on a student's grade.

After the exam, KICE posts provisional answer keys and opens a short window for objections, which an independent review panel considers before the answers are finalized. Students typically self-score using a copy of their answers they are allowed to take home. Official scores arrive on December 11, through high schools for current students and through the registration office or online for others, and universities then run their regular admissions round.

What visitors and residents should expect

  • If you fly on November 19, expect schedule adjustments around the early afternoon. Check with your airline the day before rather than assuming your departure time is unchanged.
  • If you work in Korea, check whether your office is following the 10 a.m. start. Many are, but it is not universal.
  • If you live near a high school, avoid driving near the entrance in the morning and keep noise down during the day, especially around 1 p.m.
  • If you see crowds outside schools at dawn, those are younger students and parents cheering test-takers on. It is a long-standing tradition, and it is fine to watch from a respectful distance.

If your plans touch Korea on November 19, put three times in your calendar now: 8:10 a.m. for school entry and the morning traffic peak, 10 a.m. for delayed office and market openings, and the early-afternoon listening window when flights pause. Then check the Ministry of Education and KICE notices again in the days before the exam in case anything shifts.
