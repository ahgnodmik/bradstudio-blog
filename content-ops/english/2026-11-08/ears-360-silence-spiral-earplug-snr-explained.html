If you have ever rolled a foam earplug between your fingers, pushed it into your ear and waited for it to expand, you know the trade-off. Push it too deep and it aches by morning. Leave it shallow and it works its way out on the pillow. A small Paris company called Ears 360 thinks the fix is not a better foam but a different way of holding the plug in place. Its silicone earplug, Silence, uses a soft spiral that hooks into the curves of the outer ear instead of relying on pressure inside the ear canal. It went through a Kickstarter campaign this fall. Here is what we could confirm about how it works, what the noise rating means, where the campaign stands, and what is still unknown.

How we checked: product details below were reported by at least two independent sources. Performance figures come from the maker and are labeled as such. We have not tested the product, and we found no independent reviews yet. Details are current as of November 8, 2026.

A design that anchors on the outer ear

The foam earplug most people know dates to the early 1970s. A research team led by Ross Gardner Jr. developed the slow-recovery foam behind the E-A-R plug, which reached the market in 1972, according to a paper presented to the Acoustical Society of America and a history published by 3M, which now owns the brand. The basic method has stayed the same since: compress, insert, let it expand and seal.

Silence changes the anchor point. A silicone tip sits at the entrance of the ear canal to block sound, while a flexible spiral attached to it tucks into the folds of the outer ear, along the curved rim anatomists call the helix. The spiral keeps the tip from sliding out, so the tip does not need to be pushed as deep. Yanko Design, Trend Hunter and the Brazilian site Resumer describe the mechanism in the same way. The spiral is retractable and is meant to coil to the shape of each ear.

Kicktraq lists the project creator as Youcef Abdaoui in Paris, and Yanko Design names the same person as the designer and says prototyping began in 2024. Ears 360 was previously known for a reusable ear cleaner. Trend Hunter and Yanko Design report that Silence carries the European CE mark.

The noise rating: a maker claim, read carefully

Ears 360 says Silence is rated SNR 24 dB under EN 352-2:2020, the European standard for earplugs, and Yanko Design and Trend Hunter both report the figure as coming from an outside lab. We could not find the test report itself, so treat 24 dB as the manufacturer's claim. One Yanko Design article also mentions a higher figure with alternative tips; that appears in a single source and remains unconfirmed.

The company has also published customer satisfaction percentages for concerts and commuting. Those come from its own surveys and we do not repeat them here.

SNR vs NRR: why the numbers do not line up

If you shop in the US, the box usually shows an NRR, or Noise Reduction Rating. European packaging shows SNR, or Single Number Rating. Both are expressed in decibels, so it is tempting to compare them directly. That is a mistake.

  • Where they come from. NRR is the US rating required by the Environmental Protection Agency for hearing protectors sold there, based on an ANSI test method. SNR is calculated under ISO 4869 and used with the EN 352 standards in Europe.
  • How they are calculated. The two systems use different frequency weightings and different statistical adjustments for how much protection most wearers actually get. A number on one scale is not the same number on the other.
  • Rough rule of thumb. Hearing-protection makers and safety publishers generally say the SNR for a given product tends to come out a few decibels higher than its NRR. Sources disagree on the exact gap, and there is no official conversion formula, so do not convert one into the other for anything that matters.
  • Lab vs your ear. Both ratings come from controlled lab fittings. Real-world protection depends on fit, ear shape and how carefully the plug is inserted, and is often lower than the label. That applies to every earplug, not just this one.

The practical takeaway: when you compare earplugs, compare SNR with SNR or NRR with NRR. If one product lists only SNR and the other only NRR, the comparison is approximate at best.

Who might be interested, and what we do not know

The maker and the publications that covered the launch mostly pitch Silence at side sleepers, since less of the plug sits outside the ear to get pressed by a pillow, and at people who find deep foam insertion uncomfortable. That is a reasonable design idea, but it is still the maker's argument. We found no independent tests of comfort, fit across different ear shapes, or durability. Ears differ a lot, and a spiral that sits comfortably in one person's outer ear may not suit another's.

This article makes no claims about sleep quality or hearing health. Earplugs reduce sound; they are not a treatment for anything. If you have ear pain, ringing in the ears, frequent infections or sleep problems, talk to a doctor or audiologist before changing what you put in your ears.

If you want something similar now

  • Flanged silicone plugs. Reusable, widely sold, and they come in sizes. Check the label for SNR or NRR.
  • Moldable silicone putty plugs. These cover the canal opening rather than going inside it, which some people find more comfortable for sleep.
  • Foam, inserted properly. Roll it thin, pull the top of your ear up and back to straighten the canal, insert, then hold it for a few seconds while it expands. Many people who say foam does not work for them have never been shown this.
  • Hygiene basics. Wash reusable plugs as the maker instructs, let them dry fully, and replace them when they lose shape. Stop using any plug that causes pain or itching.

Before you back or buy anything, take out the earplugs you use now, note whether the package lists SNR or NRR and the number, and keep that note next to any new product you consider. Then wait for the first independent reviews from Silence backers before deciding whether the spiral is worth switching for.
