For most of the last two decades, there has been only one way to become a lawyer in South Korea: get into a three-year law school, graduate, and pass the national bar exam. A bill introduced on Thursday 12 November 2026 would add a second route that skips law school entirely. Korean media have nicknamed it the "sasi revival act," after the old national judicial exam that was scrapped in 2017. This explainer sets out what the bill actually proposes, how it differs from the old system, where the government stands, and what each side of the debate argues. It does not take a side.

The short version

  • Choi Hyuk-jin, an independent proportional-representation member of the National Assembly, has introduced an amendment to the Bar Examination Act creating a "preliminary bar exam".
  • Passing it would make you eligible to sit the national bar exam without a law school degree. It does not make you a lawyer by itself.
  • A similar bill from Lee Un-ju of the ruling Democratic Party was reported to have been introduced in April 2026.
  • As of 16 November 2026, neither bill has been passed. Committee review and any vote are still to come.

Background: from the judicial exam to law schools

For decades, Korea picked its judges, prosecutors and lawyers through the national judicial examination, known as the sasi. It was administered by the Ministry of Justice in three stages: a multiple-choice first round, an essay-based second round and an interview. Successful candidates then trained at the Judicial Research and Training Institute. In its later years, applicants needed at least 35 credits in law subjects and an approved English test score, but no law degree. Credits earned through the Academic Credit Bank System or self-study degree routes counted.

In 2009, Korea introduced US-style graduate law schools. The judicial exam was phased down and held for the last time in 2017, when only the second and third rounds took place and 55 candidates passed the second round. Since then, the national bar exam has been open only to law school graduates, who get five attempts within five years of graduating.

There are 25 law schools with a combined annual intake cap of 2,000 students. In April 2026 the Ministry of Justice announced that 1,714 people had passed the 15th national bar exam.

The argument for the switch was that legal professionals should be trained through a structured graduate education, rather than selected by a single high-stakes exam. Critics of the old system also pointed to the so-called gosi nangin, people who spent many years, sometimes a decade or more, repeatedly sitting the exam.

What the November 2026 bill proposes

According to consistent reporting by several Korean outlets on 12 November, the Choi bill would:

  • have the Minister of Justice hold a preliminary bar exam once a year;
  • give those who pass the same eligibility to sit the bar exam as law school graduates;
  • allow five attempts at the bar exam within five years of passing, with time spent on military service excluded;
  • test public law, civil law and criminal law in a multiple-choice format;
  • require a minimum number of credits in law subjects, which could be earned at ordinary universities, Korea National Open University, cyber universities or through the Academic Credit Bank System;
  • bar current law school students, students on leave and law school graduates from taking it.

Ten lawmakers, including Choi, sponsored the bill. Choi has said the aim is not to abolish law schools but, in his words as reported, to turn a single door into two. The bill number, the exact filing date, how many people would be selected each year and how successful candidates would receive practical training were not confirmed in reporting we could cross-check.

The key difference from the old system is that the preliminary exam is a gateway, not a destination. Everyone would still have to pass the same bar exam that law school graduates take. That is why supporters describe it as a complement to law schools, while some critics still see it as the first step toward bringing back the old exam.

The earlier bill and the August forum

Choi's bill is not the first. Lee Un-ju, a Democratic Party lawmaker, introduced a preliminary-exam amendment that coverage dates to April 2026. Under her version, the number of people selected each year would be capped at one-fifth of total law school admissions, which on the current cap of 2,000 means up to 400 people.

Lee held a forum at the National Assembly on 27 August 2026. She cited the cost of law school and the five-attempt limit as problems, and argued that a preliminary exam should be seen as a supplement to law schools rather than a replacement. Measures such as night-time law school programmes, scholarships and online teaching, she said, improve access to law schools, while a preliminary exam diversifies the route into the profession. At the same forum, the Ministry of Justice was reported as sharing the concern that entry barriers should be lowered, while saying the possible side effects of a preliminary exam needed careful review (this account appeared in forum coverage we could not match in a second outlet, so treat it as unconfirmed). The ministry has not, as far as we could find, published a formal position on either bill.

The March 2026 report and the presidential office's denial

The debate flared earlier in the year. On 11 March 2026, a Korean outlet reported that Cheong Wa Dae, the presidential office, was considering reviving the judicial exam alongside the law school system, with roughly 50 to 150 people selected a year. On the same day, presidential spokesperson Kang Yu-jung said the report was not true. Newsis, Herald Business and Wikitree all carried the denial.

Part of the reason the story spread is something President Lee Jae-myung said earlier. At a town hall meeting in Gwangju on 25 June 2025, he said, as reported by NewDaily and Kukinews, that people with the ability might be able to qualify as lawyers after some form of verification even without going to law school. In the same remarks, he indicated that abolishing the law school system would not be easy.

What each side argues

Reactions from legal groups after the March report showed how divided the profession is. On 12 March, the Korean Association of Law Professors issued a statement calling for a new judicial exam to select public-sector judicial officers. On the same day, the Korean Lawyers Association called the idea of reviving the exam an "anachronistic regression." Both statements were reported by Hankyung, Herald Business and Money Today.

In coverage of the November bill, the main arguments ran roughly as follows.

Supporters say:

  • Law school tuition and living costs over three years make access to the profession depend on money, and a lower-cost route would reopen a "ladder" for people from less well-off backgrounds.
  • The five-attempt limit shuts out people permanently, and a second route would give them another chance.
  • A preliminary exam would sit alongside law schools rather than replace them.

Critics say:

  • A preliminary exam runs against the purpose of the law school system, which emphasises specialised legal education and practical training.
  • Exam-prep competition and private cram-school spending could grow again.

Which side is right is a policy judgement for lawmakers and voters. This explainer only reports who said what.

What is still unconfirmed or undecided

  • Bill number and exact filing date of the Choi bill: unconfirmed.
  • Number of people selected and how practical training would work under the Choi bill: undecided.
  • Committee review, floor vote and any implementation date for either bill: undecided.
  • Formal positions of the Korean Bar Association and the Korean Association of Law Schools on these bills: not found.
  • Any presidential office plan to restructure how legal professionals are selected: undecided.

What to watch, and what to do now

If you are weighing a legal career in Korea, nothing has changed yet: law school remains the only route to the bar exam. The next signal to watch is whether the Legislation and Judiciary Committee puts either bill on its agenda. You can follow both on the National Assembly's bill information system (likms.assembly.go.kr, in Korean) by searching for "변호사시험법 일부개정법률안" (amendment to the Bar Examination Act) with the sponsor's name, and bookmark them so you see each stage as it happens.
