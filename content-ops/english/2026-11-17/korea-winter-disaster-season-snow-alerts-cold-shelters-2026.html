If you live in Korea and your phone has ever screamed at you with a government alert, get ready for a new kind this winter: heavy-snow alerts now cover the whole country. Korea's official winter disaster season began on Sunday 15 November 2026 and runs to Monday 15 March 2027. This explainer covers what the government announced, how the new snow alerts work, where to find a warming shelter, what is changing on the roads, and the few things worth doing this week.

What was announced, and when

On Wednesday 11 November 2026, the Ministry of the Interior and Safety (MOIS) released its comprehensive plan for natural disasters over the 2026-27 winter. The main points:

  • The winter disaster countermeasure period runs from 15 November 2026 to 15 March 2027.
  • The national crisis alert for heavy snow and cold waves starts at the lowest level, "Attention".
  • Heavy-snow emergency text alerts expand from 101 trial districts to all 227 cities, counties and districts nationwide, starting Monday 23 November.
  • The number of designated cold-weather shelters rises from about 53,000 last winter to about 66,000.
  • Resident evacuation support teams, until now a summer flood measure, will operate in winter for the first time.
  • De-icing stockpiles reach 1.19 million tonnes, about 126% of last year's level.
  • 9,240 high-risk sites are under focused management.

On the same weekend, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) began its own road snow-removal period over the same dates, with a 24-hour situation room.

The new heavy-snow alerts, explained

Korea already sends cell-broadcast emergency alerts for earthquakes, heavy rain and other hazards. Heavy-snow alerts were trialled last winter in 101 districts, mainly the Seoul capital area, South Chungcheong (with Daejeon and Sejong) and North Jeolla. From 23 November they apply everywhere.

The key thing to understand is that these alerts are triggered by snow that has actually fallen, not by a forecast. That makes them different from the heavy-snow advisories and warnings issued by the Korea Meteorological Administration, which are based on expected snowfall. There are two triggers:

  • Traffic risk: 5 cm or more of fresh snow within one hour.
  • Structural collapse risk: 20 cm or more of fresh snow within 24 hours, while snow is still falling at 3 cm or more per hour. In Gangwon Province and on Ulleung Island in North Gyeongsang, the threshold is 30 cm.

"Fresh snow" means new snow only. If 30 cm is already on the ground and another 2 cm falls in an hour, that counts as 2 cm. So you may see deep snow outside and still get no alert, because what matters is how fast it is piling up now. The higher bar in Gangwon and Ulleung reflects places that routinely get heavy snow; a 20 cm trigger there would probably fire so often that people would stop paying attention.

Alerts go to phones in the affected area based on location, not on your registered address. If you are travelling, you get the local alert. If you switched emergency alerts off, you will not get them, so check your settings (search "emergency alerts" in Android settings; on an iPhone, look at the bottom of the Notifications screen).

What to do when one arrives

  • A traffic-risk alert is a strong hint to delay driving or switch to the subway.
  • A collapse-risk alert means you should not stay inside greenhouses, temporary structures or old sheds. Do not climb onto a roof to clear snow; call your local district office or 119.

Cold-weather shelters: where to warm up

"Hanpa shelters" (cold-weather shelters) are public places where anyone can come in to get warm. Many are senior centres, village halls and community service centres. This winter the government is adding private sites such as bank branches and post offices, taking the total to roughly 66,000.

To find one near you:

  • Use Gungmin Anjeon 24 (Safety Korea 24) at safekorea.go.kr. In April 2026 MOIS merged its disaster portal, the Safety e-Report reporting service and several other safety sites into this single portal.
  • Use the Emergency Ready App (Anjeon Didimdol), which shows nearby facilities when you allow location access.
  • Some cities run their own maps; Seoul, for example, lists shelters on its city safety site.

Opening hours vary a lot. Senior centres may close in the evening or at weekends, so call ahead during a cold snap. If you have older neighbours or relatives who live alone, the most useful thing you can do is look up two or three shelters near them and write the addresses down.

Evacuation support and high-risk areas

For the first time, the resident evacuation support teams used in summer floods will be deployed in winter. The government has identified about 5,100 residents who would struggle to evacuate on their own, such as older people, people with disabilities and people with chronic illness living in residential greenhouses or mountain villages. Reports give different figures for the size of the support teams themselves, so we have left that number out (unconfirmed).

The government has also designated 9,240 high-risk sites in five categories, including structures vulnerable to snow loads, roads prone to icing or hard to clear, and areas likely to be cut off. These are to be inspected at least once a month.

On the roads: black ice and navigation warnings

Black ice is the hazard Korean drivers fear most in winter, because it looks like wet asphalt. This year MOLIT has raised its internal response level for road icing from "Attention" to "Caution" and put staff on a standing emergency footing. Specific changes:

  • 492 road sections with a high risk of icing get extra brine-spraying equipment and closer patrols and monitoring from 11 p.m. to 8 a.m.
  • Warnings about likely icing sections will be shown through four navigation services: Kakao Navi, T map, Atlan and Naver Map. Exactly how each app displays the warning is up to the provider (unconfirmed).
  • Electronic road signs will show snowfall, falling temperatures, road conditions and snowplough deployment.
  • When snow is forecast, crews aim to spread de-icer one to two hours before it starts.

Common-sense driving rules still matter more than any app. Slow down before bridges, tunnel mouths and shaded bends; double your following distance; avoid sudden braking or steering; and never overtake a snowplough, because the road ahead of it has not been cleared yet.

Reporting hazards from 1 December

From Tuesday 1 December 2026 to 15 March 2027, the government runs a winter hazard reporting campaign through Safety e-Report. You can report uncleared roads and pavements or badly maintained grit boxes near you. A photo that shows a nearby building or sign, plus the time and a one-line description, makes a report much easier to act on. The service is in Korean, but a clear photo and a location pin do most of the work.

What is still unknown

  • How severe this winter will be: the outlook cited in the MOIS plan points to temperatures near or above normal but large swings, so heavy snow is still possible in places.
  • When each region will see its first heavy-snow alert: depends on the weather.
  • How each navigation app will present icing warnings: unconfirmed.

What to do this week

Tonight, check that emergency alerts are switched on, on your phone and on the phones of anyone older in your family. Look up the two closest cold-weather shelters on safekorea.go.kr and save the addresses. Update your navigation app, put gloves and an ice scraper in the car, and agree on one simple rule at home: if a 5 cm-per-hour snow alert arrives before work, you leave the car and take the train.
