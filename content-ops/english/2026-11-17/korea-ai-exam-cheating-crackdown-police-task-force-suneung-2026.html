Three days before more than half a million students sit South Korea's national college entrance exam, the police have opened a nationwide crackdown on exam cheating with AI-enabled smart glasses and tiny cameras. The new effort goes beyond test-takers: it also targets people who make, sell or advertise cheating gear, and even anonymous posts online in which people boast about having cheated. This explainer covers what was announced, why it happened now, what the exam rules say, why online sales are hard to block, and what remains undecided.

What the police announced on 16 November

On Monday 16 November 2026, the Korean National Police Agency (KNPA) set up an "exam misconduct task force" led by the head of its investigation bureau. The crackdown began the same day and covers exam cheating of all kinds, not only the college entrance test. According to multiple Korean news reports on the announcement, the plan has four parts:

  • Focused enforcement against cheating at exams across the country, starting 16 November.
  • A wider net: anyone who makes, supplies or sells equipment for the purpose of cheating, and anyone who shares cheating methods or advertises them, can be investigated.
  • Online monitoring of social media and message boards, including tracking down people who post "confessions" that they cheated with AI.
  • Dedicated investigation teams for organised cases, with the aim of identifying anyone behind them.

The police also said they would set up a hotline with the Ministry of Education and other agencies so that information can move quickly between them.

Why now

AI cheating has been building as a story in Korea for most of 2026. Over the spring and summer, Korean media reported cases in which test-takers wore AI smart glasses at national technical qualification exams and at a TOEIC English test. In the autumn, smart glasses were reported at essay exams run by individual universities as part of admissions.

The trigger for the police task force was more recent. A person who had passed a national professional licensing exam publicly admitted to cheating with AI, and a wave of anonymous posts followed online in which other people claimed to have done the same. Korean outlets stress that these posts are unverified: nobody has confirmed whether the claims or the attached material are genuine. The police say they will trace the authors and establish whether any actual offence took place.

The timing matters because the College Scholastic Ability Test, known in Korea as the Suneung, takes place on Thursday 19 November 2026. For many students it is the single most important exam of their school career.

What the exam rules say about smart glasses

The Ministry of Education added AI smart glasses to the list of items banned from Suneung test sites earlier this year. The candidate guidance for this exam bans all electronic devices, naming smart glasses alongside mobile phones, smartwatches, Bluetooth earphones and e-cigarettes. Only analogue watches without communication or payment functions or digital displays are allowed.

Several points in the rules are worth spelling out:

  • Possession alone counts. Under the candidate guidance, simply having a banned device can be treated as misconduct, even if it is never used.
  • Glasses checks every period. The Ministry of Education has said invigilators will check candidates who wear glasses in every exam period.
  • Refusing a check has consequences. A candidate who refuses an invigilator's request to inspect their glasses can be treated as having committed misconduct.
  • The current penalty for wearing or using a banned device during the exam is that the year's results are cancelled and the candidate is barred from the next year's Suneung.

At regional level, education offices are adding staff. The Gyeonggi Provincial Office of Education said it would send supervising officials and support staff to its test sites in pairs to help invigilators judge suspicious glasses on the spot.

The proposed three-year ban is not law yet

In late October, the Ministry of Education said it would push to amend the Higher Education Act so that a Suneung cheating ban lasts three years instead of one. It also said it would actively file criminal complaints or request police investigations when smart-glasses cheating is caught. The three-year rule has not passed the National Assembly, so it does not apply to this year's exam and its timing remains undecided.

What criminal charges could apply

Korean news coverage of the police announcement says exam cheating can be prosecuted as obstruction of official duties by deception, or as obstruction of business, depending on the case. Reports note that obstruction of official duties by deception carries up to five years in prison or a fine of up to 10 million won. In the technical qualification cases earlier this year, violations of the National Technical Qualifications Act were also cited.

Which charge actually applies will depend on each investigation and, eventually, on the courts. Nothing here is legal advice, and anyone worried about a specific situation should talk to a qualified Korean lawyer.

Why online sales are hard to block

This is the most contested part of the story. Reports on 17 November describe social media posts that explain cheating set-ups and even point to where the gear can be bought. The public reaction has been: why not just take these listings down?

The answer from regulators is that selling the hardware is not itself illegal. Tiny cameras and smart glasses have normal, lawful uses. The Korea Media and Communications Commission said it would work with platforms and other agencies to stop information that encourages cheating from spreading. It also said it cannot delete or block sales listings or shopping sites across the board. Each post has to be reviewed individually by the content standards body for its content and legality. A spokesperson for that body explained the difficulty: the product becomes a problem when someone uses it to cheat, not when it is sold.

Platforms have started to act on their own terms. Naver, Korea's largest portal, said its shopping service restricts products that could be used for exam cheating. Sellers may not use words such as "for exams", "cheating" or "Suneung" in product names or descriptions, and may not promote items as undetectable by invigilators. Naver said violators could face sales restrictions. How overseas marketplaces are responding is less clear: one report said related devices still appeared under other search terms, but that claim is unconfirmed.

What is still unknown

  • How many of the online confession posts, if any, describe real offences.
  • What charges sellers and advertisers will face, if any.
  • Whether the three-year Suneung ban will pass, and from which year it would apply.
  • How effective glasses checks will be, given that many smart glasses look like ordinary frames. Teachers' groups have raised concerns about the burden this places on invigilators.

What this means if you live in Korea

If you are a student or a parent of one, the practical steps are simple. Take every electronic device out of the exam bag the night before. Pick one pair of ordinary glasses for the day. If an invigilator asks to see them, hand them over straight away. Do not post jokes online about cheating with AI, because the police have said they will trace such posts.

If you are not sitting an exam, the story still touches you. Korea has many high-stakes tests beyond the Suneung, from civil service exams to professional licences and English tests, and the task force covers all of them. If you come across an advert selling "exam" gadgets, the most useful thing you can do is report it through the platform rather than share it.
