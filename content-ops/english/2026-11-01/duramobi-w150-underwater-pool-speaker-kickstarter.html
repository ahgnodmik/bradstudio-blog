Anyone who has played music at a pool party knows the problem. The speaker on the deck sounds fine until you duck your head under the water, and then the music turns into a muffled thump or disappears. Water and air do not pass sound to each other very well, so a speaker designed for one medium does a poor job of reaching the other.

Audio brand duraMOBI thinks it has a portable fix. Its Pool Audio Series W150 is a speaker that hangs off the edge of a pool and sends sound directly into the water. The company announced it in a press release on October 21, 2026, and launched it through a Kickstarter crowdfunding campaign. Yanko Design covered the product on October 26.

Here is what the W150 is, how it compares with the underwater speakers that already exist, and which claims still need independent confirmation.

How it works

The W150 is built around a transducer, a driver that vibrates the water itself rather than pushing air. Both the press release and Yanko Design describe the same layout: the body clips over the pool edge, the controls stay above the waterline, and only the transducer sits below the surface. Swimmers hear the music through the water without earbuds or headphones.

The unit hangs on an adjustable pole, which lets it fit different deck heights and pool wall shapes. The press release says the folding mount is patented. Both the release and Yanko Design stress that nothing needs to be drilled into the pool wall and no wiring has to be run, which is the main difference from the underwater speakers found in competition pools.

Music is stored on the device. Yanko Design and the FreeYork tech blog both report capacity for up to 1,000 songs and more than 12 hours of playback per charge. As with any battery figure from a manufacturer, real runtime will depend on volume and usage. How the speaker connects to a phone, if it does, was not clearly described in the sources reviewed for this article, so wireless connectivity details are unconfirmed.

Coverage and multiple units

duraMOBI says one W150 covers an area of about 20 by 20 feet, or roughly 6 by 6 meters. The press release, Yanko Design and FreeYork all cite that same figure. For a small backyard plunge pool or a compact lap pool, one unit may be enough by that measure.

Larger pools would need several units working together. Yanko Design reports that the company's setup guidance suggests two units for a typical backyard pool and more for resort-size pools. That guidance has appeared in one outlet only, so treat the exact unit counts as unconfirmed until the campaign page or independent testing backs them up.

Who it is aimed at

Backyard pool owners are the obvious audience, but the press release lists a broader set of uses:

  • Swim training, where a coach can talk to swimmers while their heads are under water.
  • Artistic swimming, formerly called synchronized swimming, where athletes need to hear music below the surface to stay in time.
  • Aqua fitness classes, where an instructor's music and cues have to reach people in the water.
  • Hotel and commercial pools, which may want underwater sound without a construction project.

For coaching, the system includes what Yanko Design calls a Live Voice Remote, which sends a coach's voice into the water in real time. The press release also describes voice communication for coaches. How much delay there is between speaking and hearing has not been measured by any independent reviewer that this article could find.

How it compares with existing options

Underwater speakers are not new. Competition and training pools have used them for decades. The Electro-Voice UW30, sold through pro audio retailers such as Full Compass, is a 30-watt flush-mount underwater speaker supplied with a 50-foot cable. Pentair sells underwater speakers and niches for commercial pools and markets them for coach-to-athlete communication and artistic swimming music. The catch with this category is installation: the speaker sits in a niche built into the pool wall, which is far easier to plan during construction than to retrofit.

Most home pool owners use floating Bluetooth speakers instead. They are inexpensive and easy, but their sound goes into the air above the water. Once a swimmer goes under, most of it is lost.

The W150 aims for the space between those two options: underwater sound like a built-in system, with the portability of a floating speaker.

Durability claims

Pool equipment has to put up with chlorine, sun and sometimes salt water. The press release and Yanko Design both say the W150 is built to resist chlorine and salt water. Several other specifications have only appeared in a single source:

  • An IP68 rating with submersion to about 1.5 meters (Yanko Design only).
  • A weight of about 2.24 kg, or roughly 4.9 pounds (Yanko Design only).
  • A charge time of about 90 minutes (Yanko Design only).

Those figures may well turn out to be accurate, but none has been confirmed by a second independent source, so this article lists them as unconfirmed.

Price and campaign status

The W150 is being sold through Kickstarter. The press release announced an October 21 launch, and the Kickstarter tracking site Kicktraq lists a campaign that started the same day.

  • Early-bird reward: from $229, reported by both the press release and Yanko Design.
  • Expected retail price: $399, mentioned by Yanko Design only, so unconfirmed.
  • VIP reward for people who signed up in advance: $199, mentioned in the press release only, so unconfirmed.
  • Campaign end date: Kicktraq shows November 25, but no second source confirms it, so it is unconfirmed.
  • Estimated delivery: early March 2027 has been mentioned in one place only, so it is unconfirmed.
  • Limited quantity: Yanko Design mentions a limited run of units at the sale price; this is a single-source claim and unconfirmed.

duraMOBI is not new to crowdfunding. It previously sold the HumBird bone-conduction speaker through Kickstarter. According to the press release, duraMOBI is a brand of WOFONE TECHNOLOGY LIMITED.

What to know before backing

Backing a crowdfunding campaign is not the same as buying from a store. A few common-sense checks apply to any hardware project, this one included:

  • Read the risks section on the campaign page. Creators are expected to explain what could delay manufacturing or shipping.
  • Check the shipping countries and costs before you pledge. Shipping, taxes and import duties are often charged separately and can add a meaningful amount.
  • Look at the delivery estimate and assume it may slip. Hardware campaigns frequently ship later than first planned.
  • Measure your pool against the 20 by 20 foot coverage claim so you know how many units you would need under the company's own numbers.
  • Wait for hands-on reviews if sound quality matters most to you. As of this writing, no independent outlet has published measurements of the W150's underwater audio.

If the W150 looks useful for your pool, open the Kickstarter page and confirm three things before pledging: that your country is on the shipping list, what the current reward price is after shipping, and which delivery month the creator now states. If you mainly want music during solo laps, compare it with waterproof bone-conduction headphones that are already in stores, since they cost less to try and do not depend on a campaign shipping on time.
