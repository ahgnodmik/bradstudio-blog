If you carry a credit card balance, a variable-rate loan or a mortgage application in progress, the October inflation report is the number that will shape your borrowing costs into the new year. Prices rose faster than forecasters expected, and markets now treat a December rate hike by the Federal Reserve as close to certain. This guide explains what was released, why one familiar figure is missing, and what the shift could mean for a household budget.

This is not investment advice. How we checked: each figure below appears in at least two independent reports, including CNBC, CBS News, HousingWire, KPMG, Kitco and USAGOLD. Anything carried by only one outlet is marked "unconfirmed." Market probabilities are bets by traders, not decisions by the Fed. Details are current as of 11 November 2026.

What the October report showed

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) released the October Consumer Price Index on 10 November. On a seasonally adjusted basis, prices rose 0.4% from September. The consensus forecast was 0.3%. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy because they swing sharply from month to month, rose 0.3% against a forecast of 0.2%. Both readings came in a tenth of a point above expectations.

A miss of 0.1 percentage point sounds small, but it matters because of the direction it points. September's headline gain was 0.6%, so October did cool. It simply did not cool as much as investors had hoped. Core inflation is the measure the Fed watches most closely for underlying pressure, and a 0.3% monthly pace is well above what is consistent with the central bank's 2% annual target if it persists.

Why there is no year-over-year number

Most inflation headlines quote an annual rate. This release did not include one. The federal government shutdown that ran from 1 October to 12 November 2025 stopped the BLS from collecting October 2025 prices, so there is no baseline for a 12-month comparison. The agency said it cannot publish 12-month changes for indexes missing October 2025 data. KPMG estimated the annual rate at about 4.2% using interpolation, and other economists put it nearer 4.0%. Neither is an official statistic, so treat any annual figure you see this month as an estimate.

How markets reacted

Gold fell roughly 2% on the day, to around $4,030 to $4,050 an ounce, according to Kitco and USAGOLD. Silver dropped about 3%, though the exact percentage varies by the time each outlet measured it, so the precise figure is unconfirmed. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed toward 5%. The logic is straightforward: higher inflation raises the odds of higher interest rates, which strengthens the dollar and lifts bond yields. Gold pays no interest, so it tends to lose appeal when yields rise.

On rates, CNBC reported that the CME FedWatch tool showed traders fully pricing a 25 basis point hike at the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting on 8 and 9 December. The probability of a larger 50 basis point move jumped from near zero to about 13.6%. CBS News cited a figure of 15% for the larger hike, so the number moved during the day. Either way, these are market prices, not policy. The Fed has not announced anything.

Some context helps. The Fed raised its benchmark rate by 25 basis points in September to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. At its 28 October meeting it held rates steady in a 10 to 2 vote, with both dissenters favoring a hike. Governor Christopher Waller has said that a core reading around 0.3% would justify considering a 50 basis point move in December, according to CNBC. That is his view, not a committee decision.

What it means for your household budget

A 0.4% monthly rise in prices means a basket of goods that cost $1,000 in September cost about $1,004 in October. One month of that is easy to absorb. The concern is the pattern: monthly increases at this pace add up over a year, and wages do not always keep up. If your grocery, insurance or utility bills feel heavier than they did last spring, the data suggest you are not imagining it.

The practical response is to look at the line items you can control. Recurring subscriptions, insurance renewals and phone plans are often the easiest places to find savings, because providers rarely lower prices unless you ask or switch. Comparing your last three months of statements side by side can show which costs are drifting upward fastest.

What it means for borrowers

Borrowers feel Fed decisions most directly. Here is how each common type of debt tends to respond, in general terms.

  • Credit cards. Most card rates are variable and tied to the prime rate, which usually moves in step with the Fed. If the Fed raises rates in December, card rates typically follow within one or two billing cycles. Paying down a revolving balance now reduces your exposure.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgages and home equity lines. These reset on a schedule linked to a benchmark index. Check your loan documents for the reset date, the index used and any caps on how much the rate can rise at each adjustment.
  • Fixed-rate mortgages. These follow longer-term Treasury yields more than the Fed's short-term rate. With the 10-year yield near 5%, new mortgage quotes have come under upward pressure. Some outlets reported specific daily mortgage rate jumps after the release, but we found that figure in only one source, so we have left it out as unconfirmed.
  • Auto and personal loans. Existing fixed-rate loans do not change. New loans may be priced higher if rates keep rising, so if you are shopping for one, getting several quotes within a short window is worth the effort.
  • Savings. There is a modest upside. High-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit often raise their rates after a Fed hike, which can help savers keep pace with prices.

What is still unknown

  • The December decision. The FOMC meets on 8 and 9 December. Whether it raises rates, and by how much, is undecided.
  • The annual inflation rate. There is no official October figure. Private estimates range from about 4.0% to 4.2%.
  • The November CPI date. Whether the next report arrives before the December meeting should be checked against the BLS release calendar.
  • Bank forecast changes. Reports that major investment banks raised their hike forecasts after this release could not be confirmed by two sources and remain unconfirmed.

What to do this week

Pull up your three most recent statements for any variable-rate debt and note the current rate, the index it follows and the next reset date. If you hold a credit card balance, list it next to its rate and direct any spare cash to the highest-rate balance first. If you are planning to borrow before year-end, ask lenders whether they offer a rate lock and for how long. Then mark 9 December on your calendar, when the Fed's decision will show whether these preparations need to go further. This is not investment advice.
