If you are booking a room in Korea this winter through an app, the listing you see should now come with a legal check behind it. From Thursday 12 November 2026, online booking platforms operating in South Korea must confirm that a property holds a valid lodging business report before they can sell it. Listings that cannot be confirmed have to come down. This explainer covers what the rule says, why Korea chose to put the burden on platforms, how Korea's lodging categories work, and what visitors should check before paying.

How we checked: every date and figure below appears in at least two independent sources, including the Ministry of Health and Welfare's announcement as carried by Korea's government briefing service and the KDI Economic Information Center, plus reports from SBS Biz, Herald Economy and Newsis. Items that have not been officially announced are marked "not yet announced," and items we could not confirm in two places are marked "unconfirmed." Details are current as of 12 November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

The rule in one paragraph

An amendment to the Public Health Control Act takes effect on 12 November 2026. It applies to "mail-order sales intermediaries," the legal term for online platforms that broker sales between sellers and customers. Before listing a lodging product, a platform must confirm the property's business report certificate, which is issued by the local district office. If the certificate cannot be confirmed, the platform may not broker the stay. If the platform learns that a property is operating without a report, it must take the listing down immediately. A platform that brokers an unconfirmed lodging product faces an administrative fine of 5 million won.

Timeline

  • 11 November 2025: The amended Public Health Control Act was promulgated. It banned the online brokering of unreported lodging businesses and allowed fines of up to 10 million won.
  • July 2026: The Ministry of Health and Welfare published a draft enforcement decree setting the actual fine. Korean outlets reported the proposed figure of 5 million won.
  • 20 October 2026: The ministry promulgated the amended enforcement decree, fixing the fine at 5 million won.
  • Early November 2026: The government briefing service and SBS Biz listed the rule among the laws changing this month.
  • 12 November 2026: The act and its decree take effect together, one year after promulgation.

Why two fine amounts keep appearing

Readers who follow Korean coverage will see both 10 million won and 5 million won. Both are correct, and they do different jobs. In the Korean system, an act passed by the National Assembly sets the framework and a ceiling. A presidential enforcement decree then fills in the operating details. The act allows a fine of up to 10 million won. The decree sets the standard amount at 5 million won per violation. When you read about the rule in practice, 5 million won is the figure that applies. Any reductions or increases for specific cases are set out in the decree text itself.

Who is covered

The duty falls on the platform, not on the property owner. Coverage reported by SBS Biz and the ministry's own explanation say that online travel agencies that take listings directly are covered. So are platforms that pull inventory from other agencies or suppliers and display it on their own screens to arrange sales. In plain terms, if an app or website sells you a night in a Korean property, it is expected to have checked the paperwork.

Listings that were already live before 12 November are not exempt. To keep selling them, platforms must check those properties too. That means some listings may disappear from search results in the first weeks while hosts submit documents. A missing listing does not automatically mean the property is illegal; it may simply be waiting on paperwork.

The ministry has also distributed operating guidelines on restricting the brokering of unreported lodging businesses to local governments and platforms. The full text of those guidelines was not available to us, so how each lodging category is verified in detail is unconfirmed.

Why Korea targeted platforms instead of hosts

Registering a lodging business is a local matter in Korea. Owners file a report with the city, county or district office, which checks that the building's permitted use, facilities and hygiene standards fit the category. Once reported, the property enters the local inspection system for sanitation and safety. An unreported property sits outside that system, so problems with fire exits, heating or cleanliness are less likely to be caught early.

Finding unreported properties one by one is slow for district offices, because they are numerous and scattered. Most of them, however, rely on a handful of booking platforms to reach guests. Checking at that chokepoint filters many listings at once and weakens the income stream that keeps unreported operations going. A ministry official told SBS Biz that platforms previously had no legal duty to check business reports. Existing penalties on owners who run unreported lodging remain in place; the amendment adds a second checkpoint at the sales stage.

Some global platforms had already moved in this direction voluntarily. Airbnb began requiring Korean hosts to submit business report details before the law took effect, and Newsis reported in February 2026 that major global online travel agencies were removing illegal short-term rentals. The new law turns these varied company policies into a single legal standard.

A quick guide to Korean lodging categories

Part of the confusion around legal stays in Korea comes from the fact that different types of lodging are registered under different laws. A rough map helps:

TypeTypical examplesGoverning law
General and residential lodging businessHotels, motels, serviced residencesPublic Health Control Act
Tourist accommodation businessMany tourist hotels and hostelsTourism Promotion Act
Urban homestay business for foreign touristsRooms in city homes rented to visitorsTourism Promotion Act
Rural homestayFarm and fishing-village guesthousesRural Community Improvement Act

The urban homestay category is the one most visitors meet through sharing apps. As its name suggests, it is designed for foreign guests. If you are a Korean resident or travelling with Korean friends, ask the host whether the property can legally accept you. How the new platform rule interacts with categories registered under laws other than the Public Health Control Act is unconfirmed until the operating guidelines are published in full.

What is still open

  • Refunds when a listing is pulled after booking: No common standard has been announced. Each platform's own cancellation and refund policy applies for now. Not yet announced.
  • Enforcement against overseas platforms: Platforms that broker stays in Korea are reported to be covered, but there is no public case yet of a fine against a foreign company. Unconfirmed.
  • Direct bookings outside platforms: Stays arranged through social media or messaging apps do not pass through a platform, so this rule does not screen them.
  • First fines: No enforcement case has been reported yet.

What this means for visitors

For most travellers, the change works quietly in the background. A listing that stays visible on a major platform after 12 November should have passed a business report check. That raises the odds that the room falls under local sanitation and fire-safety inspections. It does not guarantee quality, and it does not cover every risk, such as forged documents or a host who lists one address and hands over another.

Price and reviews are not proof of legality. A property with hundreds of glowing reviews can still be unreported, and a rate far below the neighbourhood average deserves a polite question. Requests to pay by direct bank transfer outside the app are a warning sign, because you lose the platform's dispute process.

Winter adds its own safety concerns. Ask how the room is heated, whether there is a carbon monoxide alarm, and where the nearest exit is. Reported properties are on the local inspection list, which is one reason the business report check matters beyond paperwork.

What to do before your next booking

Open your booking app today and confirm that any Korean stay you have already reserved is still showing as confirmed, and that the property page still loads. For new bookings, scroll to the host or business information section and look for a business report number, a business name and a full street address. If none appear, message the host through the app and ask for the business report number, then screenshot the reply. Choose a rate with free cancellation where you can, pay only inside the platform, and if you suspect a listing is unreported, use the platform's report button or contact the local district office for the property's address.
