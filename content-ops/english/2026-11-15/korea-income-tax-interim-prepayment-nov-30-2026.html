If you run a business in Korea as a sole proprietor, or you have a side business on top of a salaried job, you may have found a tax bill in your mailbox or your KakaoTalk notifications this month that you were not expecting. It is the interim prepayment of global income tax, and the deadline is Monday 30 November 2026. This explainer covers who gets the bill, how the amount is calculated, the two ways to pay less or pay later, what happens if you miss the date, and how to deal with it in the next two weeks.

This is general information, not tax advice. How we checked: the dates, thresholds and figures below appear in at least two independent Korean reports of the National Tax Service (NTS) announcement, from two Korean tax trade papers, Jose Geumyung Sinmun and Hanguk Sejeong Sinmun, with a third trade outlet, Ilgan NTN, used as a cross-check. Anything we could not confirm in two places is marked "unconfirmed." Details are current as of 15 November 2026.

What is happening this month

Starting on Monday 2 November, the NTS began sending interim prepayment notices to about 1.54 million individual business owners with global income. Each notice asks for half of the person's global income tax for the 2025 tax year. The money is due by 30 November.

Korea's global income tax works on a calendar year. You report and settle last year's income in May. For people whose income is not taxed at source every month, that means one large payment each spring. The interim prepayment splits that load in two: part of this year's tax is collected in November, and it is then credited against the final bill when you file in May 2027.

It is not an extra tax and you are not paying twice. Think of it as a deposit on this year's bill.

Who gets a bill and who does not

The notice goes to individuals who earned business income and paid global income tax for 2025. Salaried employees generally do not receive one, because their employer withholds tax from each pay packet and settles it through the year-end tax settlement. If you are an employee who also earned business income on the side, such as freelance work registered as a business, you may receive one.

According to the reports, no notice is sent in these cases:

  • the calculated interim amount is less than 500,000 won;
  • your only income is interest, dividends or employment income, with no business income;
  • you started your business this year, so there is no 2025 business tax figure to base the bill on.

Skipping a small amount is not a tax break. Whatever you owe for 2026 still gets settled when you file in May 2027. The NTS simply does not chase amounts below the threshold in November.

How the amount is set

The calculation looks backwards, not forwards. In November nobody knows your full income for the year, so the NTS uses a number that is already fixed: your 2025 tax. The bill is half of that figure, after taking out tax that was already paid in other ways, such as tax withheld at source. If you paid 4 million won for 2025, expect a notice for roughly 2 million won.

The logic is simple. Half the year has passed, so half of last year's tax is collected in advance. The obvious problem is that a business can have a very different year from the last one. That is what the two relief options are for.

Option one: file an estimate if your income fell sharply

If the first half of 2026 was much weaker than 2025, you can recalculate the tax using your actual results from January to June. This is called an estimated interim return. If the estimated amount is less than 30% of your 2025 tax, you can file and pay the estimate instead of the billed amount. The deadline is the same: 30 November.

Here is how the numbers might look. Say your 2025 tax was 6 million won, so the notice asks for 3 million won. Thirty percent of 6 million is 1.8 million. If your first-half results produce an estimate of 1.5 million won, you can file it and pay 1.5 million won instead. If your estimate comes out at 2.5 million won, it is above the 30% line and you pay the billed 3 million won.

The 30% bar is low on purpose. It is aimed at businesses whose income has collapsed, not those that had a slightly slower year. Some guidance says the estimated return has to be filed on the Hometax website rather than the Sontax mobile app; we could not confirm this in two places, so treat it as unconfirmed and check on the day.

Filing an estimate that is too low only shifts the tax. Whatever you underpay now lands in your May 2027 bill.

Option two: pay in instalments if the bill is large

If the billed amount, or your estimate, is more than 10 million won, you can pay part of it later. The rules are:

  • up to 20 million won: you can defer the portion above 10 million won;
  • over 20 million won: you can defer up to half.

The deferred part is due by Monday 1 February 2027.

Some tax guides say that you do not need to apply separately: you simply pay the non-deferred portion by the deadline and the rest is treated as an instalment. We saw this in only one type of source, so it is unconfirmed. If the payment screen does not offer an instalment field, call your local tax office before the deadline.

What happens if you miss 30 November

Late payment triggers a penalty. As reported, it starts at 3% of the unpaid amount and then adds 0.022% for each day of delay. On a 2 million won bill paid ten days late, that is 60,000 won plus about 4,400 won. The 3% applies even if you are only a day late, which is why paying a few days early makes sense.

Businesses in genuine difficulty, for example after a natural disaster or a long slump, can ask for the deadline to be extended through Hometax or at a tax office. Reports put the maximum extension at nine months. Approval is up to the tax office, so apply before the deadline rather than after it.

How to check and pay

Log in to Hometax on a computer or the Sontax app on your phone using a joint certificate, a financial certificate or a simple authentication app. Look for the notice and payment section and find a line for global income tax marked as interim prepayment. Note the amount, the due date and the electronic payment number.

Common ways to pay include direct transfer through Hometax or Sontax, the virtual bank account printed on the notice, a bank counter with the paper notice, and a credit card. Card payment usually carries a fee, so compare it with a transfer. Whichever method you use, save the receipt. In May 2027 you will want to check that this payment appears as tax already paid in your return.

If you have a Korean bank account but find the Korean-language screens hard to navigate, the NTS runs an English version of its website, and many tax offices can help foreign residents in person. Bring your notice and your residence card.

What is still unconfirmed

  • Exact mailing date of the instalment notices: some guides say early January 2027; we could not confirm this in two places.
  • The May 2027 filing schedule: the NTS has not yet published the detailed timetable for the 2026 tax year.
  • Automatic extensions for disaster areas: whether any region will get an automatic extension for this deadline has not been announced.

Who should pay attention

This matters most to shop owners, restaurant operators, freelancers registered as businesses, landlords reporting rental income as business income, and salaried workers with a registered side business. Foreign residents who run a sole proprietorship in Korea are subject to the same rules. If your income dropped this year, the 30% estimate rule is worth checking before you pay.

What to do now

Today, log in to Hometax or Sontax and look up the exact amount on your interim prepayment notice, then compare it with what you paid in May. If your first-half income fell sharply, gather your January to June sales and expense records this week and work out whether your estimate falls below 30% of your 2025 tax. If the bill is over 10 million won, decide how much to defer and put 1 February 2027 in your calendar. Then pay, or file your estimate, by Friday 27 November, so a slow website or a bank holdup on the last day cannot cost you the 3% penalty.
