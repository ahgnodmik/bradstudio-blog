If you were stuck at a US airport on Friday 13 November 2026, you were caught in only the third nationwide ground stop in American aviation history. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halted every departure in the country for about four hours and twenty minutes after an air traffic control communications failure. Thousands of flights were cancelled or delayed, and the knock-on effects ran into the weekend. This guide sets out what is confirmed, what is still unknown, what refunds you can ask for, and what to do differently the next time the system stops.

How we checked: every time, figure and name below appears in at least two independent reports, including the FAA's own statement, CNN, CNBC, ABC News, CBS News, NPR, NBC News, Al Jazeera, local broadcaster WSB-TV and several Korean outlets. Anything only one outlet reported is marked "unconfirmed." The cause of the failure has not been established by any official investigation, so we treat the root cause as unconfirmed.

What happened during the FAA nationwide ground stop

The FAA ordered the ground stop at 3:54 pm Eastern Time on Friday 13 November, according to CNN and Al Jazeera. A ground stop does not bring planes down from the sky. Aircraft already airborne keep flying to their destinations. What it does is keep every aircraft on the ground from taking off until controllers are confident they can separate traffic safely.

In its official statement, the FAA said several Air Route Traffic Control Centers had lost communications. These centres handle aircraft cruising between airports at high altitude, so a fault there affects routes across the whole country rather than a single hub. The order was lifted at 8:15 pm Eastern the same evening. That is roughly four hours and twenty minutes, which CNN and CNBC described as a stoppage of more than four hours. WSB-TV in Atlanta ran live updates as departures at Hartsfield-Jackson froze along with every other US airport.

The disruption did not end when the order was lifted. Aircraft and crews were left in the wrong cities, and airlines needed time to rebuild their schedules. Flight-tracking data from FlightAware, as reported by ABC News and CBS News, showed more than 9,000 cancellations and over 7,000 delays on Friday alone; the exact figures differ slightly because each outlet captured them at a different time. NBC News and CNN reported thousands more cancellations on Saturday.

Rail took some of the strain. Fox 2 Detroit and WCVB reported that Amtrak's website and app booking system went down for a while on Friday evening as stranded passengers tried to switch to trains. Amtrak said trains kept running, and Fox 5 DC reported that booking returned to normal later that night.

What caused it? Ageing telecom equipment blamed, root cause unconfirmed

Officials have pointed at old equipment. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said routers on the agency's legacy telecommunications network became overloaded with data and began sending out inconsistent information, so the FAA stopped traffic as a safety measure. ABC News, GeekWire and Notebookcheck all carried that explanation. NPR reported on Saturday that the FAA attributed the outage to an "equipment issue" during a telecoms network upgrade.

Federal officials have ruled out a cyberattack for now. The Department of Homeland Security said it had seen no evidence of one, and the White House said there was no sign of a "nefarious act," according to CBS News and Al Jazeera. Speculation that a new AI-assisted traffic management tool played a part was rejected by the FAA and Secretary Duffy, who said that tool is not connected to the core systems controllers use to track aircraft.

What nobody has published yet is a full account of which component failed, why it failed at that moment, and why there was no working fallback. Al Jazeera and Notebookcheck both noted that the precise cause had not been established. Until the FAA releases investigation findings, the root cause should be treated as unconfirmed. Claims you may see online about a specific router model or a particular software patch each come from a single report and are also unconfirmed.

Why it matters: only the third nationwide ground stop

Nationwide halts are rare. Yahoo Travel, Latin Times and the Wikipedia summary of the event all list this as the third in US history. The first was on 11 September 2001. The second was in January 2023, when the NOTAM system that publishes safety notices to pilots failed. NPR and ABC News called this the second since 9/11, which counts the same events from a different starting point.

The 2023 failure involved a notice database. This one hit communications between control centres, which is closer to the core of how aircraft are kept apart. Expect pressure on Congress and the FAA over the pace of replacing legacy equipment. No replacement schedule or budget has been announced, so any timeline you read is unconfirmed.

Incheon–US flights: cancelled, then reinstated with delays

Because the stop began before dawn on Saturday in Korea, morning departures from Incheon International Airport were hit straight away. Hankook Ilbo, Financial News and Wikitree reported that Korean Air initially announced cancellations on its Incheon–Atlanta service (KE033 and KE034), and Asiana Airlines did the same for its Incheon–New York round trip. Once the US order was lifted, at 10:15 am Korea time, both carriers reversed course and operated the flights with delayed departures, according to Hankook Ilbo and Seoul Shinmun.

Not every outlet agrees on the final status of each flight; one Korean report described the same services as cancelled. Reports that other Korean Air routes to Boston or New York might be cancelled were not confirmed. A figure of 42 delayed flights out of 111 on Korea–US routes appeared in a single outlet and is unconfirmed. If you were booked on one of these flights, your airline's own record is the only reliable source for what happened to your booking.

What you can claim after a cancellation or long delay

For flights to, from or within the US, start with the Department of Transportation (DOT) refund rule finalised in 2024. Under that rule, if an airline cancels your flight and you decline the rebooking or other alternative it offers, you are entitled to a cash refund, whatever the reason for the cancellation (reported by WSMV, Consumers' Checkbook and Marketplace). A "significant delay" also qualifies: three hours or more on a domestic flight, or six hours or more on an international one. Vouchers or miles are only an option if you agree to them. Check the DOT's aviation consumer pages for the current version of the rule before you file.

A refund is not the same as compensation. Whether the airline also covers a hotel, meals or other costs during a disruption caused by a government air traffic control order depends on the carrier's own policy and its contract of carriage. Do not assume either way. Ask the airline in writing and keep the answer.

If you flew with a Korean carrier, Korea's consumer dispute resolution standards set compensation for international arrival delays at 10% of the fare for two to four hours, 20% for four to twelve hours and 30% beyond twelve hours, according to several Korean consumer guides. Airlines are exempt when they can show the delay came from weather, airport conditions or unforeseen safety measures. Whether a US government ground stop falls under that exemption will be decided case by case, so treat any outcome as open until the airline responds.

Travel insurance is a separate track. Delay and cancellation cover varies by policy, with different time thresholds and payout limits. Insurers usually ask for an airline-issued delay or cancellation letter plus receipts for what you spent. Whether your policy pays out for this event depends entirely on its wording, so read the terms rather than relying on general advice.

How to handle the next ground stop

  • Check before you leave home. The FAA posts live airport status and ground stops on its public status page. Your airline app usually updates faster than airport screens.
  • Rebook from the app or by phone while you queue. During a mass disruption, the first passengers to grab open seats on later flights get them. Try the app, the phone line and the desk at the same time.
  • Ask for written confirmation. Request a delay or cancellation notice before you leave the airport. Insurers and dispute bodies rarely accept screenshots of departure boards.
  • Keep every receipt. Meals, taxis, hotels and rail tickets should be photographed on the spot and stored in one folder.
  • Decide on refund versus rebooking deliberately. Accepting a voucher can close off a cash refund. If your plans no longer work, say clearly that you are declining the alternative and want your money back.
  • Have a ground backup. On shorter routes, a train or rental car may get you there first, but booking systems can strain under the same rush, as Amtrak's did.
  • Build slack into connections. Avoid the last flight of the day and tight self-made connections on separate tickets when travel matters.

If you were disrupted on 13 or 14 November, do three things this week: ask your airline for a written cancellation or delay notice, file a refund request if you declined rebooking, and send your insurer the notice with your receipts. Then bookmark the FAA status page and your airline app's trip alerts so you see the next stop before you reach the terminal.
