Most people will never touch the inside of a wind turbine. The big ones are built in factories and installed by specialists. PureSelfMade, a small Austrian nonprofit, takes the opposite approach: it runs multi-day workshops where ordinary people carve the blades, wind the coils, and assemble a small turbine from raw materials, using designs by Scottish wind pioneer Hugh Piggott. If you have ever wondered whether you could build one yourself, here is what the program is, where the designs come from, and what has not been confirmed for the coming year.

Workshop dates and fees for 2026 and beyond are unconfirmed. This article makes no claims about how much electricity a workshop-built turbine will produce or how much money it might save.

Who runs it

PureSelfMade was founded by Jonathan Schreiber, an Austrian who had been tinkering with turbines since he was young. Reasons to be Cheerful and the Wind Empowerment network both describe the same origin story. Schreiber traveled to Scoraig, a remote peninsula in northwest Scotland, and learned turbine building from Hugh Piggott. The following year he ran his first workshop of his own and set up PureSelfMade as a nonprofit dedicated to teaching people to make their own turbines.

The group's workshops have been held mainly in Austria and Germany, with earlier courses elsewhere in Europe. Science.lu, a Luxembourg science outlet, documented a workshop Schreiber led with students from a Luxembourg technical school and the University of Luxembourg. Piggott's own blog regularly lists PureSelfMade sessions alongside other courses that use his designs.

Where the designs come from

Hugh Piggott has lived off-grid on Scoraig since the mid-1970s. According to his own biography page and his publisher listings, he has spent decades designing, building and selling small turbines in a range of sizes, many of them supplying power to homes on the peninsula, which has no connection to the main grid.

His designs are deliberately buildable. Recent versions use axial-flux alternators with neodymium magnets, a layout that can be made in a workshop with jigs and molds rather than precision factory tooling. Piggott has published the plans in books, including A Wind Turbine Recipe Book, which covers turbines in a range of sizes, and the older Windpower Workshop, which explains how to make efficient blades from wood and match them to a generator. Because the plans are widely available, Piggott-style turbines are built by groups around the world, and the Wind Empowerment network connects many of them.

Researchers have taken the designs seriously too. BOKU, the University of Natural Resources and Life Sciences in Vienna, ran a project on the life cycle assessment and energy payback of the Piggott DIY turbine, built in PureSelfMade workshops. A separate academic study, the Scoraig wind trials, measured the real-world performance of locally built turbines on the peninsula.

What you actually build

The workshop most often builds a turbine with a rotor about 2 meters across, which Piggott calls the 2F design. Participants do the work themselves, with instructors guiding each step:

  • Blades: shaped by hand from wood.
  • Alternator: magnets set into rotor discs and copper coils wound and cast into a stator.
  • Frame and tail: the metal mounting that carries the alternator, plus the tail that keeps the rotor facing the wind.
  • Wiring: connecting the alternator to the rest of the electrical system.

The rated power of the 2-meter design differs between sources. A 2017 Luxembourg workshop write-up described a turbine of almost 500 watts rated power, while a later PureSelfMade crowdfunding page listed 600 watts. A rating is not the same as what a turbine produces in a real yard. Output depends heavily on the local wind, tower height, turbulence from trees and buildings, and the battery or grid setup it feeds.

Workshops run over several days, with participants working on the turbine together from start to finish. The exact length varies by course and is not consistent across sources, so check the specific listing before you plan travel.

The biggest practical advantage of building your own is that nothing on the machine is a mystery. When a bearing wears out or a blade gets damaged, you know how to fix it. The trade-off is that the responsibility for safety, siting and permits sits entirely with you.

What is not confirmed

  • 2026 and 2027 dates and locations: not confirmed in independent sources. PureSelfMade's own site is the place to check.
  • Fees: past listings have shown fees in the hundreds of euros, and some sessions were subsidized. Current pricing is unconfirmed.
  • Cumulative numbers: totals for turbines built and participants trained differ between sources and dates, so treat any single figure as approximate.
  • Output and savings: no independent source in our search gives a reliable figure for energy produced or money saved by a workshop-built turbine.

Before you sign up

A few common-sense checks will save frustration. First, find out whether a small turbine is even allowed where you live. Many towns and counties regulate tower height, setbacks from property lines and noise, and some require a building permit. Second, think about wind honestly. Small turbines in built-up areas often disappoint because buildings and trees break up the airflow; open, exposed sites do better. Third, decide what the turbine will connect to. Charging a battery bank off-grid is a different project from feeding a home connected to the utility, which usually requires the utility's approval and certified equipment.

Physical readiness matters as well. You will be using power tools, handling strong magnets that can pinch fingers badly, and possibly working near metalworking equipment. Wear the protective gear the instructors provide, and tell them in advance about anything that limits what you can do.

If this sounds like your kind of project, start by visiting PureSelfMade's workshop page to see whether any upcoming dates are listed, and browse Hugh Piggott's Scoraig Wind blog, which posts courses using his designs across Europe. Then call your local planning or building office and ask what rules apply to a small turbine on your property before you spend money on travel or materials.
