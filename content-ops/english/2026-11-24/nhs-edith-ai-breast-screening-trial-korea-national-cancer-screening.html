If you have seen headlines saying the NHS is now using AI to read mammograms, you may be wondering what that actually means for the women being screened, and whether anything similar is happening in Korea. On 23 November 2026 the first women joined EDITH, a UK trial testing AI inside the routine NHS breast screening programme. This explainer covers what the trial does, what it does not do, how it compares with Korea's national breast cancer screening, and what you can do if you are due for a mammogram.

This is general information, not medical advice. Follow the screening schedule set by your national programme and talk to your doctor about your own situation.

What started on 23 November

Recruitment opened at Cambridge University Hospitals, the first of about 30 breast screening sites in the UK that will take part. Women in Cambridgeshire who received their routine screening invitation became the first participants. The trial is run by the University of Warwick and Cambridge University Hospitals, with research funding from the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR).

The headline figure is close to 700,000 women. The official trial registry entry lists a slightly lower target of about 660,000 women across roughly 30 screening centres, so treat 700,000 as the announced figure rather than a final count. Funding is reported inconsistently: the UK government's original February 2025 announcement put it at £11 million, while the University of Cambridge release gives £10 million. We could not settle that difference, so the exact amount is unconfirmed.

How NHS breast screening works today

In England, women are invited for a mammogram from age 50 until just before their 71st birthday, roughly every three years. Each set of images is read independently by two trained specialists. This double reading is meant to catch what one reader might miss. It works, but it is labour-intensive: every mammogram needs two expert reads, and the specialist workforce is stretched.

That staffing pressure is the problem EDITH is designed to address. If AI can safely do the work of one of those two readers, specialist time could be freed for other patients. The question is whether that can happen without missing more cancers or calling back more women unnecessarily.

What the AI actually does in EDITH

The trial compares three ways of reading mammograms, assigned by screening site rather than by individual woman.

  • Group one, standard care: two human specialists read every mammogram, as they do now.
  • Group two, AI as second reader: a human specialist reads the images first, and AI takes the place of the second reader, looking for cancers the first reader may have missed.
  • Group three, AI first: the AI looks at the images first and sorts them into higher-risk and lower-risk, which then shapes how human readers review them.

In every group, the researchers say, professional oversight stays in place. The AI flags and sorts; it does not hand down a diagnosis on its own. Nothing changes at the appointment itself either. The X-ray is taken the same way whichever group a site belongs to.

What the researchers will measure

The trial is looking at three main things. First, how many women are recalled for further tests, because too many recalls cause anxiety and cost. Second, how many interval cancers turn up, meaning cancers found between screening rounds after a normal result, which is a rough signal of what screening missed. Third, how well each AI approach performs across different groups of women, so that it does not work well for some and poorly for others.

Can women say no?

Yes. The trial uses an opt-out model. Information about EDITH arrives with the routine screening invitation, and a woman who does not want her images read with AI can tell the screening centre. Her mammogram will then be read by two specialists in the usual way, and her data will be left out of the trial results.

Is there evidence that this works?

The study most often cited is MASAI, a randomised trial in Sweden involving about 100,000 women. Its final results were published in The Lancet in early 2026. In the AI-supported group, the interval cancer rate was 1.55 per 1,000 women, compared with 1.76 per 1,000 in the standard double-reading group.

That sounds like a clear win, but read it carefully. The trial was designed to show the AI approach was not worse than standard care, and it met that bar. The difference between the two groups was not large enough to prove the AI approach is better. Sweden's screening system is also not identical to the NHS. That is exactly why the UK is running its own large trial rather than simply adopting the Swedish model.

How this compares with Korea

Korea's National Cancer Screening Program offers breast cancer screening to women aged 40 and over, with a mammogram every two years. That means Korean women start ten years earlier than in England and are screened on a shorter cycle. Clinical guidelines developed with the National Cancer Center are reported to recommend biennial mammography for women aged 40 to 69, so women aged 70 and over are generally advised to discuss continued screening with their doctor.

Is AI already used in Korea?

AI reading-support software exists in Korea. Lunit INSIGHT MMG, a mammography analysis tool from the Korean company Lunit, received Class 3 medical device approval from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety in July 2019, according to Korean trade press. It marks suspicious areas on an image and scores how suspicious they look, to help the reading doctor.

That approval means doctors may use it as an aid. It is a different question from the one EDITH asks, which is whether AI can replace one of two human readers in a national programme. We found no announcement from the Ministry of Health and Welfare or the National Cancer Center that AI has been officially introduced into national screening reads. Whether a particular clinic uses AI is something you can simply ask when you book.

What is still unknown

  • The results: EDITH will take years to report. There is no fixed publication date.
  • Which AI products: we could not confirm in two independent sources which vendors' systems are being used at which sites.
  • Final numbers: the 700,000 versus 660,000 gap and the £10 million versus £11 million gap are unresolved.
  • Korea: any plan to bring AI into national screening reads is unannounced.

Practical steps if you are due for screening

  • Check whether you are eligible this year. In Korea, look up your status on the National Health Insurance Service website or The Health Insurance app (in Korean). Because screening runs every two years, not every year is your year.
  • Book early. Clinics can get busy towards the end of the year. Calling two or three nearby screening centres now gives you more choice of dates.
  • Bring your history. Note your last screening date and clinic, and any family history of breast or ovarian cancer. Mention it on the intake form.
  • On the day, wear a two-piece outfit and skip deodorant, powder or lotion on the chest and underarms unless the clinic says otherwise. Tell staff if you might be pregnant, are breastfeeding, or have implants.
  • After the result, if you are asked to come back for more tests, book promptly. A recall is not a diagnosis. If your report mentions dense breasts, ask your doctor whether further imaging makes sense for you.

Symptoms change everything. A new lump in the breast or armpit, bloody nipple discharge, or puckered skin is a reason to see a doctor now, not at your next scheduled screen.

What to do now

If you are a woman aged 40 or over in Korea, open The Health Insurance app today and check whether breast screening is due for you this year. If it is, call a screening clinic this week and book a date before the year ends. If you are in England and receive an invitation mentioning EDITH, read the leaflet, decide whether you are comfortable with AI-assisted reading, and either way keep the appointment.
