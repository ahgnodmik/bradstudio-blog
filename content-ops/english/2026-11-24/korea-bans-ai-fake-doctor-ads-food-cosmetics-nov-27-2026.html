If you scroll Korean YouTube, Instagram or shopping apps, you have probably seen a calm man in a white coat telling you which supplement will fix your blood sugar or melt belly fat. Increasingly, that "doctor" never existed. From Friday 27 November 2026, South Korea bans advertisements in which an AI-generated doctor, pharmacist, professor or other expert recommends food, cosmetics or medicines. This explainer covers what the new rule says, why Korea went further than a simple disclosure label, what it means for sellers, and how to protect yourself as a shopper.

What changes on 27 November

Three amended laws take effect on the same day: the Act on Labeling and Advertising of Foods (which also covers health functional foods), the Cosmetics Act and the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act. Each now explicitly bans ads that use AI-made voices, images or video to make it look as if an expert is endorsing, certifying or recommending a product. The amendments passed the National Assembly plenary session on 23 April 2026 and were promulgated on 26 May 2026, according to the government's Korea.kr policy news service and the medical trade paper Dailymedi.

The list of protected titles is broad. Reports in Dailymedi and Dailypharm name doctors, dentists, practitioners of Korean medicine, veterinarians, pharmacists, Korean-medicine pharmacists and university professors, plus other experts in a related field. In practice, that catches the typical scam format: a synthetic face, a lab coat or a pharmacy shelf in the background, and a script that says "as a doctor, I recommend this."

Why Korea moved against AI fake doctor ads

The problem grew quickly. In December 2025 the government's policy coordination meeting announced a package of measures against false and exaggerated advertising that uses AI, including faster review of suspicious ads and a plan for punitive damages, according to Korea.kr and the Financial News. The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), Korea's food and drug regulator, then pushed the law changes through.

Enforcement cases show what was at stake. In June 2026 the MFDS referred officials of a distribution company to prosecutors for using an AI-generated doctor to market ordinary food as if it were a weight-loss drug. Newsis and Digital Daily reported that the company sold around 650,000 units worth about 8.1 billion won between September 2025 and May 2026. At the time, regulators had to prove the health claims were false. After 27 November, the AI expert format alone is enough to break the rule.

A disclosure label is not enough for these products

Korea already has a general rule for AI personas in advertising. The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) revised its review guidelines on endorsements and testimonials so that, from 1 June 2026, any ad in which an AI-generated virtual person recommends or endorses something must say so. Korea.kr and Digital Daily both reported the change. For text-based posts such as blogs, the label is expected near the title or at the start of the post.

That general rule works on a "disclose and you may use it" basis. The 27 November amendments work on a "do not use it at all" basis, but only for food, cosmetics and medicines. We could not find any official guidance saying that adding a "virtual person" label makes an AI doctor ad lawful in these categories. The logic is simple. These are products people eat, swallow or put on their skin, and the word "doctor" does a lot of persuasive work. A label in small print does not undo the impression that a medical professional vouched for the product.

Real experts are not off the hook either. In food advertising, a real doctor or pharmacist vouching for a product's functional claims was already a recognised category of unfair advertising. Dailypharm and the Korean Pharmaceutical Association's paper Yaksagongron reported that the MFDS has raised the sanction for that behaviour to the same level as for AI experts.

What the penalties look like

For food businesses, the MFDS has created a new administrative sanction standard. An AI virtual expert endorsement ad can lead to a business suspension of up to two months, according to Dailypharm and Yaksagongron. Those reports describe the standard as going through the rule-making process, so the final text is unconfirmed.

Some outlets have published criminal penalty figures for breaches of the food advertising law. We could not confirm the same figures in two independent sources, so we have left them out. If you run a business, check the current text of the law on the Korean Law Information Center before relying on any number.

What about medical devices?

Medical devices are covered by a different law and a separate timetable. One legal commentary says the device rule starts in December. We found only one source for that date, so treat it as unconfirmed and check the MFDS announcements before assuming device ads are covered on 27 November.

What it means if you sell online in Korea

If you run a smart store, an Instagram shop or a brand account selling supplements or skincare, the safest move is to audit every ad and product page before Friday. Practical steps:

  • Pull AI expert creatives. Remove any image or video where an AI-made person appears with a medical or academic title, a white coat, a stethoscope, or a clinic or pharmacy setting.
  • Check captions and voice-overs. Words such as "doctor", "pharmacist", "specialist", "professor" or "researcher" next to a synthetic face are the core of the ban.
  • Ask your agency in writing. If an agency produced your ads, get written confirmation of whether AI was used and how.
  • Fix drug-like claims. Describing ordinary food as a diet pill or a blood-sugar drug was already risky, AI or not.
  • Label other AI models. Non-expert AI personas still need the KFTC "virtual person" disclosure that has applied since June, and they should not speak as though they personally used the product.
  • Keep a record. Note which creatives you removed and when. It helps if a regulator or platform asks questions later.

How to spot and report a fake expert ad as a shopper

AI video has become convincing, so visual tells are only a starting point. Lips slightly out of sync with the voice, odd blinking, warped fingers or ears, and meaningless text in the background all suggest synthesis. The stronger checks are about facts you can verify.

  • Look for a real name and workplace. Genuine professionals are usually named. A title with no name is a warning sign.
  • Check what the product actually is. Food, health functional food and medicine are regulated differently. Health functional foods carry an official certification mark on the pack.
  • Be wary of cure-like claims. If ordinary food is described as treating a disease, that is a red flag whether or not AI is involved.
  • Save evidence. Take a screenshot that shows the ad account and date, and copy the product page link.
  • Report it. For food and supplements, Korea's illegal food reporting line is 1399, and the Food Safety Korea portal takes online reports. For refunds and delivery disputes, the national consumer counselling line is 1372. You can also use the report button on the platform where the ad appeared.

If you have already bought a product, check the seller's cancellation and refund terms. Many online purchases in Korea can be cancelled within seven days of delivery, but conditions vary by seller and product. If you have been taking the product and feel unwell, stop and talk to a doctor or pharmacist. This article is general information, not medical or legal advice.

What to do this week

As a shopper, open the last supplement or skincare ad you saved and check two things: is the expert a real, named person, and what is the product registered as? If either answer worries you, hold off on buying and report the ad. As a seller, go through every food and cosmetics creative before Thursday night and take down anything that puts an AI-made expert in front of your product.
