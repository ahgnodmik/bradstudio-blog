Every kilogram of glue you ship to the Moon is a kilogram you pay rocket prices for. That simple cost problem sits behind a new result from the University of Arkansas, where a small team used a laser to fuse simulated moon dust into interlocking, LEGO-style blocks with no binder at all. The blocks are small, they are weaker than binder-based versions, and nobody has tested them in a vacuum yet. This piece walks through what was actually made, how the process works, what the strength numbers do and do not tell you, and how the work fits alongside earlier lunar brick experiments in Europe, the US and South Korea.

What the Arkansas team made

The project was led by Wan Shou, an assistant professor of mechanical engineering at the University of Arkansas. The first author is Cole McCallum, who was an undergraduate when the work began, which is why Popular Science framed the story around a college student building moon bricks. The paper, titled "Additive manufacturing of lunar regolith for reconfigurable building blocks toward lunar habitation," appears in the journal npj Advanced Manufacturing. A preprint under the same title was posted to arXiv (2506.06392) in June 2025, and the university announced the journal publication in November 2026. EurekAlert!, Popular Science, 3DPrinting.com, VoxelMatters and Universe Today all covered the release.

The team did not have access to real lunar soil. Instead it used LHS-1, the Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant, a commercial powder designed to mimic highland samples returned by the Apollo astronauts. Regolith is the loose layer of fine dust and broken rock that covers the lunar surface, and it is the most obvious raw material for anyone hoping to build there.

From that powder the team printed blocks a few centimeters across in two shapes: a cross and a letter H. The shapes slot together, so individual pieces can be assembled into larger structures, taken apart, and rebuilt into something else. That is what "reconfigurable" in the paper's title refers to.

How binder-free laser sintering works

The process is laser powder bed fusion, the same family of techniques used to 3D print metal parts. A thin layer of powder is spread across a build plate. A laser traces the outline of the part, heating only the grains it touches. The plate drops slightly, a fresh layer of powder is spread on top, and the laser traces the next slice. Unheated powder stays loose and can be brushed away at the end.

The university and the trade press describe this as sintering, not melting, and the distinction matters. Melting turns the powder into a liquid that then solidifies. Sintering heats the grains enough that atoms migrate across the points where neighboring particles touch, growing small bridges between them while the bulk of each grain stays solid. Think of squeezing loose snow into a snowball: the flakes stick together, but gaps remain. Ceramics such as porcelain are made this way. Some social media summaries of the Arkansas work described the dust as being melted at high temperature; the original release does not say that. Process temperatures were not reported in the public coverage we reviewed, so we do not quote one here.

According to the university, this is the first demonstration of sintering a regolith simulant directly into reconfigurable building blocks without a binder, using a relatively low-power light source. Note the qualifiers. Lunar soil bricks themselves are not new, and the claim is specific to that combination of conditions.

Why leave the binder out?

Most lunar construction research to date has mixed regolith with something that holds it together: polymers, salts, or other additives. None of those exist in useful quantities on the Moon, so they would have to be launched from Earth, and resupplied every time a crew wanted to build more. Universe Today quoted the researchers as saying that using only lunar material in the structures would remove the bottleneck of binder resupply missions. If the only things you need to send are a printer, a power source and spare parts, the logistics look very different.

How strong are the blocks?

Not very, at least not yet. The university release, Popular Science and 3DPrinting.com all report that the binder-free blocks are more porous and structurally weaker than blocks printed with binders. The researchers say more testing is needed to see whether strength and consistency can be pushed high enough for real use.

We could not confirm whether the two numbers come from the same test conditions, or whether the journal version revised the preprint value. Treat both as unconfirmed until you read the published paper. For a sense of scale, a megapascal is a force of one newton on every square millimeter. Either figure sits well below what conventional structural masonry is designed for, which matches the team's own description of the blocks as weak.

What has not been tested

Every result so far comes from a laboratory on Earth, using simulant. Shou has said the next step is to study how the process behaves in microgravity and vacuum. Those conditions could plausibly change the outcome: without air, heat cannot escape by convection, and fine dust can behave differently when it is electrostatically charged. That last point is general engineering reasoning, not something the team has reported testing. There is also no announced plan from any space agency to adopt this method for a lunar base.

How it compares with earlier lunar brick experiments

Turning moon dust into building material is an old ambition, and the Arkansas work is one approach among several.

  • ESA, 2017: The European Space Agency printed bricks from simulated moondust at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) solar furnace in Cologne, using concentrated sunlight to bake the powder layer by layer. No binder was needed. ESA called it a proof of concept and noted that some bricks warped at the edges. Both ESA and Phys.org reported the work.
  • University of Central Florida, 2022: UCF researchers used binder jetting, spraying salt water onto regolith simulant to form bricks that were then heat treated. The work was framed around a possible Artemis base camp. The binder is the obvious difference from the Arkansas method.
  • ESA and LEGO, 2024: ESA scientists 3D printed LEGO-compatible bricks from ground meteorite dust mixed with other materials, and the bricks went on display in selected LEGO stores. That was largely a public engagement project, but it explored the same idea of small, interlocking units.

Against that background, the Arkansas contribution is the pairing of a laser, no binder, and a modular block design. It does not solve the strength problem, and it does not claim to.

South Korea's lunar regolith research

South Korea has its own line of work on the same question. The Korea Institute of Civil Engineering and Building Technology (KICT) developed a Korean lunar regolith simulant called KLS-1 and has used it in a series of construction material studies, published in Korean journals indexed on KoreaScience. One strand of that research sinters KLS-1 with microwaves rather than a laser. Papers on the method highlight that it needs no separate binder and can be energy efficient, and The Brighter Side of News has reported on KICT's sintered KLS-1 blocks. The heat source differs, but the goal is identical: build with what is already on the Moon.

What to take away

  • The blocks are made from a simulant, not real lunar soil.
  • The process is laser sintering with no binder, not melting.
  • The pieces are a few centimeters wide and designed to interlock and be rebuilt.
  • Strength is low by the team's own account; the published figures disagree and are unconfirmed.
  • Vacuum and microgravity tests have not been done.

If you want to judge the work for yourself, open the arXiv preprint 2506.06392 and look at the cross-section images and the compression test plot, then compare them with the figures in the npj Advanced Manufacturing version. Watch for the team's vacuum and microgravity results; those will say far more about lunar construction than any headline does today.
