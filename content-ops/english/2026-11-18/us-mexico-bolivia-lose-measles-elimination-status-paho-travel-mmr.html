The United States is no longer counted as a country that has eliminated measles. On Tuesday 17 November 2026 the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) withdrew the measles elimination status of the United States, Mexico and Bolivia. For the US, that ends a run that began in 2000. If you are planning a trip to North America over the holidays, or you live there and are not sure about your own vaccination history, this piece explains what was decided, what the numbers show, what elimination status actually means, and how to check that you are protected before you travel.

This is general information, not medical advice. Whether you need a vaccine dose is a decision to make with a doctor, pharmacist or local public health office.

What PAHO decided on 17 November

PAHO is the regional office of the World Health Organization (WHO) for the Americas, and it is the body that verifies whether countries in the region have eliminated measles. An independent regional commission met from 10 to 13 November to review the evidence. It concluded that in all three countries the same lineage of measles virus had kept spreading for 12 months or more without a break. PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa accepted the commission's recommendation, and PAHO published the decision on 17 November. ABC News, NBC News, CNN, Health Policy Watch and Reuters (via US News) all reported it the same day.

The outcome was not a complete surprise. The Wall Street Journal, followed by Axios and The Korea Times, reported on 13 and 14 November that the US had lost its status, citing people familiar with the review. The formal announcement came three days later.

Canada lost its own status a year earlier, in November 2025. At that point PAHO said the Americas as a whole had lost verification as a region free of endemic measles. With the US, Mexico and Bolivia now added, the three largest countries in North America are all outside the eliminated group.

The numbers behind the decision

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US had recorded 5,412 confirmed measles cases in 2026 as of 12 November. Case counts first passed 5,000 in early November, the highest annual figure since 1991, according to Euronews and the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. The full-year total for 2025 was 2,289, so this year's count is already more than double. The CDC says the great majority of this year's cases were in people who were not vaccinated, and it describes the 2025 and 2026 figures as preliminary.

Death counts depend on who is counting. The CDC has confirmed six measles-related deaths this year, according to Reuters reports carried by the Star-Advertiser and GMA News. Totals compiled from state health department announcements are higher: ABC News and Al Jazeera put them at ten after Pennsylvania and Wisconsin each reported a further death on 13 November. Part of the gap has been attributed to a change in how the CDC tracks measles deaths. We report both figures rather than pick one.

Vaccination coverage is the other figure most outlets point to. CDC data for the 2025-26 school year show that 92.4% of US kindergartners had received the MMR vaccine, which protects against measles, mumps and rubella, as reported by CIDRAP and Axios. Public health agencies commonly use 95% as the level needed to keep measles from spreading in a community. Exemptions from one or more school vaccines reached a record 4.2%, up from 3.6% the year before, according to the same reports.

What "measles elimination" actually means

Elimination does not mean zero cases. A country can see dozens or hundreds of cases a year and still hold elimination status, as long as each chain of infection dies out. Most of those cases start with a traveller who caught measles abroad. The test is whether one chain keeps going.

Health authorities answer that question with genetic sequencing. As measles spreads it picks up small changes in its genome, so investigators can compare samples from different patients and see whether they belong to the same lineage. If one lineage keeps circulating inside a country for 12 months or longer, transmission is treated as endemic again, and elimination status is lost.

Can the US get its status back?

Yes, but not quickly. Each country that loses its status has to submit a corrective plan to PAHO, and Barbosa said he expected the three countries to do so in the coming weeks. To regain verification, a country then has to show that the outbreak lineage has stopped circulating and that no endemic spread has occurred for at least 12 months. That makes a return before late 2027 unlikely, although no review date has been set. The content of the plans is unconfirmed until they are published.

Does losing the status make travel unsafe?

No agency has said that. The decision is a technical judgement about how long the virus has been circulating, not a travel advisory or a risk rating. What it does tell you is that measles is spreading in more places, and for longer, than it was a few years ago. For a traveller, the practical question is simple: are you immune?

How to check you are protected before you fly

Health agencies in many countries give similar advice to travellers: make sure you have two documented doses of a measles-containing vaccine before you leave. Here is a practical way to work through it.

  • Find your record. Look for a childhood vaccination card, school health records, or an online immunisation register if your country has one. In the US, state immunisation information systems hold many records. In the UK, your GP practice can check.
  • Count the doses. Two documented doses of MMR is the usual standard for adults and older children. One dose gives strong but not complete protection.
  • If you cannot find a record, ask. A clinician can advise whether to get vaccinated or to have a blood test that checks for immunity. Being vaccinated again when you may already be immune is generally considered safe, but that is a conversation for your own doctor.
  • Leave time. Two doses need to be spaced at least four weeks apart, so start four to six weeks before departure if you think you need both.
  • Check special cases. MMR is a live vaccine and is generally not given during pregnancy or to people with significantly weakened immune systems. Babies under one year are below the normal age for the first dose. Families travelling with infants should ask a paediatrician about timing.

Advice for travellers from South Korea

South Korea was certified by WHO as having eliminated measles in March 2014, and its cases since then have been linked mostly to infections picked up abroad. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) has advised, as reported by several Korean outlets including Newsis and Asia Economy, that people who have not completed two doses of MMR, or are unsure, should get two doses at least four weeks apart, four to six weeks before departure. Korean residents can check their records on the KDCA immunisation helper site (nip.kdca.go.kr, in Korean). KDCA has not yet issued separate guidance in response to the 17 November decision, as far as we could find.

What to watch for after you come home

Measles usually starts like a bad cold: fever, cough, runny nose and red, watery eyes. A rash follows a few days later, starting on the face and spreading downward. KDCA advises travellers to watch for fever or rash for three weeks after returning from an area with measles activity, according to its own press release and a Segye Ilbo report.

  • If you develop symptoms, call a clinic or doctor before you walk in, and tell them where you travelled. That lets them protect other patients in the waiting room.
  • Wear a mask and avoid public transport and crowded places until you have been assessed.
  • Take extra care if you share a home with a baby, a pregnant person, or someone with a weakened immune system.

What is still unknown

  • Corrective plans: the US, Mexican and Bolivian plans have not been published. Unconfirmed.
  • Review date: PAHO has not said when it will next assess the three countries.
  • Final death count: the gap between the CDC's six confirmed deaths and the ten in state reports has not been resolved.
  • Regional total: several reports describe cases across the Americas this year as very high, but we could not confirm a precise mid-November figure in two places. Unconfirmed.

If you have a trip to the US or Mexico on the calendar, take ten minutes today to find your vaccination record. If you cannot see two MMR doses, book a short appointment with your doctor or local health office this week, so there is still time to act before you fly.
