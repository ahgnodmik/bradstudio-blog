If you own a flat or a house in South Korea, or you are thinking of buying one, the number that will shape your property tax bill next year is about to be set. On Friday 20 November 2026, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) holds a public hearing where it will unveil the government's proposed "realization rate" for 2027. That single percentage decides how close the state's official valuation of your home sits to its market price. This explainer covers what the rate is, why it has been frozen since 2023, what the hearing could change, and what you can do before and after the announcement.

What is happening on 20 November

MOLIT will hold the hearing at 10 am at the Seoul Gangnam branch of the Korea Real Estate Board (REB) in Seocho District, Seoul. Its official title is the public hearing for the promotion of real estate price disclosure in 2027. The ministry announced the date on 9 November and accepted written opinions from people and organisations unable to attend until 13 November.

Korean press coverage, including Dnews (Daehan Gyeongje), Pinpoint News and Special Economy, frames the event around one question: will the government keep the realization rate where it has been for four years, or start raising it again?

What "official price" and "realization rate" mean

Every year the Korean government assigns an official price, known in Korean as gongsi gagyeok, to every home and plot of land in the country. Apartments, row houses and multi-household villas get one under the apartment category. Detached houses and land have their own categories. The figures are published online and anyone can look them up by address.

The official price is not the price you could sell for. It is deliberately set below market value. The realization rate is simply the official price divided by the estimated market price. A rate of 69% means a flat the government believes would sell for 1 billion won is officially valued at 690 million won.

Why not use 100%? Market value is hard to pin down. Two flats in the same building can sell for very different sums depending on floor, view and timing, and many homes do not trade at all in a given year. Setting the official price below the market leaves a margin so that no one is taxed on a value higher than their home is really worth. How wide that margin should be is a political choice, and it has swung back and forth.

Why the rates differ by property type

Apartments trade often, so their market value is easier to estimate. Detached houses and land change hands less often and vary more, so their estimates are less certain. That is one reason the current rates differ: 69.0% for apartments, 65.5% for land and 53.6% for detached houses. Critics argue the gap is unfair, because owners of similar-value properties can end up paying different tax.

Why the rate has been frozen since 2023

In 2020 the government of the day adopted a plan to raise realization rates step by step to 90% of market value by 2030. The plan collided with a period of sharply rising house prices. Owners saw their official prices jump twice over: once from market gains and again from the rate increase. Holding taxes rose quickly, and the plan became a political flashpoint.

From 2023 the government rolled the rates back to their 2020 levels and has kept them there since. According to Newsis and Nocut News coverage, the current administration says it still aims for 90% in the long run, but wants to replace the old plan with a new five-year roadmap. The law change needed to underpin that roadmap is still pending in the National Assembly.

Because of that delay, several Korean outlets expect the 20 November hearing to settle only the rate for 2027, not a multi-year path. Market observers quoted in the press see a good chance that the apartment rate stays at 69%. That is a forecast, not a decision. The actual proposal is unconfirmed until MOLIT publishes it.

A frozen rate does not mean a frozen tax bill

This is the point most people miss. The official price moves with two things: the market price and the realization rate. Freezing one does not freeze the result.

The 2026 numbers show this clearly. MOLIT finalised apartment official prices on 30 April 2026. The realization rate stayed at 69%, yet official prices rose 9.13% nationwide on average because market prices had risen. Seoul led with 18.60%, followed by Gyeonggi Province at 6.37% and Sejong at 6.28%. Some areas fell: Gwangju by 1.27%, Daejeon by 1.11%, Daegu by 0.78% and Jeju by 1.81%. Owners could file objections until 29 May, and MOLIT received about 14,000 opinions on the draft prices, adjusting 1,903 of them.

So even if the 2027 rate is frozen again, owners in markets that rose this year should expect higher official prices next spring.

What a rate rise would mean in won

Korean press reports have used a simple example. Take an apartment with a market price of 2 billion won.

  • At a 69% rate, its official price is 1.38 billion won.
  • At a hypothetical 75% rate, its official price becomes 1.5 billion won.
  • The difference is 120 million won, with no change at all in market value.

A six-point rise in the rate produces an increase of about 8.7% in the official price, because the change is measured against the old rate. The 75% figure is purely an illustration used in press coverage. It is not the government's proposal.

Where the official price feeds into your costs

The official price is the starting point for several bills and eligibility checks.

  • Property tax (jaesanse): levied by local governments on all homeowners, based on the official price.
  • Comprehensive real estate holding tax (jongbuse): a national tax on higher-value holdings, collected by the National Tax Service (NTS). For a single-home household, the current basic deduction is 1.2 billion won of official price.
  • National Health Insurance premiums: for self-employed and other "regional" subscribers, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) counts property using the property tax base, so a rise in official prices can lift premiums.
  • Welfare eligibility: several benefits look at household assets, so official prices can affect who qualifies. Rules differ by programme.

The tax reform running in parallel

The rate decision lands while Korea's 2026 tax reform bill is still before the National Assembly. The government's proposal, reported by Jose Geumyung Sinmun and Newsis in August, would raise the basic deduction for a single home the owner actually lives in from 1.2 billion won to 1.4 billion won, and cut it to 900 million won for a single home the owner does not live in. It would also lift the fair market value ratio for the holding tax, the share of the official price that is taxed, from 60% to 70%. Lawmakers have debated changes to both points, and the final shape is unconfirmed. Next year's holding tax will depend on three moving parts at once: market prices, the realization rate and the outcome of that bill.

What to do now

  • Look up your official price. Use the official price lookup site run by MOLIT and the Korea Real Estate Board (search for "부동산 공시가격 알리미"). Enter your address and note both the 2025 and 2026 figures. Choose the right category: multi-household houses are listed under detached houses, not apartments.
  • Estimate your own ratio. Find two or three recent sales of similar units on MOLIT's real transaction price system, then divide your official price by the typical sale price. Treat the result as rough.
  • Read the 20 November numbers by type. Note the rate for your property type only, and check whether a five-year roadmap is mentioned.
  • Do the quick sum. Expected market price multiplied by the new rate gives an approximate 2027 official price.
  • Mark spring 2027 in your calendar. When draft prices appear, you can submit an opinion if yours looks too high. In 2026 the objection deadline was 29 May.
  • Hold off on big moves. Do not sell or gift property on the strength of rumours before the rate and the tax bill are final. Get professional tax advice for your own case.

Look up your 2026 official price today, write it down, and plug in MOLIT's figure the moment it is published on Friday.
